# Caput XXXVIII: Laudāns

*Liber IV, Verba Agentia · Chapter 38*

*In which the verb starts doing things. Chapter 37's* laudātus *was something done to a noun, finished before the main verb. Today's* laudāns *is its mirror: the noun is **doing** it, **right now**, at the same moment as the main verb. "Dad walks along singing." "I hear the barking dog." "A crowd of angels praising God." It's English "-ing," and it declines like a word you already own: Chapter 20's* ingēns. *Along the way: the Christmas angels again, John the Baptist's voice in the desert, the Trojan horse, swimmers in a storm, Icarus calling his father, Seneca's Fates, the Creed, and where "infant," "parent," and "adolescent" came from. Speaking goal: say what people are doing while they do something else.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Pater cantāns ambulat* · *Canem lātrantem audiō* · *Avus dormiēns stertit* · *Dūcunt volentem fāta, nōlentem trahunt* · *Vōx clāmantis in dēsertō* · *Timeō Danaōs et dōna ferentīs*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** present active participle (the "-ing" verb-adjective; old-school "present participle")
    - **Forms:** *laudāns, laudantis* · *habēns, legēns, audiēns, capiēns* · *iēns, euntis*; declined like Chapter 20's *ingēns* (ablative *-e* while it's working as a participle)
    - **Laws:** the Law of *-ns* and *-nt* · the Law of One Shape · the Law of the Same Moment (plus the Many Englishes, present edition)
    - **Words:** *amāns, parēns, adulēscēns, īnfāns, sapiēns, potēns, absēns, praesēns, innocēns, volēns, nōlēns, tamen, somniō, mīca*
    - **Reading:** *Avus Dormiēns* (a nap, a song, a barking dog, and a cheese that vanishes from sleeping hands)
    - **Speaking:** say what five people are doing while they do something else

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep three old friends close: Chapter 20's one-ending *ingēns, ingentis*, Chapter 37's *laudātus*, and Chapter 17's *audiēbam, capiēbam*.

## 1. Praising, not praised

:: Laudātus et laudāns. || Praised and praising. (teaching line)

Avus laudātus rīdet. Avus laudāns rīdet.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 37: *Avus laudātus rīdet.* Who did the praising, and when? | **Someone else** praised Grandpa (he **received** it: passive), and it was **done before** he laughs: "Grandpa, praised, laughs." |
| The blurb's second line: *Avus laudāns rīdet.* Same verb *laudō*, new ending *-āns*. Guess. | "Grandpa, **praising**, laughs." Now Grandpa does the praising himself, and he does it **while** he laughs. Latin *-āns* = English "-ing." |
| Grammar books call *laudāns* the **present active participle** (old-school just "present participle"): the "-ing" verb-adjective. Why "present," and why "active"? | "**Present**": it happens **at the same time** as the main verb. "**Active**": the noun **does** it (Chapter 30's voice). *Laudātus* was perfect passive; *laudāns* is its mirror on both counts. |
| *Pater cantāns ambulat.* (*Cantō*: sing; *ambulō*: walk.) | "Dad walks along **singing**." Everyone in this book knows that sound. |
| *Canis laudātus* vs *canis laudāns*. | *Canis laudātus*: "the **praised** dog" (Chapter 37's good dog). *Canis laudāns*: "the **praising** dog," a dog doing the praising. (In this house, barking counts.) |

## 2. Making it: *-āns, -ēns, -iēns*

:: Laudāns, habēns, legēns, audiēns. || Praising, having, reading, hearing. (teaching line)

Laudā-ns. Habē-ns. Leg-ēns. Audi-ēns.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Take *laudāre*, drop *-re*: *laudā-*. Add *-ns*: *laudāns*. Its "of" form is *laudantis*. What stem do all the other cases use? | ***laudant-***. *Laudāns* is only the naming form, just like Chapter 20's *ingēns, ingentis*. Every other case is built on *laudant-*. |
| Second family: *habeō, habēre*. "Having." | ***habēns, habentis.*** *Habē-* + *-ns*. Likewise *videō → vidēns*, *rīdeō → rīdēns*. |
| Third family: *legō, legere*. The stem is just *leg-*. Guess "reading." | ***legēns, legentis.*** The third family takes ***-ēns***: not *-ins*, not *-āns*. So *dīcēns, edēns, currēns, scrībēns*. |
| Fourth family and the *-iō* verbs: *audiō*, *capiō*. Chapter 17's imperfect said *audiēbam, capiēbam*. Guess "hearing," "taking." | ***audiēns, audientis · capiēns, capientis.*** The *i* stays and *-ēns* follows, as in Chapter 17: *veniēns, dormiēns, faciēns*. |
| Look at the vowels: *laudāns / laudantis*, *legēns / legentis*. What happens? | Long before ***-ns*** (*-āns, -ēns*), **short** before ***-nt*** (*-antis, -entis*). A Latin sound rule: a vowel before *ns* is always long. Before *nt* it's almost always short, and in these participles always. |

!!! success "The Law of *-ns* and *-nt*"
    Make the present active participle from the infinitive:

    | family | infinitive | participle | "of" form |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | first | *laudāre* | *laudāns* | *laudantis* |
    | second | *habēre* | *habēns* | *habentis* |
    | third | *legere* | *legēns* | *legentis* |
    | fourth | *audīre* | *audiēns* | *audientis* |
    | *-iō* | *capere* | *capiēns* | *capientis* |

    The vowel is **long before *-ns*** and **short before *-nt***.

## 3. The rule-breakers fall in line

:: Crēdō in Deum Patrem omnipotentem. || I believe in God the Father almighty. (the Apostles' Creed)

Iēns, euntis. Ferēns. Volēns, nōlēns. Potēns.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 32's Law of Ī and E: *eō* uses *e-* before a vowel and *ī-* before a consonant. Its participle's naming form is ***iēns***. Guess the "of" form. (Hint: *eunt*, "they go.") | ***euntis***: stem *eunt-*, just like *eunt*. *Iēns* stands alone; every other case is *euntem, euntī, euntēs…* |
| Compounds follow along: *redeō, exeō*. "Dad, coming back, sings." "I see the kids going out." | ***Pater rediēns cantat. Līberōs exeuntēs videō.*** |
| *Ferō, ferre* (Chapter 32). Is its participle wild? | No: ***ferēns, ferentis***, "carrying, bringing," like *legēns*. |
| *Volō* and *nōlō* (Chapter 18)? | ***volēns, volentis***, "willing"; ***nōlēns, nōlentis***, "unwilling." English borrowed the pair as *volens nolens*, "willy-nilly." |
| *Sum* has no everyday present participle: classical Latin has no word for "being." Its compounds do. *Absum*: be away. *Prae-* "before, in front" + *sum*. *Possum* (Chapter 18). | ***absēns*** "being away, absent"; ***praesēns*** "being at hand, present"; ***potēns*** "being able, powerful" (from *pot-*). English kept all three. |
| The motto, from the Apostles' Creed: *Crēdō in Deum Patrem omnipotentem.* (*Omni-* from *omnis*.) | "I believe in God the Father **almighty**": "able to do everything." *Omnipotentem* is accusative, agreeing with *Patrem*. |

## 4. Declining *laudāns*

:: Glōria in altissimīs Deō. || Glory to God in the highest. (Luke 2:14)

Laudāns, laudantis, laudantī, laudantem, laudante.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 20's Law of the Three Shapes: a one-ending adjective like *ingēns, ingentis* uses one naming form for all genders, then declines like *fortis*. Do the same: "of the praising one," "to the praising one." | ***laudantis*** ("of"), ***laudantī*** ("to/for"). Same as *ingentis, ingentī*. |
| Object: "I hear the singing boy." "I hear the singing girl." | ***Puerum cantantem audiō. Puellam cantantem audiō.*** Masculine and feminine share *-em*. |
| Neuter object: "I see the shouting mouth" (*ōs*, Chapter 14). | ***Ōs clāmāns videō.*** Neuter Twins: the object form is the naming form. |
| Ablative. Chapter 20's third-family adjectives always took *-ī* (*cum rēge fortī*, *ingentī*). A participle prefers ***-e***: *cum Patre cantante*. Why the split? | While the word is **working as a participle** (doing something right then), it takes ***-e***: *cum Patre cantante*, "with Dad singing." When it has settled into a **plain adjective**, it takes ***-ī***: *ā virō sapientī*, "by a wise man." |
| Plural: *laudantēs, laudantium, laudantibus*. Luke 2:13, the title's source, has *multitūdō… laudantium Deum*. Which case is *laudantium*? | **Genitive plural**, "of (ones) **praising**": i-stem *-ium*, like *fortium* (Chapter 20). "A crowd of (angels) praising God." |

!!! success "The Law of One Shape"
    The present participle is a one-ending adjective, declined like Chapter 20's *ingēns*:

    | | masc. / fem. | neuter | masc. / fem. plural | neuter plural |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | subject | *laudāns* | *laudāns* | *laudantēs* | *laudantia* |
    | object | *laudantem* | *laudāns* | *laudantēs* (*-īs*) | *laudantia* |
    | of | *laudantis* | *laudantis* | *laudantium* | *laudantium* |
    | to / for | *laudantī* | *laudantī* | *laudantibus* | *laudantibus* |
    | by / with / in | *laudante* | *laudante* | *laudantibus* | *laudantibus* |

    Ablative ***-e*** while it works as a participle; ***-ī*** once it's a plain adjective (*sapientī*). Plural *-ium* and *-ia*: i-stem, like *fortis*.

## 5. Any noun, any case

:: Canis lātrāns. || A barking dog. (teaching line)

Canem lātrantem audiō. Vōx Patris cantantis.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Lātrō, lātrāre*: bark (Chapter 15). "I hear the barking dog." | ***Canem lātrantem audiō.*** *Lātrantem* follows *canem* into the accusative. |
| "The voice of Dad singing" (*vōx*, Chapter 13). | ***Vōx Patris cantantis.*** Both genitive, both *-is*. (It doesn't always rhyme: "the voice of the singing girl" is *vōx puellae cantantis*.) |
| "I give cheese to the laughing boy." (*Rīdeō*: laugh.) | ***Puerō rīdentī cāseum dō.*** Dative: *-ō* on the noun, *-ī* on the participle. Two families, one case (Chapter 20's Law of the Two Families). |
| "We sit with Grandpa, who is sleeping." (*Sedeō*: sit.) | ***Cum Avō dormiente sedēmus.*** "We sit with Grandpa while he sleeps." Working participle, ablative *-e*. |
| "The running girls." "I see the running girls." | ***Puellae currentēs. Puellās currentēs videō.*** The *-ēs* does double duty, subject and object (Chapter 13). |

## 6. At the same moment

:: Pater cantāns ambulat, ambulābat, ambulābit. || Dad walks, was walking, will walk, singing. (teaching line)

Avus dormiēns stertit. Avus dormiēns stertēbat.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Avus dormiēns stertit.* (*Stertō*: snore, Chapter 17.) When is the sleeping, and when is the snoring? | **At the same time**: "Grandpa, sleeping, snores," so "Grandpa snores **in his sleep**." The sleeping isn't finished; it's going on. |
| Move the main verb into the past: *Avus dormiēns stertēbat.* | "Grandpa **was snoring** as he slept." Still the same moment, now in the past. *Dormiēns* didn't change. |
| Chapter 37: *Cāseus emptus in mēnsā est.* Today: *Pater cāseum portāns venit.* Compare the two participles' times. | *Emptus*: the buying was **finished before** "is." *Portāns*: the carrying is **going on during** "comes": "Dad comes **carrying** the cheese." |
| So the two participles split the work. Say what each one means. | *Laudātus*: **passive** and **before** ("having been praised"). *Laudāns*: **active** and **at the same time** ("praising"). Opposite on both counts. |
| Can *laudāns* ever mean "having praised"? *Pater, cāseum edēns, cantat.* | **No.** "Dad sings **while eating** the cheese," never "after eating it." For "after," Latin needs other tools (Chapter 40 brings one). |

!!! success "The Law of the Same Moment"
    The present participle shows a deed the noun is **doing** **at the same time** as the main verb, whatever its tense:

    | main verb | Latin | English |
    |---|---|---|
    | present | *Pater cantāns **ambulat**.* | Dad **walks** along singing. |
    | past | *Pater cantāns **ambulābat**.* | Dad **was walking** along singing. |
    | future | *Pater cantāns **ambulābit**.* | Dad **will walk** along singing. |

    | | *laudātus* (Chapter 37) | *laudāns* (today) |
    |---|---|---|
    | voice | passive: done **to** the noun | active: done **by** the noun |
    | time | **before** the main verb | **same time** as the main verb |

## 7. Still a verb

:: Laudantium Deum et dīcentium. || (Of angels) praising God and saying… (Luke 2:13)

Pater cāseum portāns cantat.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| A participle is still part verb. Can it have an **object**? *Pater cāseum portāns cantat.* | Yes: *cāseum* is the object of *portāns*. "Dad sings **carrying the cheese**." Latin likes to tuck the object **between** the noun and its participle: *Pater cāseum portāns*. |
| Luke 2:13: *laudantium Deum*. Who's being praised? | ***Deum***, accusative: the object of *laudantium*. "(Of angels) praising **God**." |
| Adverbs too: "the dog running fast" (*celeriter*, Chapter 34). | ***Canis celeriter currēns.*** An adverb describes the participle because it's still a verb. |
| And places: "Grandpa sleeping in the garden." | ***Avus in hortō dormiēns.*** Find the participle at the end; what's between belongs to it. |
| Matthew 3:1–2 shows *Iōannēs Baptista* (Chapter 30) *praedicāns in dēsertō Iūdaeae et dīcēns: "Paenitentiam agite."* (*Praedicō*: preach, proclaim; *dēsertum*: desert; *Paenitentiam agite*: "Repent.") Find both participles and what hangs on each. | ***Praedicāns*** ("preaching") with *in dēsertō Iūdaeae* (where); ***dīcēns*** ("saying") with the quotation as its object. Both describe John: "**preaching** in the desert of Judea and **saying**, 'Repent.'" |

## 8. The Many Englishes, again

:: Rīdentem dīcere vērum quid vetat? || What forbids (a man), laughing, to tell the truth? (Horace, *Satires* 1.1.24–25)

Puer cantāns. Quīntus, Aviam timēns, pennās colligit.

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 37's Law of the Many Englishes works here too. *Puer cantāns rīdet.* Try "who." | "The boy **who is singing** laughs." Who-English with "is …ing," not "was …ed." |
| *Avus, cāseum edēns, cantat.* Try "while." | "**While he eats** the cheese, Grandpa sings." (With his mouth full.) |
| *Quīntus, Aviam timēns, pennās colligit.* (Chapter 37's feathers; *timeō*: fear.) Try "because." | "**Because he's afraid of** Grandma, Quīntus is gathering the feathers." |
| *Avus, cāseum amāns, tamen nōn edit.* (*Tamen*: still, all the same; Chapter 37 promised it.) | "**Although he loves** cheese, Grandpa still doesn't eat it." (A miracle.) *Tamen* in the main sentence, like *nihilō minus*, signals "although." |
| The motto, Horace, *Satires* 1.1.24–25: *rīdentem dīcere vērum quid vetat?* (*Vērum*: the truth; *vetō*: forbid someone to do something.) Who is *rīdentem*? | No one named: an accusative participle as a **person**, the object of *vetat*: "What forbids someone **laughing** to tell the truth?" Horace's defense of joking: a laugh can carry the truth. (Next line: teachers give boys **cookies**, *crustula*, so they'll learn their letters.) |

!!! success "The Many Englishes, present edition"
    One present participle can come into English many ways. Try them in order and keep the one that fits:

    1. **Plain:** "singing," "praising" (*Pater cantāns*, "Dad, singing").
    2. **Who / which:** "who is singing," "who was singing."
    3. **While / as / when:** "while he sings," "as he slept."
    4. **Because:** "because he fears."
    5. **Although:** "though he loves" (look for *tamen* or *nihilō minus*).

    The time always matches the main verb: "is …ing" with a present, "was …ing" with a past.

## 9. The ones who…

:: Dūcunt volentem fāta, nōlentem trahunt. || The Fates lead the willing, drag the unwilling. (Seneca, *Letters* 107.11; Chapter 18)

Amantēs. Sapiēns. Īnfāns. Parentēs.

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 37: a participle with no noun becomes a person (*victī*, "the conquered"). *Amāns, amantis* alone? | "**One who loves**": a lover. *Amantēs*: "lovers." (Terence, *Andria* 218, puns on it: *āmentium, haud amantium*, "(a plan) of madmen, not of lovers"; *āmēns* means "out of one's mind.") |
| Chapter 18's Lectiō motto: Seneca, *Dūcunt volentem fāta, nōlentem trahunt.* (*Fāta*: the Fates; *trahō*: drag.) Read it now. | *Volentem* and *nōlentem* are participles as **people**, both objects: "The Fates lead the one who's **willing**; the one who's **unwilling** they drag." (Seneca ends his Latin version of a prayer by the Greek Stoic Cleanthes with this line.) |
| Chapter 20's *sapiēns*, "wise." *Sapiō, sapere* is an *-iō* verb: "taste, have sense" (Chapter 6's *Sapere audē*). So what is *sapiēns*, really? | The present participle of *sapiō*: "**having sense**," so "wise"; alone, "**a wise person**." *Homō sapiēns*: "the wise human" (the name naturalists gave us in 1758). |
| Chapter 37's Luke 2:12 had *īnfantem*. *Īnfāns* = *in-* "not" + *fāns* "speaking" (from an old verb for "speak"). | "**Not speaking**": a baby, someone who can't talk yet. English *infant*. *Īnfāns, īnfantis*. |
| *Pariō, parere*: give birth (an *-iō* verb). Today's Law says its participle should be *pariēns*, and it is. But an older form without the *i* survives as a noun: *parēns, parentis*. And *adolēscō*, grow up, gives *adulēscēns, adulēscentis* (spelled with *u* as a noun). | ***Parēns***: "one giving birth," a **parent** (*parentēs*, "parents"). ***Adulēscēns***: "growing up," a **young person**, roughly from the teens to about thirty for a Roman. English *parent*, *adolescent*. |
| John 1:23: *Iōannēs Baptista* about himself: *Ego vōx clāmantis in dēsertō.* (Matthew 3:3 has *Vōx clāmantis in dēsertō*, from Isaiah.) | "I (am) the voice **of one crying out** in the desert." *Clāmantis*: genitive participle as a person, "of someone shouting." (No *sum*; Latin often leaves it out.) |

## 10. Old adjectives, new eyes

:: Libenter. Sapienter. Dīligenter. || Gladly. Wisely. Carefully. (teaching line; Chapter 34)

Sapiēns → sapienter. Libēns → libenter.

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 34's Law of the How-Word made *sapienter* from *sapiēns*: *-nt-* + *-er*. Where does the *-nt-* come from? | The participle stem: ***sapient-*** + *-er* → *sapienter*, "wisely." Same with *dīligēns → dīligenter*. |
| Chapter 34 traced *libenter*, "gladly" (Chapter 17's Mark 6:20), to an adjective *libēns, libentis*. (*Libet*: it pleases.) What is *libēns*, really? | The present participle of *libet*: "**being pleased**, glad." Roman thank-offerings end with ***libēns meritō***, "gladly, as deserved." |
| *Dīligēns* "careful" comes from *dīligō*, "love, value" (Chapter 37's *dīlēctus*). How do "loving" and "careful" connect? | You're **careful** with what you **value**: *dīligēns* is "valuing," so "diligent." One verb, two participles: ***dīligēns*** "valuing" and ***dīlēctus*** "valued, beloved." |
| Chapter 20's *ingēns* looks just like *laudāns*. Is it a participle? | **No**, just a look-alike: no verb *ingeō*. It declines the same way, which is why Chapter 20 already gave you every ending you need today. |
| *Noceō, nocēre*: harm. *Innocēns, innocentis*? | *In-* "not" + *nocēns* "harming": "**harmless, innocent**." Watch for it in the Lectiō. |

## 11. Real lines

:: Timeō Danaōs et dōna ferentīs. || I fear the Greeks, even bringing gifts. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 2.49)

Appārent rārī nantēs in gurgite vāstō.

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Luke 2:13, right after Chapter 37's manger: *Et subitō facta est cum angelō multitūdō mīlitiae caelestis laudantium Deum et dīcentium: "Glōria in altissimīs Deō…"* (*Mīlitia caelestis*: the army of heaven.) Why are the participles genitive plural? | They hang on *mīlitiae caelestis*, "of the heavenly army": one army, but many angels, so **plural** by sense and **genitive** by case. "Suddenly there was with the angel a crowd of the heavenly army, **praising** God and **saying**: 'Glory to God in the highest…'" |
| Vergil, *Aeneid* 2.49: Laocoön warns the Trojans about the wooden horse. *Timeō Danaōs et dōna ferentīs.* (*Danaī*: the Greeks.) What's *ferentīs*? | = ***ferentēs***, Chapter 15's old object plural *-īs*. It agrees with *Danaōs*: "I fear the Greeks even **when they bring** gifts." (*Et* here: "even.") |
| *Aeneid* 1.118, the shipwreck: *Appārent rārī nantēs in gurgite vāstō.* (*Appāreō*: appear; *rārus*: scattered; *nō, nāre*: swim; *gurges*: flood, whirlpool; *vāstus*: huge.) Who appears? | ***Nantēs***, a participle as a noun: "**swimmers**." "Scattered swimmers appear in the vast flood." (Many editions print *Adparent*, the same word.) |
| Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 8.229–230, Icarus falling (Chapter 36): *ōraque caeruleā patrium clāmantia nōmen / excipiuntur aquā.* (*Caeruleus*: sea-blue; *patrius*: father's; *excipiō*: catch.) | "And his mouth, **shouting** his father's name, is caught by the sea-blue water." *Clāmantia* agrees with *ōra* (neuter plural *-ia*; *ōra* is plural for one mouth, as poets like); *nōmen* is its object. Chapter 36 gave you the rest of the line: *quae nōmen trāxit ab illō*. |

## 12. Traps

:: Nōn "laudāns sum," sed "laudō." || Not "praising I am," but "I praise." (teaching line)

Laudō = "I praise" = "I am praising."

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| English "I am praising" has "am" + "-ing." Is it *laudāns sum*? | **No.** Latin says ***laudō*** (Chapter 5: one word means "I praise" or "I am praising"). The present participle doesn't build tenses with *sum*, unlike Chapter 31's *laudātus sum*. |
| English "Singing is fun." Is it *Cantāns iūcundum est*? | **No.** That "singing" is a thing, not a describer. Latin uses the infinitive: ***Cantāre iūcundum est*** (like Chapter 20's *Dulce et decōrum est… morī*; *iūcundus*, Chapter 34). |
| *Avus cāseum edēns cantāvit.* "Grandpa, having eaten the cheese, sang"? | **No**: "Grandpa sang **while eating** the cheese." Same moment, past tense. |
| Spelling: *laudāntem* or *laudantem*? *legāns* or *legēns*? *dormēns* or *dormiēns*? | ***laudantem*** (short before *nt*), ***legēns*** (third family takes *-ēns*), ***dormiēns*** (fourth family keeps its *i*). |
| *Canēs lātrant.* vs *Canēs lātrantēs.* | *Canēs lātrant* is a **sentence**: "Dogs bark." *Canēs lātrantēs* is only a **phrase**: "barking dogs." It still needs a main verb: *Canēs lātrantēs nōn mordent* (*mordeō*: bite): "Barking dogs don't bite." |

## 13. Fix it

:: Errāta corrigentēs. || Correcting mistakes. (teaching line)

Lātrantem an lātrāns? Cantante an cantantī?

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "I hear the barking dog": *Canem lātrāns audiō.* | ***Canem lātrantem audiō.*** The participle follows *canem* into the accusative. |
| "I give cheese to the laughing girl": *Puellae rīdēns cāseum dō.* | ***Puellae rīdentī cāseum dō.*** Dative, to agree with *puellae*. |
| "We sit with Dad singing": *Cum Patre cantantī sedēmus.* | ***Cum Patre cantante sedēmus.*** He's in the act of singing: working participle, *-e*. |
| "The boys reading": *puerī legantēs.* | ***puerī legentēs.*** Third family: *-ēns, -entis*. |
| "The voices of those praising": *vōcēs laudantum.* | ***vōcēs laudantium.*** Genitive plural *-ium*, like Luke's. |

## 14. Lectiō: *Avus Dormiēns*

:: Vōx cantantis in hortō. || The voice of one singing in the garden. (after John 1:23)

Avus dormiēns. Pater cantāns. Canis lātrāns.

Diē sextā: the next afternoon, the day after Chapter 37. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. As you read, hunt the participles and ask: which noun does it agree with? Doing or done? Same time or before? New in the story: *sella*, chair (Chapter 2); *excitō*, wake up; *lūdō*, play (Chapter 36); *somniō*, dream; *mīca*, crumb; *barba*, beard (Chapter 35); *ergō*, so, then (Chapter 12's *Cōgitō, ergō sum*).

{#g-14}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Grandma: *Avus, in sellā dormiēns*. Cornēlia: *Avum dormientem*. Which case is each participle, and why? | ***Dormiēns***: nominative, with *Avus*. ***Dormientem***: accusative, with *Avum*, the object of *excitāre*. Same Grandpa, two jobs. |
| Dad: *Cāseum laudāns cantō.* What is *cāseum* doing, and what's a good English? | *Cāseum* is the **object of *laudāns***: "I sing, praising cheese," so "I'm singing **in praise of** cheese." The participle keeps its verb power. |
| John's rule: *ōs cantantis cāseum nōn edit.* What is *cantantis*? | A genitive participle as a **person**: "the mouth **of one singing**," like John the Baptist's *vōx clāmantis*. |
| Quīntus: *Manūs meae, pennās tenentēs…* Which noun does *tenentēs* agree with? | ***Manūs***: feminine plural nominative (Chapter 23). *Pennās* is the object of *tenentēs*: "my hands, **holding feathers**." |
| *Fūr absēns* · *praesēns*. Which verbs are these from? | *Absum* and *praesum*, compounds of *sum*: "being away," "being here." *Sum* alone has no everyday participle. |
| End: *Cāseus, ā nōbīs emptus… in stomachō Avī dormientis.* Which participle is "before," which "same time"? | ***Emptus***: passive, **before** (we bought it first). ***Dormientis***: active, **same time** (he ate it while sleeping); genitive, agreeing with *Avī*. Both Laws in one sentence. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Loquēns discō. || I learn by speaking. (teaching line)

1. Make the participle of ten verbs: *laudō, cantō, habeō, rīdeō, legō, currō, audiō, dormiō, capiō, eō*, each with its "of" form.
2. Decline *canis lātrāns* and *animal lātrāns* in the singular and plural.
3. The three Laws in your own words: *-ns* and *-nt*, One Shape, the Same Moment.
4. Say what five people are doing while they do something else: *Pater cantāns ambulat · Avus dormiēns stertit · Avia crustula coquēns cantat…*
5. Give one participle three Englishes: *Quīntus, Aviam timēns, pennās colligit* (plain, "while," "because").
6. The real lines: *multitūdō… laudantium Deum et dīcentium · Dūcunt volentem fāta, nōlentem trahunt · Vōx clāmantis in dēsertō · Timeō Danaōs et dōna ferentīs · Appārent rārī nantēs in gurgite vāstō · Crēdō in Deum Patrem omnipotentem.*
7. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part (Dad's singing too). Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
