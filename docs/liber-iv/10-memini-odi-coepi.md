# Caput XLVI: Meminī, Ōdī, Coepī

*Liber IV, Verba Agentia · Chapter 46*

*In which three old puzzles get solved. Back in Chapter 5 Catullus said* Ōdī et amō, *and the book told you* ōdī *was "an 'I' form you'll meet much later." In Chapter 16 Vergil said* meminisse *and Chapter 41 told you to wait for Chapter 46. In Chapter 19 Archimedes said* "Nōlī," inquit, *and nobody explained* inquit. *Today: verbs with missing parts. Two have only perfect forms but mean the present (*meminī, ōdī*), and *nōvī*, from an ordinary verb, uses its perfect the same way; one has a perfect and borrows its present from a neighbor (*coepī, incipiō*); two are mostly scraps (*inquit, ait*). Then the genitive goes to work for verbs and adjectives: remember* of, *forget* of, *pity* of, *accused* of, *ashamed* of, *eager* for, *full* of. Along the way: a lover who hates and loves, a poet who hates the crowd, an apostle who says he isn't mad, a standard-bearer who jumps into the sea, an oracle that says two things at once, a psalm that begs for mercy, and a queen who is tired of the sky. Speaking goal: say what you remember, forget, hate, know, and begin; report what someone said with* inquit; *say what you're ashamed of, sorry about, and tired of.*

    - **Saying:** *Meminī · Meministīne? · Ōdī · Nōvī · Ambulāre coepimus · Cantāre incipiō · "Salvē," inquit Avus · Quid ais? · Nōlī oblīvīscī! · Miserēre meī · Mē pudet · Nōs taedet itineris · Cāseī cupidus sum*

    - **Laws:** the Law of the Perfect That Means Now · the Law of the Slipped-In *Inquit* · the Law of the Genitive's Grip

## 1. Three old puzzles

:: Ōdī et amō. || I hate and I love. (Catullus 85; Chapter 5)

Ōdī. Meminisse. Inquit.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 5's motto, *Ōdī et amō*. Chapter 5 said *ōdī* is "an 'I' form you'll meet much later." Look at the ending. Which tense of *amō* ends in *-ī*? | The **perfect** (Chapter 25): *amāvī, vēnī, vīdī, vīcī.* So *ōdī* looks like "I have hated." But it means "**I hate**," right now, side by side with *amō*. |
| Chapter 16's motto, *Forsan et haec ōlim meminisse iuvābit*. Chapter 41 said *meminisse* is a perfect infinitive whose verb waits for Chapter 46. What's the verb? | ***Meminī***, "**I remember**." Another perfect look with a present meaning. |
| Chapter 19's Archimedes: *"Nōlī," inquit, "obsecrō, istum disturbāre."* Where does *inquit* ("he said") stand? | **Inside** the quotation, after its first word: "'Don't,' he said, 'please, mess up that one.'" |
| Grammar books call verbs like these **defective verbs**. Plain words? | "**A verb with missing parts.**" *Dēficiō* means "fall short, be lacking." *Ōdī* and *meminī* have no present-stem forms at all (no *ōdō*, no *meminō*); *inquit* has only a handful of forms. |
| English has them too. "I can, I could." Now say "to can," or "I will can." | You can't: English *can* is defective. It borrows "be able to": "I will **be able to**." Latin borrows too, as you'll see with *coepī*. |

## 2. *Meminī*: a perfect that means now

:: Forsan et haec ōlim meminisse iuvābit. || Maybe someday it will be a pleasure to remember even these things. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 1.203; Chapter 16)

Meminī. Meministī. Meminit.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chant *meminī* like Chapter 25's *vīdī*: I, you, he or she, we, you all, they. | ***Meminī, meministī, meminit, meminimus, meministis, meminērunt.*** "I remember, you remember … y'all remember, they remember." |
| Why would "I remember" be a perfect? Think of it as "I have called to mind." | Then "**I have it in mind**," which is to remember, now. *Me-min-ī* even doubles its first sound like Chapter 25's *dedī* and Chapter 36's *cecidī*. Its root is the *men-* of *mēns*, "mind." |
| "Do you remember Grandma's cheese?" "I remember!" | ***Meministīne cāseum Aviae? Meminī!*** (Chapter 1's *-ne* on the verb.) |
| If the perfect *meminī* means "I remember" (now), what do the pluperfect ***memineram*** and the future perfect ***meminerō*** mean? | "**I remembered**" (then) and "**I will remember**" (later). Each form slides one step: the "had" form becomes a plain past, the "will have" form a plain future. *Herī memineram* (yesterday I remembered) · *Meminerō!* (I'll remember!) |
| Back to Vergil: *Forsan et haec ōlim meminisse iuvābit.* What is *meminisse* now? | "**To remember**." A perfect-infinitive look (Chapter 41's *-isse*) with a present meaning, the subject of *iuvābit*: "Maybe someday **to remember** even these things will be a pleasure." Chapter 41's promise, paid. |

## 3. *Ōdī*: hate in the perfect

:: Ōdī profānum vulgus et arceō. || I hate the uninitiated crowd and keep it away. (Horace, *Odes* 3.1.1)

Ōdī, ōderam, ōderō.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chant *ōdī*. Then: "Grandpa hates walking." (An infinitive can name the thing, like Chapter 44's *Mihi placet ambulāre*.) | ***Ōdī, ōdistī, ōdit, ōdimus, ōdistis, ōdērunt.*** "I hate … they hate." ***Avus ambulāre ōdit.*** |
| *Ōderam* and *ōderō*? | "**I hated**" and "**I will hate**," sliding one step, exactly like *memineram* and *meminerō*. *Canis aquam ōderat*: "The dog hated water." |
| Catullus 85 in full. After *Ōdī et amō*, line 1 asks a question in a mood you'll meet in Liber V: "Why do I do that, you may ask." Line 2 answers: *nesciō, sed fierī sentiō et excrucior.* (*Fierī*: to happen, Chapter 32's *fīō*; *excruciō*: torture.) | "**I don't know, but I feel it happening, and I am tortured.**" *Nesciō, sentiō, excrucior*: presents. *Ōdī et amō* stands among them as a present too. |
| Horace, *Odes* 3.1.1, a priest of the Muses clearing the room: *Ōdī profānum vulgus et arceō.* (*Profānus*: uninitiated, not let in on the secret; *vulgus*: the common crowd; *arceō*: keep away. Some editions spell it *volgus*.) | "**I hate** the uninitiated crowd **and keep it away**." *Ōdī* and *arceō*, a perfect form and a present form, stand side by side as two "now" verbs. |
| *Ōdī* keeps one more piece: a future participle (Chapter 39), ***ōsūrus***. "Grandpa is going to hate the trip." | ***Avus iter ōsūrus est.*** (Chapter 45's *iter*.) And "to hate" is ***ōdisse***. |

## 4. *Nōvī*: I've learned, so I know

:: Nōvit Dominus viam iūstōrum. || The Lord knows the way of the righteous. (Psalm 1:6)

Nōscō, nōvī, nōveram.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 21's *Nōsce tē* used ***nōscō***, get to know. Its full keys: ***nōscō, nōscere, nōvī, nōtum*** (Chapter 40's *cognōscō, cognōvī* is the same verb with *co-* in front). If *nōvī* is "I have gotten to know," what does it mean now? | "**I know**." Like English "I've got it" = "I have it." So ***nōvī*** = I know, ***nōveram*** = I knew, ***nōverō*** = I will know. *Cognōvī* works the same way. |
| Chapter 6's *sciō* also means "know." What's the difference? | ***Sciō***: know a **fact** (*Sciō Avum cāseum ēdisse*, Chapter 42). ***Nōvī***: know a **person or place**, be acquainted with (*Avum nōvī. Rōmam nōvī.*). |
| Psalm 1:6: *Quoniam nōvit Dominus viam iūstōrum, et iter impiōrum peribit.* (*Quoniam*: because, Chapter 16; *iūstus*: righteous; *impius*: wicked; *pereō*: perish, Chapter 32's *eō*.) | "Because **the Lord knows** the way of the righteous, and the road of the wicked will perish." *Nōvit*: perfect form, present meaning. *Peribit*: future of *pereō*. |
| Matthew 26:72. Peter, waiting in the high priest's courtyard, is recognized a second time as one of Jesus's friends, and denies it with an oath (Chapter 42's *negō*): *Nōn nōvī hominem!* | "**I don't know the man!**" Some editions add *Quia* (that) in front; the denial is the same. |
| "I'm getting to know Rome" vs "I know Rome." | ***Rōmam nōscō*** (present: still learning) · ***Rōmam nōvī*** (perfect form: learned it, so I know it). |

!!! success "The Law of the Perfect That Means Now"
    *Meminī* and *ōdī* live only in the **perfect system**, and *nōvī* (from *nōscō*) uses its perfect the same way. Every form slides **one step** toward the present:

    | verb | perfect = now | pluperfect = then | future perfect = later | "to ___" |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | *meminī* | I remember | *memineram*, I remembered | *meminerō*, I will remember | *meminisse* |
    | *ōdī* | I hate | *ōderam*, I hated | *ōderō*, I will hate | *ōdisse* |
    | *nōvī* | I know | *nōveram*, I knew | *nōverō*, I will know | *nōvisse* |

    The endings are Chapter 25's perfect, Chapter 27's pluperfect and future perfect, and Chapter 41's *-isse*. Only the clock moves. (*Meminī* also has a command, *mementō*, "remember!": Chapter 39's *Aequam mementō*. Chapter 47 explains its form.)

## 5. *Coepī* and *incipiō*: begin

:: Dīmidium factī, quī coepit, habet. || He who has begun has half the deed done. (Horace, *Epistles* 1.2.40)

Incipiō. Coepī. Coeperam.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| ***Coepī***: I began. A real past this time, no sliding. What's missing? | The **present** side: there's no *coepiō*. For "I begin," Latin borrows a neighbor: ***incipiō, incipere, incēpī, inceptum*** (*in* + Chapter 6's *capiō*, "take on"). In the past, Romans usually preferred *coepī*; its *coeperam* and *coeperō* are ordinary "had begun" and "will have begun." |
| "We began to walk." "I'm beginning to sing." | ***Ambulāre coepimus. Cantāre incipiō.*** Both take an infinitive, like Chapter 18's *possum*. |
| Horace, *Epistles* 1.2.40–41: *Dīmidium factī, quī coepit, habet; sapere audē, / incipe.* (*Dīmidium*: half; *factum*: deed, the fourth key of *faciō*; *quī*: he who, Chapter 28.) | "**He who has begun** has half the deed done: dare to be wise, **begin!**" Chapter 6's motto, *Sapere audē*, had *incipe*, the command of *incipiō*, right after it all along. |
| Matthew 4:17: *Exinde coepit Iēsus praedicāre et dīcere: "Paenitentiam agite; appropinquāvit enim rēgnum caelōrum."* (*Exinde*: from then on; *praedicō*: preach, Chapter 38.) | "From then on Jesus **began** to preach and to say: 'Repent, for the kingdom of heaven has come near.'" Jesus begins with the words of John the Baptist from Chapter 38, *Paenitentiam agite*. *Coepit* + two infinitives. |
| With a **passive** infinitive, *coepī* goes passive too: *Mūrus aedificārī coeptus est.* (*Aedificārī*: "to be built," Chapter 41's present passive infinitive of Chapter 40's *aedificō*.) | "**The wall began to be built.**" *Coeptus est* agrees with *mūrus*, like any Chapter 31 perfect passive. |
| The fourth key as a noun: ***coepta, coeptōrum*** (n. pl.), "things begun, undertakings." Vergil, *Aeneid* 9.625, young Ascanius praying before his first shot in battle: *Iuppiter omnipotēns, audācibus adnue coeptīs.* (*Omnipotēns*: almighty, Chapter 38; *audāx*: bold; *adnuō*: nod to, favor.) | "Almighty Jupiter, **nod to** (favor) **my bold beginnings**." *Adnuō* takes the dative, Chapter 44's hidden "to." The motto on the back of the US dollar bill, *Annuit coeptīs*, "He has favored our undertakings," is borrowed from Vergil. |

## 6. *Inquit*: the slipped-in "said"

:: "Dēsilīte," inquit, "mīlitēs!" || "Jump down, soldiers!" he said. (Caesar, *Gallic War* 4.25.3)

"Salvē," inquit Avus.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The forms you'll meet: ***inquam, inquis, inquit, inquiunt.*** | "I say, you say, he or she says, they say." *Inquit* also serves as "**he said**": present and past look the same. (*Inquimus* and *inquitis* exist but are rare.) |
| Where does it go? Look at Archimedes again: *"Nōlī," inquit, "obsecrō, istum disturbāre."* | **Never in front** of the quotation. It slips in after the first word or phrase, like English "'Don't,' he said, 'please…'" |
| "'Hello,' says Grandpa." | ***"Salvē," inquit Avus.*** The speaker usually comes **after** *inquit*. |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 4.25.3. Britain, 55 BC. The soldiers hesitate to jump from the ships into the deep sea. The man who carries the eagle (*aquila*) of the Tenth Legion shouts: *"Dēsilīte," inquit, "mīlitēs, nisi vultis aquilam hostibus prōdere; ego certē meum reī pūblicae atque imperātōrī officium praestiterō."* (*Dēsiliō*: jump down; *nisi*: unless, Chapter 29; *prōdō*: betray, hand over; *rēs pūblica*: the state; *officium*: duty; *praestō*: perform.) | "'**Jump down**,' he said, '**soldiers**, unless you want to hand the eagle over to the enemy; I, at least, will have done my duty to the state and to my general.'" Then he leapt into the sea and *aquilam … ferre coepit*, "began to carry the eagle" at the enemy, and the soldiers followed. *Praestiterō*: Chapter 27's future perfect. |
| Acts 26:25. The governor Festus has shouted that Paul is mad. Paul: *"Nōn īnsāniō," inquit, "optime Fēste, sed vēritātis et sobrietātis verba loquor."* (*Īnsāniō*: be mad; *vēritās*: truth; *sobrietās*: sober sense; Chapter 43's *loquor*.) | "'**I am not mad**,' he said, '**most excellent Festus**, but I speak words of truth and sober sense.'" *Inquit* after the first two words; *Fēste* is a vocative. |
| Chapter 27's dinner invitation, Catullus 13.6–7: *haec sī, inquam, attuleris, venuste noster, / cēnābis bene.* (*Venustus*: charming.) | "If, **I say**, you bring these things, my charming friend, you'll dine well." *Inquam*, "I say," slipped in for emphasis, like English "if, I say, …" |

## 7. *Aiō*: say yes, say so

:: Aiō tē, Aeacidā, Rōmānōs vincere posse. || I say that you, son of Aeacus, can conquer the Romans. (Ennius, quoted by Cicero, *On Divination* 2.116)

Ait. Aiunt. Quid ais?

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| ***Aiō, ais, ait, aiunt***: say, say yes. It's the opposite of Chapter 42's *negō*, "say no." *Avus negat; Avia ait.* | "Grandpa says no; Grandma says yes." Like *inquit*, *ait* means "he says" or "he said." |
| Chapter 43's Matthew 9:9: *et ait illī: "Sequere mē."* What's *ait* doing? | "**He says** to him: 'Follow me.'" The Vulgate's favorite word for "said." Unlike *inquit*, *ait* can stand **before** the quotation. |
| ***Quid ais?*** | "**What do you say?**" In Roman comedy it often means "What?! You don't say!" |
| *Aiunt* with Chapter 42's accusative and infinitive: "They say Grandpa ate the cheese." | ***Aiunt Avum cāseum ēdisse.*** |
| King Pyrrhus of Epirus, about to fight Rome, asks Apollo's oracle for an answer. In Ennius's poem the answer is: *Aiō tē, Aeacidā, Rōmānōs vincere posse.* (*Aeacidēs*: descendant of Aeacus, that is, Pyrrhus.) Two accusatives, one infinitive. Who conquers? | **Either!** "I say that **you** can conquer **the Romans**," or "that **the Romans** can conquer **you**." Chapter 42's trap: with two accusatives, only sense decides. Pyrrhus read it his way, won costly battles, and in the end lost the war. Cicero laughs at the story: *Prīmum Latīnē Apollō numquam locūtus est*: "First of all, Apollo never **spoke** Latin." |

!!! success "The Law of the Slipped-In *Inquit*"
    | | I | you | he, she | they |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | *inquam* | *inquam* | *inquis* | *inquit* | *inquiunt* |
    | *aiō* | *aiō* | *ais* | *ait* | *aiunt* |

    ***Inquit*** only quotes exact words, and it never stands first: it **slips in** after the first word or phrase (*"Salvē," inquit Avus, "Iōannēs!"*). ***Ait*** and ***dīxit*** may stand before the quote, or report with an accusative and infinitive (*Aiunt Avum dormīre*). *Inquit* never does.

## 8. Remember *of*, forget *of*

:: Nōlī oblīvīscī omnēs retribūtiōnēs eius. || Do not forget all his benefits. (Psalm 102:2)

Meminī tuī. Oblīvīscor cāseī? Numquam!

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 21 gave you *meī, tuī, nostrī, vestrī*, "of me, of you…," and said everyday "my" is *meus*, not *meī*. Here's a real job for them: *Meminī tuī.* | "**I remember you**": literally, "I am mindful **of** you." Verbs of remembering love the **genitive**. |
| A **person** you remember: usually genitive. A **thing**: genitive or accusative. "Do you remember Grandpa?" "I remember the cheese." | ***Meministīne Avī? Cāseī meminī*** (or *cāseum meminī*). |
| Vergil, *Eclogues* 7.69, a shepherd after a singing match: *Haec meminī, et victum frūstrā contendere Thyrsim.* (*Victus*: beaten, from *vincō*; *frūstrā*: in vain; *contendō*: compete; *Thyrsis*: the losing shepherd.) | "**I remember these things**, and that Thyrsis, beaten, competed in vain." *Haec*: a thing remembered, accusative. Then Chapter 42's accusative and infinitive. |
| ***Oblīvīscor, oblīvīscī, oblītus sum***: forget (a Chapter 43 deponent). "Don't forget the cheese!" "I forgot." | ***Nōlī oblīvīscī cāseī!*** (or *cāseum*) · ***Oblītus sum*** (a woman: *oblīta sum*). Same genitive-or-accusative choice as *meminī*. |
| Psalm 102:2 (Vulgate numbering; 103 in English Bibles): *Benedīc, anima mea, Dominō, et nōlī oblīvīscī omnēs retribūtiōnēs eius.* (*Benedīcō*: bless, with a dative, Chapter 44; *anima*: soul; *retribūtiō*: benefit, kindness repaid.) | "Bless the Lord, my soul, and **do not forget all his benefits**." Here the things forgotten are accusative: *omnēs retribūtiōnēs*. |

## 9. Pity, and the charge

:: Miserēre meī, Deus. || Have mercy on me, O God. (Psalm 50:3)

Miserēre meī! Fūrtī accūsō.

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| ***Misereor, miserērī, miseritus sum***: pity, have mercy on. Deponent, with the **genitive**. Chapter 43's deponent command ends in *-re*. "Pity me!" | ***Miserēre meī!*** "Have mercy **on** me": in Latin, "have pity **of** me." |
| Psalm 50:3 (51 in English Bibles): *Miserēre meī, Deus, secundum magnam misericordiam tuam.* (*Secundum* + accusative: according to; *misericordia*: mercy.) | "**Have mercy on me, O God**, according to your great mercy." The opening of Allegri's famous *Miserere*. |
| The Mass's *Agnus Deī* sings ***miserēre nōbīs***. Which case is *nōbīs*? | The **dative** (Chapter 21). Church Latin often uses the dative here; the psalm and classical Latin use the genitive (*meī, nostrī*). Read both; write *nostrī*. |
| ***Accūsō, accūsāre***: accuse. ***Fūrtum, fūrtī*** (n.): theft (Chapter 13's *fūr*, thief). *Avum fūrtī accūsō.* | "**I accuse Grandpa of theft.**" The person is accusative; the crime is genitive. Grammar books call this the **genitive of the charge**: "the 'of what crime' genitive." |
| ***Damnō, damnāre***: condemn. Chapter 40's *absolvō*: acquit. *Avus fūrtī damnātus est · Avus fūrtī absolūtus est.* | "Grandpa was **condemned for theft**" · "Grandpa was **acquitted of theft**." Same genitive of the charge. |

## 10. The feeling "it" verbs

:: Taedet caelī convexa tuērī. || It wearies her to look at the vault of the sky. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 4.451)

Mē pudet. Mē taedet. Mē paenitet. Mē miseret.

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 44's Law of the Empty "It": *oportet* and *decet* put the person in the **accusative**. Four "it" verbs of **feeling** do the same. ***Pudet***: it shames. *Mē pudet.* | "**It shames me**" = "**I'm ashamed.**" The one who feels is accusative. Grammar books call these **impersonal verbs of feeling**: plain words, "feeling 'it' verbs." |
| What you're ashamed **of**? The genitive. *Avum pudet fūrtī.* | "**Grandpa is ashamed of the theft.**" Accusative for the feeler, genitive for the cause. |
| ***Paenitet***: it causes regret. *Mē paenitet cēnae meae.* | "**I'm sorry about** my dinner" (I regret it). Matthew 4:17's *paenitentia*, "repentance," is its noun. |
| ***Taedet***: it wearies, it bores. "We're tired of the trip." | ***Nōs taedet itineris.*** |
| ***Miseret***: it moves to pity. *Mē tuī miseret.* | "**I pity you.**" The same idea as *misereor* + genitive, with the feeler in the accusative. |
| The cause can also be an **infinitive**. Vergil, *Aeneid* 4.450–451: Dido, knowing Aeneas will leave her, *mortem ōrat; taedet caelī convexa tuērī.* (*Mors*: death; *ōrō*: pray for; *convexa*: the vault; *tuērī*: to look at, a Chapter 43 deponent.) | "She prays for death; **it wearies her to look at the vault of the sky.**" No accusative written: the story supplies "her." |

!!! info "Four feeling verbs"
    | verb | feeling | example | English |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | *miseret* | pity | *Mē tuī miseret.* | I pity you. |
    | *paenitet* | regret | *Mē paenitet cēnae.* | I'm sorry about the dinner. |
    | *pudet* | shame | *Mē pudet fūrtī.* | I'm ashamed of the theft. |
    | *taedet* | weariness | *Mē taedet itineris.* | I'm tired of the trip. |

    The feeler is **accusative**; the cause is **genitive** or an infinitive. They have "it" forms only: *pudet, pudēbat, puduit…* (A fifth, *piget*, "it annoys," works the same way.)

## 11. Adjectives that need an "of"

:: Integer vītae scelerisque pūrus. || Upright in life and free of crime. (Horace, *Odes* 1.22.1)

Cāseī cupidus. Reī mīlitāris perītus.

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 44's Catullus had *cupidus*, "eager." Eager **for** what? The genitive. "Grandpa is eager for cheese." | ***Avus cāseī cupidus est.*** |
| ***Perītus, -a, -um***: skilled, experienced (*imperītus*: unskilled). Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.21.4: *P. Cōnsidius, quī reī mīlitāris perītissimus habēbātur.* (*P.* = *Pūblius*; *rēs mīlitāris*: military science; *habeor*: be considered.) | "Publius Considius, who was considered **most experienced in military science**." Chapter 33's superlative, and the genitive says *in what*. |
| Chapter 10's Catullus 13.8: *plēnus sacculus est arāneārum.* | "The purse is **full of cobwebs**." *Plēnus* takes the genitive (sometimes the ablative). You've been using this since Chapter 10. |
| ***Memor, memoris***: mindful, remembering (*immemor*: forgetful). Psalm 110:5: *Ēscam dedit timentibus sē; memor erit in saeculum testāmentī suī.* (*Ēsca*: food, from *edō*; *timentēs*: those who fear, Chapter 38; *in saeculum*: forever; *testāmentum*: covenant.) | "He has given **food** to those who fear him; he will be **mindful of his covenant** forever." *Memor* + genitive, like *meminī*. |
| The motto, Horace, *Odes* 1.22.1: *Integer vītae scelerisque pūrus.* (*Integer*: whole, upright; *scelus, sceleris*: crime; *pūrus*: pure.) | "**Upright in life and free of crime.**" Poets stretch the genitive to mean "in respect of": whole **in** life, pure **of** crime. |

!!! success "The Law of the Genitive's Grip"
    Some verbs and adjectives finish their thought with an "**of**," and Latin's "of" case is the genitive:

    | what | example | English |
    |---|---|---|
    | remember, forget | *meminī tuī · oblīvīscor cāseī* (things may be accusative) | I remember you · I forget the cheese |
    | pity | *Miserēre meī* | Have mercy on me |
    | accuse, condemn, acquit | *Avum fūrtī accūsō* | I accuse Grandpa of theft |
    | feeling "it" verbs | *Mē pudet fūrtī* | I'm ashamed of the theft |
    | wanting, knowing, remembering, full | *cupidus, perītus, memor, immemor, plēnus* (and Chapter 44's *similis*: *Patris similis*) | eager for, skilled in, mindful of, full of |

    If English says "of" or "for" and Latin won't take a preposition, try the genitive.

## 13. Look-alikes and traps

:: Ōdī, nōn ōderam. || I hate, not I hated. (teaching line)

Meminī? Memineram?

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Meminī* is a perfect. So it means "I remembered"? | **No.** ***Meminī*** = I remember (now). "I remembered" is ***memineram***. Same for *ōdī/ōderam*, *nōvī/nōveram*. |
| *Coepī* is a perfect. So it means "I begin"? | **No:** ***coepī*** = **I began**, a real past. "I begin" is ***incipiō***. *Coepī* is the odd one out. |
| *Inquit: "Salvē."* | Wrong order. ***"Salvē," inquit.*** *Inquit* never stands before the quote. |
| *Mē pudet* vs *Ego pudeō*? | ***Mē pudet***, "it shames me." Classical Latin says it with the "it" form: the feeler is accusative. |

## 14. Fix it

:: Errāre hūmānum est. || To err is human. (later saying; Chapter 41)

Ego pudeō? Mē pudet!

{#g-14}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "I'm tired of the trip": *Ego taedeō iter.* | ***Mē taedet itineris.*** Feeler accusative, cause genitive. |
| "Yesterday I remembered the cheese": *Herī meminī cāseī.* | ***Herī memineram cāseī*** (or *cāseum*). The past of *meminī* is the pluperfect. |
| "Grandpa is eager for cheese": *Avus cāseum cupidus est.* | ***Avus cāseī cupidus est.*** *Cupidus* takes the genitive. |
| "I'm beginning to sing": *Coepiō cantāre.* | ***Cantāre incipiō.*** There is no *coepiō*: the present comes from *incipiō*. |

## 15. Lectiō: *Meministīne Cāseum?*

:: Forsan et haec ōlim meminisse iuvābit. || Maybe someday it will be a pleasure to remember even these things. (Vergil; today's reading)

Meministīne cāseum, Ave?

Diē quārtā decimā: the morning after Chapter 45. The cheese is in Grandpa's pack, and the family is walking to Rome, five miles. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. As you read, hunt the perfects that mean now, every *inquit*, and every genitive that finishes a verb or an adjective. New in passing: *quārtus decimus*, fourteenth; *prīma lūx*, first light (Chapter 31's *lūx*); *pondus*, weight; *mīliārium*, milestone; *lambō*, lick. Old friends: *cauda* (Chapter 43), *relinquō* (Chapter 36), *ōs* (Chapter 14), *sarcina* (Chapter 45), *iter* (Chapter 45), *sequor* (Chapter 43), *licet* (Chapter 44), *forum* (Chapter 7), *humī* (Chapter 45), and an ablative absolute (Chapter 40).

::: dialogue lectio
Iōannēs | Diē quārtā decimā, prīmā lūce, Rōmam ambulāre coepimus. Avus sarcinam portat; in sarcinā est cāseus. || John | On the fourteenth day, at first light, we began to walk to Rome. Grandpa carries the pack; in the pack is the cheese.
Avia | Ave, meministīne cāseum? || Grandma | Grandpa, do you remember the cheese?
Avus | Meminī! Cāseī semper meminī. || Grandpa | I remember! I always remember cheese.
Pater | Iter longum est. Cantāre incipiam! || Dad | The trip is long. I'll start singing!
Omnēs | Nōn licet! || Everyone | No, you may not!
Iōannēs | "Ōdī," inquit Pater, "nōn cantāre!" || John | "I hate," says Dad, "not singing!"
Avus | Hanc viam nōvī! Abhinc quīnquāgintā annōs hāc viā Rōmam ambulāvī. Meminī… || Grandpa | I know this road! Fifty years ago I walked to Rome by this road. I remember…
Marcus | Nōn iterum, Ave! Nōs taedet fābulārum! || Marcus | Not again, Grandpa! We're tired of stories!
Avus | Mē nōn taedet! Mīles eram, reī mīlitāris perītissimus. || Grandpa | I'm not tired of them! I was a soldier, extremely skilled in military matters.
Cornēlia | Ave, ēsurīmus. Cāseī cupidī sumus! || Cornelia | Grandpa, we're hungry. We're eager for the cheese!
Avus | Nōn in itinere! Rōmae edēmus. || Grandpa | Not on the way! We'll eat at Rome.
Quīntus | Herī dīxistī: "In itinere cāseum edēmus." || Quintus | Yesterday you said: "We'll eat the cheese on the way."
Avus | Nōn meminī. || Grandpa | I don't remember.
Iōannēs | Duo mīlia passuum ambulāmus. Canis nōs sequitur. || John | We walk two miles. The dog follows us.
Avia | Ecce canis! Nōnne domī eum relīquimus? || Grandma | Look, the dog! Didn't we leave him at home?
Pater | Canis cāseī meminit. || Dad | The dog remembers the cheese.
Iōannēs | Ad secundum mīliārium Avus sarcinam humī posuit et dormīre coepit. || John | At the second milestone Grandpa set the pack on the ground and began to sleep.
Marcus | Avus dormit, et canis… canis sarcinam spectat. || Marcus | Grandpa is sleeping, and the dog… the dog is watching the pack.
Iōannēs | Hōrā tertiā Avus surgit, et sarcinam portāre incipit. "Pondus minus est," inquit, "sed mīlitī nihil est!" || John | At the third hour Grandpa gets up and starts carrying the pack. "The weight is less," he says, "but to a soldier, that's nothing!"
Iōannēs | Hōrā quīntā Rōmam vēnimus. In forō sedēmus. Avus sarcinam aperit. || John | At the fifth hour we came to Rome. We sit in the forum. Grandpa opens the pack.
Avus | Ecce cāseus… ubi est cāseus? Sarcina vacua est! || Grandpa | Here's the cheese… where's the cheese? The pack is empty!
Avia | Ave, quid meministī? || Grandma | Grandpa, what do you remember?
Avus | Nihil meminī. Oblītus sum. || Grandpa | I remember nothing. I've forgotten.
Avia | Tū, immemor? Cāseī numquam immemor es! || Grandma | You, forgetful? You're never forgetful of cheese!
Quīntus | Ave, tē fūrtī accūsō! || Quintus | Grandpa, I accuse you of theft!
Avus | Nōn ego! Hunc cāseum nōn nōvī! || Grandpa | Not me! I don't know this cheese!
Cornēlia | Et canis? Canis tōtum iter nōs sequēbātur… et sarcinam spectābat. || Cornelia | And the dog? The dog was following us the whole way… and watching the pack.
Iōannēs | Omnēs canem spectant. Canis caudam movet et ōs lambit. || John | Everyone looks at the dog. The dog wags his tail and licks his mouth.
Omnēs | In stomachō… || Everyone | In the stomach of…
Avus | …canis! Mē dormiente! Mē pudet. || Grandpa | …the dog! While I was sleeping! I'm ashamed.
Avia | Mē tuī miseret, Ave. Sed mē nōn paenitet: domī alterum cāseum habeō! || Grandma | I feel sorry for you, Grandpa. But I'm not sorry: I have another cheese at home!
Avus | Ōdī et amō: canem ōdī, Aviam amō! || Grandpa | I hate and I love: I hate the dog, I love Grandma!
Iōannēs | "Rōmae sumus," inquit Pater. "Nunc cantāre incipiō!" || John | "We're at Rome," says Dad. "Now I'm starting to sing!"
Omnēs | Nōn licet! || Everyone | No, you may not!
Avia | Forsan et haec ōlim meminisse iuvābit. || Grandma | Maybe someday it will be a pleasure to remember even this.

{#g-15}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Grandma: *Meministīne cāseum?* Grandpa: *Cāseī semper meminī.* Two ways to remember the cheese. Which cases? | ***Cāseum***: accusative; ***cāseī***: genitive. *Meminī* takes either for a thing. |
| *"Ōdī," inquit Pater, "nōn cantāre!"* Where's *inquit*, and what does Dad hate? | Slipped in after the first word, with the speaker after it. He hates **not singing**: the infinitive phrase *nōn cantāre* is the thing hated. |
| *Rōmam ambulāre coepimus · Cantāre incipiam · Sarcinam portāre incipit.* Why *coepimus* in one and *incipiō* in the others? | *Coepimus* is a past (we began). For present and future, Latin borrows *incipiō*: *incipiam*, I'll begin; *incipit*, he begins. |
| Grandpa: *Hunc cāseum nōn nōvī!* Whose line is he borrowing? | **Peter's**, Matthew 26:72: *Nōn nōvī hominem!* A denial, with *nōvī* = "I know." |
| *Mē pudet · Mē tuī miseret · Mē nōn paenitet.* Who feels what? | Grandpa **is ashamed**; Grandma **pities** Grandpa (*tuī*, genitive); Grandma **is not sorry**. Every feeler is *mē*, accusative. |
| Grandpa: *Ōdī et amō: canem ōdī, Aviam amō!* | Catullus 85, split in two: he hates the dog and loves Grandma. *Ōdī* sits beside *amō*, both "now." |

## 16. Sound check

:: Meminī, ōdī, nōvī. || I remember, I hate, I know. (teaching line)

Audī et dīc.

{#g-16}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *meminī · meminisse · ōdī · ōdisse*? | ME-mi-nee · me-mi-NIS-se · OH-dee · oh-DIS-se. In *meminisse* the *-nis-* is long by position (two consonants follow), so it takes the emphasis (stress). |
| *coepī · inquit · aiō · ait · aiunt*? | KOY-pee (*oe* sounds like "oy") · IN-kwit · AI-yoh · A-it · AI-yunt. In *aiō* and *aiunt* the *i* sounds double (*ai-yō*); in *ait* and *ais* the *i* is a vowel: two beats. |
| *oblīvīscor · misereor · paenitet · taedet · pudet*? | ob-lee-WEES-kor · mi-SE-re-or · PYE-ni-tet · TYE-det · PU-det. *Ae* is "eye," *v* is *w*. |
| *Miserēre meī*? | mi-se-RAY-re ME-ee. Church singers say mi-zeh-REH-reh: the classical *s* is always hissed, never *z*. |
