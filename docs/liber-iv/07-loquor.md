# Caput XLIII: Loquor

*Liber IV, Verba Agentia · Chapter 43*

*In which some verbs wear the wrong clothes. Back in Chapter 30 you learned a chunk that looked odd:* Loquerisne Latīnē? Latīnē loquor! *The ending is passive, like* amor, *"I am loved," but the meaning is plain active: "I speak." Latin has a whole family of these verbs, passive on the outside and active on the inside, and some of the commonest verbs in the language belong to it: speak, follow, promise, suffer, be born, die, use, enjoy. Today you meet the family, learn their three dictionary keys, and see the participle Chapters 37 and 39 promised: one that means "having done," not "having been done." Then come the half-and-half verbs (*audeō, ausus sum*), their commands (*Sequere mē!*), and five verbs that take the ablative instead of the accusative. Along the way: why "to die" in Horace's famous line ends in* -ī, *a wine jar born the same year as its poet, Aeneas cheering his shipwrecked men, a tax collector who gets up and follows, Cicero losing his patience, and the poet who said he would not wholly die. Speaking goal: say what you speak, follow, use, and enjoy, and give commands like* Loquere! *and* Fruere!

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Latīnē loquor* · *Canis Avum sequitur* · *Locūtus sum* · *Sequere mē!* · *Loquiminī!* · *Ūtor Latīnā* · *Fruere cāseō!* · *Ausus sum*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** deponent (a verb that looks passive but means active; from *dēpōnō*, "lay aside") · semi-deponent (a half-deponent: active in the present, imperfect, and future; passive-looking but active in the perfect)
    - **Forms:** *hortor, hortāris, hortātur…* · *loquor, loqueris, loquitur…* · three keys: *loquor, loquī, locūtus sum* · infinitives *loquī, locūtus esse, locūtūrus esse* · participles *loquēns, locūtus, locūtūrus* (all active) · commands *loquere! loquiminī!* · *audeō, audēre, ausus sum*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Laid-Aside Voice · the Law of the Having-Done · the Law of the Five Ablative Verbs
    - **Words:** *loquor, sequor, hortor, vereor, patior, mentior, morior, nāscor, arbitror, polliceor, ūtor, fruor, vescor, audeō, gaudeō, soleō*
    - **Reading:** *Sequere Cāseum!* (Grandma uses the cheese for dinner, Grandpa follows the cheese, the dog follows Grandpa, Dad dares to sing, and somebody isn't lying)
    - **Speaking:** say what people speak, follow, and fear in three tenses; give commands with *Loquere!* and *Sequiminī!*; say what you use and enjoy with the ablative

## 1. Passive clothes, active meaning

:: Latīnē loquor! || I speak Latin! (Chapter 30's chunk, now opened)

Loquor: "I am spoken"? Minimē! "I speak."

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 30 handed you a chunk: *Loquerisne Latīnē? Latīnē loquor!* Look at the ending of ***loquor***. What does it look like? | Chapter 30's **passive** ending *-or*, as in *amor*, "I am loved." So *loquor* ought to mean "I am spoken"… |
| But what does it actually mean? | "**I speak.**" Passive clothes, active meaning. *Loqueris*: you speak. *Loquitur*: he or she speaks. |
| Grammar books call a verb like this **deponent**. Plain words? | "A verb that **looks passive but means active**." The name comes from *dēpōnō*, "lay aside" (*dē* + Chapter 31's *pōnō*): the usual explanation is that these verbs have **laid aside** their passive meaning and kept only the passive look. Chapter 30 named it in passing. |
| Is there an active *loquō, loquis, loquit*? | **No.** A deponent has only the passive set of endings (apart from a few active-looking forms in sections 5 and 6), and they all mean active. |
| Compare Chapter 30's ***videor***, "I am seen, I seem." Is *videor* deponent? | **No.** *Videor* is the real passive of an ordinary verb, *videō*, "I see." *Loquor* has no active partner; there's no *loquō* for it to be the passive of. That's what makes it deponent. |
| So when you meet a passive ending, how do you know which kind it is? | **The dictionary.** If the first key ends in *-or* (*loquor*, not *loquō*), the verb is deponent: translate it active. (Chapter 30's tip still works: if "is being …ed" makes no sense, suspect a deponent.) |

## 2. Chant a deponent

:: Hortor tē: loquere! || I urge you: speak! (teaching line)

Hortor, hortāris, hortātur…

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| A first-family deponent: *hortor*, "I urge, encourage." Chant it with Chapter 30's passive endings. | ***Hortor, hortāris, hortātur, hortāmur, hortāminī, hortantur.*** "I urge, you urge, he/she urges, we urge, y'all urge, they urge." Just like *amor, amāris…* |
| Third family: *loquor*. Chant it like Chapter 30's *legor*. | ***Loquor, loqueris, loquitur, loquimur, loquiminī, loquuntur.*** *Loquuntur*, "they speak," has two *u*'s in a row (lo-KWUN-tur). |
| Second family: *vereor*, "I fear, I'm wary of," like *habeor*. Fourth family: *mentior*, "I lie, tell a lie," like *audior*. | ***Vereor, verēris, verētur, verēmur, verēminī, verentur.*** ***Mentior, mentīris, mentītur, mentīmur, mentīminī, mentiuntur.*** |
| The *-iō* family: *patior*, "I suffer, put up with," like *capior*. | ***Patior, pateris, patitur, patimur, patiminī, patiuntur.*** (*Pateris*: the short *i* turns to *e* before *r*, as in *caperis*.) |
| *Avus mentītur. Canis Avum sequitur.* (*Sequor*: follow.) | "Grandpa **is lying**. The dog **follows** Grandpa." *Avum* is a plain direct object: a deponent takes objects like any active verb. |

!!! success "The Law of the Laid-Aside Voice"
    A **deponent** verb has passive endings and active meaning.

    1. Its dictionary form ends in ***-or***: *loquor*, not *loquō*.
    2. It has no active partner to be the passive of.
    3. Translate it **active**, whatever it wears.

    | family | deponent | chant | meaning |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | 1st | *hortor* | *hortor, hortāris, hortātur…* | I urge |
    | 2nd | *vereor* | *vereor, verēris, verētur…* | I fear |
    | 3rd | *loquor* | *loquor, loqueris, loquitur…* | I speak |
    | 3rd *-iō* | *patior* | *patior, pateris, patitur…* | I suffer |
    | 4th | *mentior* | *mentior, mentīris, mentītur…* | I lie |

## 3. Three keys

:: Locūtus sum. || I have spoken. (teaching line)

Loquor, loquī, locūtus sum.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 25 gave ordinary verbs four keys: *amō, amāre, amāvī, amātum*. What does the third key, *amāvī*, give you? | The **perfect active**: "I loved, I have loved." |
| A deponent has no active perfect. So how many keys? | **Three:** ***loquor, loquī, locūtus sum***: "I speak, to speak, I spoke (have spoken)." The third key is Chapter 31's two-piece perfect, *locūtus* + *sum*. |
| Read these keys: *hortor, hortārī, hortātus sum · vereor, verērī, veritus sum · patior, patī, passus sum · mentior, mentīrī, mentītus sum.* What does *hortātus sum* mean? | "**I urged**, I have urged." Not "I was urged." Active, like everything a deponent says. |
| Chapter 31: *Cāseus ēsus est*, "the cheese was eaten." Now: *Avia locūta est.* | "Grandma **spoke**" (has spoken). *Locūta* is feminine to agree with *Avia*, just as in Chapter 31. Same two pieces, active meaning. |
| "The kids followed the dog." (*Sequor, sequī, secūtus sum*.) | ***Līberī canem secūtī sunt.*** *Secūtī*: masculine plural, for the kids. *Canem*: the object. |
| The second key, *loquī, hortārī, patī*: what kind of form is it? | Chapter 30's **present passive infinitive** (*amārī, habērī, legī, audīrī, capī*), used as the deponent's everyday infinitive: "to speak, to urge, to suffer." |

## 4. Every tense

:: Dum loquimur, fūgerit invida aetās. || While we talk, envious time will have fled. (Horace, *Odes* 1.11.7–8)

Loquēbar, loquar, locūtus eram.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Imperfect: Chapter 30's *amābar, legēbar*. "Grandpa was talking." | ***Avus loquēbātur.*** |
| Future: Chapter 30's *amābor* and *legar*. "I'll speak." "I'll urge." | ***Loquar · hortābor.*** The third family goes *loquar, loquēris, loquētur…*; the first goes *hortābor, hortāberis, hortābitur…* |
| Chapter 30's trap: *legeris* (you are read) vs *legēris* (you will be read). Now: *loqueris · loquēris.* | "**You speak**" · "**you will speak**." Only the length of the *e* tells them apart: short now, long later. |
| Chapter 31's other two-piece tenses: "I had spoken," "I will have spoken." | ***Locūtus eram · locūtus erō.*** (A woman says *locūta eram, locūta erō*.) |
| Chapter 26's motto, from Cato the Elder: *Rem tenē, verba sequentur.* You only recognized *sequentur* then. Now? | *Sequor*, third family, future, "they": "**they will follow**." "Hold on to the matter; the words **will follow**." Like *legentur*. |
| The motto, Horace, *Odes* 1.11.7–8: *Dum loquimur, fūgerit invida / aetās: carpe diem…* (*Dum*: while, Chapter 5's *Dum spīrō, spērō*; *fugiō, fūgī*: flee; *invidus*: envious; *aetās*: time, a lifetime.) Name the tenses. | *Loquimur*: present deponent, "**while we talk**." *Fūgerit*: Chapter 27's future perfect, "**will have fled**." "While we talk, envious time will have fled: **pick the day**." That's where Chapter 6's *Carpe diem* comes from. |

## 5. To speak, to die

:: Dulce et decōrum est prō patriā morī. || It is sweet and fitting to die for your country. (Horace, *Odes* 3.2.13; Chapter 20)

Loquī, sequī, morī.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 20 called *morī* "an odd infinitive for later." It's from ***morior, morī, mortuus sum***, "die." Why does "to die" end in *-ī*? | *Morior* is deponent, so its infinitive wears the passive *-ī* (like *capī*). *Morī*: "**to die**," active. And Chapter 39's *mortuus*, "dead," is its third key: "having died." |
| Horace's very next line, *Odes* 3.2.14: *mors et fugācem persequitur virum.* (*Mors, mortis*: death; *et*: even; *fugāx, fugācis*: running away; *persequor*: chase down, *per* + *sequor*.) | "Death **chases down** even the man who runs away." *Persequitur*: deponent, active; *virum* is its object. |
| Chapter 18's infinitive after *volō* and *possum*, and Chapter 6's *nōlī*: "I want to speak." "I can't follow." "Don't lie!" | ***Loquī volō · Sequī nōn possum · Nōlī mentīrī!*** The deponent infinitive goes anywhere an infinitive goes. |
| Chapter 41's six infinitives. A deponent has three, all active: *loquī · locūtus esse · locūtūrus esse.* Meaning? | "**To speak · to have spoken · to be going to speak.**" Present, perfect, future. *Locūtus esse* looks like *laudātus esse*, "to have been praised," but means "to have spoken." |
| Chapter 42's reported speech, with two deponent verbs of thinking and promising: *Arbitror Avum mentīrī.* *Avus pollicētur sē nōn mentītūrum esse.* (*Arbitror*: think, judge; *polliceor*: promise.) | "I think that Grandpa **is lying**." "Grandpa promises that he **won't lie**." *Arbitror* and *polliceor* open the door like *putō* and *spērō*; *mentītūrum* agrees with *sē*. |

## 6. The having-done participle

:: Ō passī graviōra, dabit deus hīs quoque fīnem. || O you who have suffered worse, a god will grant an end to these too. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 1.199)

Loquēns, locūtus, locūtūrus.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 37: every fourth-key adjective meant "having been …ed." Chapter 39: ordinary verbs have no participle for "having eaten." Now ***locūtus***, from the third key, *locūtus sum*. | "**Having spoken.**" Active! A deponent's perfect participle means "having …ed," not "having been …ed." That's the rule-bender Chapters 37 and 39 promised. |
| *Avus, cāseum secūtus, in culīnam vēnit.* | "Grandpa, **having followed** the cheese, came into the kitchen" (after following the cheese). *Secūtus* even takes an object, *cāseum*, like an active verb. |
| The present participle: *loquēns, sequēns, hortāns*. Whose shape is that? | Chapter 38's active ***-ns***: *loquēns, loquentis*, "speaking." Deponents borrow the active shape here: one of the few active-looking forms they own. Meaning: active, same time. |
| The future participle: *locūtūrus, secūtūrus*. And Chapter 39's *moritūrī*? | Chapter 39's active ***-ūrus***: "going to speak, going to follow." *Moritūrī*, from *morior*: "those about to die." Active shape, active meaning. (*Moritūrus* is irregular: it isn't built from *mortuus*.) |
| Give all three participles of *sequor*. | ***Sequēns*** following · ***secūtus*** having followed · ***secūtūrus*** going to follow. All three active. An ordinary verb's three (*laudāns, laudātus, laudātūrus*) are two active and one passive. |
| The motto, Vergil, *Aeneid* 1.199, Aeneas to his shipwrecked men: *Ō passī graviōra, dabit deus hīs quoque fīnem.* (*Patior, passus sum*: suffer; *graviōra*: heavier things, worse things, from *gravis*, heavy; *fīnis*: end.) Who are *passī*? | The men themselves: "**O you who have suffered** worse." A participle used as people, like Chapter 39's *moritūrī*, and active: they **have suffered**, not "have been suffered." "A god will give an end to these too." |

!!! success "The Law of the Having-Done"
    A deponent's participles are **all active**. Its perfect participle (the one inside the third key, *secūtus sum*) means "**having …ed**," the one ordinary verbs don't have.

    | time | ordinary *laudō* | deponent *sequor* |
    |---|---|---|
    | same time | *laudāns*: praising | *sequēns*: following |
    | before | *laudātus*: **having been** praised | *secūtus*: **having** followed |
    | after | *laudātūrus*: going to praise | *secūtūrus*: going to follow |

## 7. Untied pairs, deponent edition

:: Dominō cooperante. || With the Lord working with them. (Mark 16:20)

Sōle oriente. Sōle ortō.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 40's ablative absolute with a deponent participle: *Avō loquente, canis dormit.* | "**While Grandpa talks**, the dog sleeps" (with Grandpa talking). *Loquente*: present participle, ablative, same time. |
| *Orior, orīrī, ortus sum*: rise. *Sōle oriente · Sōle ortō.* (*Sōl*: sun, Chapter 36.) | "**As the sun rises**" · "**when the sun had risen**," at sunrise. *Ortō*: the perfect participle, active: "having risen." |
| *Sōl oriēns* is the rising sun. So what part of the world is *oriēns*? | "**The East**," where the sun rises: English **Orient**. |
| Mark 16:20 (from the longer ending of Mark): *Illī autem profectī praedicāvērunt ubīque, Dominō cooperante…* (*Proficīscor, profectus sum*: set out; *praedicō*: preach; *ubīque*: everywhere; *cooperor*: work together.) | "But they **set out** (having set out) and preached everywhere, **with the Lord working with them**." *Profectī*: active "having set out." *Dominō cooperante*: an ablative absolute built on a deponent's present participle. |

## 8. Commands: *Loquere!*

:: Sequere mē. || Follow me. (Matthew 9:9)

Loquere! Loquiminī!

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Ordinary commands: *Venī! Venīte!* A deponent's commands: ***Loquere!*** to one person, ***Loquiminī!*** to y'all. Meaning? | "**Speak!**" · "**Speak, y'all!**" The plural is the same as "y'all speak," *loquiminī*. The singular ends in *-re*. |
| Trap: *loquere* looks like an infinitive (*legere*). Does it mean "to speak"? | **No.** "To speak" is *loquī*. ***Loquere!*** is a command. Same with ***Sequere!*** follow!, ***Patere!*** put up with it!, ***Ūtere!*** use! |
| The other families: "Urge him!" "Be wary!" And, from Grandpa's mischief, "Lie!" | ***Hortāre eum! · Verēre! · Mentīre!*** To y'all: ***Hortāminī! · Verēminī! · Mentīminī!*** Singular: the stem vowel + *-re*. Plural: the "y'all" form. |
| Matthew 9:9: Jesus sees a tax collector named Matthew, *et ait illī: "Sequere mē." Et surgēns secūtus est eum.* (*Ait*: he says, he said; *surgō*: get up.) | "And he said to him, '**Follow me**.' And getting up, he **followed** him." *Sequere*: a command; *secūtus est*: the perfect, active. *Surgēns* is an ordinary present participle. |
| Grandma at dinner: "Speak Latin, y'all!" Then: "Don't talk, Dad: eat!" | ***Latīnē loquiminī!*** · ***Nōlī loquī, Pater: ede!*** Chapter 6's *nōlī* + infinitive works with *loquī* too. |

## 9. Born and dying

:: Nōn omnis moriar. || I shall not wholly die. (Horace, *Odes* 3.30.6)

Nāscor, nātus sum. Morior, mortuus sum.

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| ***Nāscor, nāscī, nātus sum***: be born. "I was born in this city." | ***In hāc urbe nātus sum*** (a woman: *nāta sum*). Latin's deponent perfect looks passive, and English "was born" happens to look passive too. |
| Chapter 41 told Horace's wine jar in English. Now his own words, *Odes* 3.21.1: *Ō nāta mēcum cōnsule Mānliō…* | "O you, **born** with me in Manlius's consulship" (65 BC). *Nāta* is feminine because the jar, *testa*, is feminine (Horace names it three lines later, *pia testa*); *cōnsule Mānliō* is Chapter 40's year by consul. |
| *Morior* in the future: "I shall die." | ***Moriar.*** The *-iō* family, like *capiar*: *moriar, moriēris, moriētur…* |
| Chapter 33's Horace: *Exēgī monumentum aere perennius*, "I have finished a monument more lasting than bronze." Five lines later, *Odes* 3.30.6–7: *Nōn omnis moriar multaque pars meī / vītābit Libitīnam.* (*Omnis*: here "all of me"; *meī*: of me; *vītō*: avoid, escape; *Libitīna*: the goddess of funerals.) | "**I shall not wholly die**, and a great part of me will escape the funeral goddess." His poems keep him alive, and here you are, reading them. |

## 10. Half-deponents

:: Sapere audē. || Dare to be wise. (Horace, *Epistles* 1.2.40; Chapter 6)

Audeō, ausus sum.

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 6's motto *Sapere audē* came from *audeō, audēre*, "dare": active, *audeō, audēs, audet*. Now "I dared." | ***Ausus sum.*** Not *audīvī* (that's "I heard," from *audiō*) and not *audēvī*. Active present; deponent-style perfect. |
| Grammar books call this a **semi-deponent**. Plain words? | "A **half-deponent**": active in the present, imperfect, and future (Chapter 27's present system), passive-looking but active in the perfect system. Its keys: ***audeō, audēre, ausus sum.*** |
| Two more: *gaudeō, gaudēre, gāvīsus sum*, "be glad" (Chapter 35's *gaudium*, joy), and *soleō, solēre, solitus sum*, "usually do, be used to." "Grandma was glad." | ***Avia gāvīsa est.*** Feminine, to agree with Grandma. And Chapter 42's acc. + inf. works here: *Gaudeō tē venīre*, "I'm glad that you're coming." |
| Chapter 42's Catullus: *namque tū solēbās…*, "for you used to…" Which verb? And: "Grandpa usually sleeps after dinner." | *Solēbās* is the imperfect of ***soleō***: active, because it's the present system. ***Avus post cēnam dormīre solet.*** *Soleō* + infinitive: "usually (does)." |
| "Dad dares to sing." "Dad dared to sing." | ***Pater cantāre audet · Pater cantāre ausus est.*** Same verb, two outfits. (One more, for reading: *fīdō, fīdere, fīsus sum*, "trust.") |

!!! info "Half-deponents"
    | keys | meaning | present system (active) | perfect system (deponent-style) |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | *audeō, audēre, ausus sum* | dare | *audet* he dares | *ausus est* he dared |
    | *gaudeō, gaudēre, gāvīsus sum* | be glad | *gaudet* she is glad | *gāvīsa est* she was glad |
    | *soleō, solēre, solitus sum* | usually do | *solet* he usually… | *solitus est* he used to… |
    | *fīdō, fīdere, fīsus sum* | trust | *fīdit* he trusts | *fīsus est* he trusted |

## 11. *Ūtor* and the ablative

:: Quō usque tandem abūtēre, Catilīna, patientiā nostrā? || How long, Catiline, will you go on abusing our patience? (Cicero, *Against Catiline* 1.1)

Ūtor Latīnā.

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| ***Ūtor, ūtī, ūsus sum***: use. "I use Latin." Is it *Ūtor Latīnam*? | **No:** ***Ūtor Latīnā.*** *Ūtor* takes the **ablative**, not the accusative. |
| Why the ablative? Think of Chapter 30's tool in the ablative, *manū*, "by hand." | Grammar books file it under Chapter 30's **ablative of means**: *ūtor* is roughly "I serve myself **by means of**." *Ūtor Latīnā*: "I serve myself with Latin." The thing used is a tool, so it's ablative. |
| Four more verbs work the same way: *fruor* (enjoy), *fungor* (perform, carry out), *potior* (get hold of), *vescor* (feed on). "Enjoy the cheese!" to one, then to y'all. | ***Fruere cāseō! · Fruiminī cāseō!*** (*Fruor, fruī, frūctus sum*: enjoy, "get pleasure from.") Ablative *cāseō* both times. |
| "The dog gets hold of the cheese." "The dog feeds on the cheese." (*Potior, potīrī, potītus sum; vescor, vescī*, no third key.) | ***Canis cāseō potītur. · Canis cāseō vescitur.*** Ablative again. |
| Cicero's first speech against Catiline opens: *Quō usque tandem abūtēre, Catilīna, patientiā nostrā?* (*Quō usque*: how far, how long; *tandem*: in a question, "tell me"; *abūtor*: use up, abuse; *patientia*: patience.) | "How long, tell me, **will you abuse** our patience, Catiline?" *Abūtēre* = *abūtēris*, future "you" (Chapter 30's warning: *-re* can stand for *-ris*, and not only in poets). *Patientiā nostrā* is ablative because of *abūtor*. |
| Vergil, *Aeneid* 1.546–547: a Trojan tells Dido, not knowing whether his lost king is alive, *quem sī fāta virum servant, sī vescitur aurā / aetheriā…* (*Quem … virum*: this man, Aeneas; *fātum*: fate; *aura aetheria*: the air of heaven.) | "If the fates are keeping this man safe, if he still **feeds on the air of heaven**…": a poet's way of saying "if he's still **alive**." *Vescitur* + the ablative *aurā aetheriā*. |

!!! success "The Law of the Five Ablative Verbs"
    Five deponents take the **ablative**, the "by means of" case, instead of an accusative object:

    | verb | meaning | example |
    |---|---|---|
    | *ūtor, ūtī, ūsus sum* | use | *Ūtor Latīnā.* I use Latin. |
    | *fruor, fruī, frūctus sum* | enjoy | *Fruere cāseō!* Enjoy the cheese! |
    | *fungor, fungī, fūnctus sum* | perform, carry out | *Officiō fungor.* I do my duty. |
    | *potior, potīrī, potītus sum* | get hold of | *Canis cāseō potītur.* The dog gets hold of the cheese. |
    | *vescor, vescī* | feed on, eat | *Canis cāseō vescitur.* The dog feeds on the cheese. |

    A memory word some teachers use: **PUFFV**, for *potior, ūtor, fruor, fungor, vescor*.

## 12. Look-alikes and traps

:: Nōn "loquī," sed "loquere!" || Not "to speak," but "speak!" (teaching line)

Loquere? Loquī? Loqueris?

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Loquere! · loquī · loqueris · loquēris* | "**Speak!**" (command) · "**to speak**" · "**you speak**" · "**you will speak**." |
| *Hortātur · laudātur* | "He **urges**" (deponent: active) · "he **is praised**" (ordinary verb: passive). Same ending; the dictionary decides. |
| *Locūtus · laudātus* | "**Having spoken**" · "**having been praised**." The deponent's participle is active. |
| *Ausus sum · audīvī* | "I **dared**" (*audeō*) · "I **heard**" (*audiō*). |
| *Videor · vereor* | "I **seem**, I am seen" (passive of *videō*) · "I **fear**" (deponent; there's no *vereō*). |

## 13. Fix it

:: Nōlī verērī: corrige! || Don't be afraid: fix it! (teaching line)

Loquit? Loquitur!

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Grandpa is talking": *Avus loquit.* | ***Avus loquitur.*** A deponent has no active endings. |
| "I spoke": *Loquīvī.* | ***Locūtus sum.*** No third key in *-ī*: the perfect is two pieces. |
| "Grandma followed the dog": *Avia canem secūtus est.* | ***Avia canem secūta est.*** The participle agrees with Grandma. |
| "Use the cheese!": *Ūtere cāseum!* | ***Ūtere cāseō!*** *Ūtor* takes the ablative. |
| "Dad dared to sing": *Pater cantāre audēvit.* | ***Pater cantāre ausus est.*** *Audeō* is a half-deponent: its perfect is *ausus sum*. |

## 14. Lectiō: *Sequere Cāseum!*

:: Nōn mentior: innocēns sum! || I'm not lying: I'm innocent! (Grandpa, in today's reading)

Avia cāseō ūtitur. Avus cāseum sequitur. Canis…?

Diē ūndecimā: the day after Chapter 42, when Grandma bought a big cheese and Grandpa ate "just a piece." Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. As you read, hunt the deponents and ask: active meaning? which case after *ūtor*, *fruor*, *potior*, *vescor*? New in passing: *parō*, prepare; *tantum*, only; *cārus*, dear; *saltem*, at least; *quaerō*, look for; *absum*, be away, be gone; *laetus*, happy; *cauda*, tail; *moveō*, move, wag.

{#g-14}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Grandma: *Cāseō in cēnā ūtar.* Why *cāseō*? And what tense is *ūtar*? | ***Cāseō***: the ablative, because *ūtor* takes it. ***Ūtar***: future, "I **will use**," third family *-ar* like *legar*. |
| Grandpa: *Tē nōn sequor, cāra… cāseum sequor.* Does *sequor* mean Grandpa is being followed? | **No.** *Sequor* is deponent: "I'm following," not "I'm being followed." He's after the cheese. And for once Grandpa is honest: Grandma says *Saltem nōn mentīris*. |
| Grandma: *Parte cāseī in cēnā ūsa sum; partem in culīnā servāvī.* Why *parte*, then *partem*? | *Ūsa sum* (from *ūtor*) takes the **ablative**, *parte*; *servāvī* is an ordinary verb with an accusative object, *partem*. *Ūsa* is feminine for Grandma. |
| Grandpa: *Sine cāseō moriar! Sed nōn omnis moriar…* Whose line is he stealing? | **Horace's**, *Odes* 3.30.6, *Nōn omnis moriar*: Grandpa's "monument more lasting than bronze" is the part of him that lives on in the cheese. |
| Dad: *Sērō! Iam ausus sum!* What kind of verb, and what does *ausus sum* mean? | The half-deponent ***audeō***: present *audeō* active, perfect ***ausus sum***, "I **have dared**." Passive look, active meaning. |
| Marcus, Cornelia, Quintus: *secūtus est · potītus est · vescēbātur.* Who is the thief, and why did no one hear him? | **The dog**: he stayed in the kitchen, followed the cheese, got hold of it, and was eating it while Dad was singing, so nobody heard a thing. *In stomachō… canis!* And Grandpa, for once, told the truth. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Librō clausō, loquere! || Book closed, speak! (teaching line)

1. Chant *hortor* and *loquor* in the present, then *loquor* in the imperfect (*loquēbar…*) and future (*loquar, loquēris…*).
2. Give the three keys of *loquor, sequor, hortor, vereor, patior, mentior, morior, nāscor, ūtor*, and say what each third key means ("I spoke," not "I was spoken").
3. The three Laws in your own words: the Laid-Aside Voice, the Having-Done, the Five Ablative Verbs.
4. Give the three participles of *sequor* and *loquor*, all active, and use *secūtus* in a sentence about Grandpa and the cheese.
5. Five commands: *Loquere! Loquiminī! Sequere mē! Fruere cāseō! Nōlī mentīrī!*
6. Three half-deponents, present and perfect: *audeō / ausus sum, gaudeō / gāvīsus sum, soleō / solitus sum.*
