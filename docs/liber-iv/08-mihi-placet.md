# Caput XLIV: Mihi Placet

*Liber IV, Verba Agentia · Chapter 44*

*In which the "to/for" case gets a promotion. Back in Chapter 11 the dative was the receiver's case:* Dā mihi pānem. *But Latin also uses it where English has a plain object. Romans don't "like" cheese; cheese "is pleasing **to** them":* Cāseus mihi placet. *Today you meet the verbs with a hidden "to" (obey, harm, believe, forgive, persuade, spare), verbs with a preposition glued on front (be in charge of, block, put on), adjectives like "dear to" and "faithful to," three "it" verbs with no person as subject (it pleases, it's allowed, one ought), and two new jobs for the dative: "as far as I'm concerned" and "for a purpose." Along the way: the Roman's calling in Vergil's underworld, a love poem that ought to be written on water, a prophet who tells a king what isn't allowed, a man who is a wolf to man, and the two little words every detective asks. Speaking goal: say what you like, what you're allowed to do, and what you ought to do, and ask* Licetne mihi?

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Mihi placet* · *Cāseus Avō placet* · *Canis Aviae pāret* · *Crēde mihi!* · *Ignōsce mihi!* · *Persuādē eī!* · *Licetne mihi?* · *Tē oportet dormīre* · *Hoc mihi cūrae est*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** compound verb (a verb with a preposition glued on front) · impersonal verb (an "it" verb: no person as subject) · dative of reference (the "as far as X is concerned" dative) · dative of purpose (the "serves as" dative; with a person, the double dative)
    - **Forms:** *placet, placuit* · *licet, licuit* · *oportet, oportuit* · *Mihi placet ambulāre* · *Licet mihi īre* · *Avum oportet partem dare* · *Canis Aviae auxiliō vēnit*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Hidden "To" · the Law of the Empty "It" · the Law of the Two Datives
    - **Words:** *placeō, pāreō, noceō, faveō, serviō, ignōscō, imperō, persuādeō, studeō, parcō, invideō, adsum, praesum, obstō, cārus, fidēlis, grātus, ūtilis, licet, oportet*
    - **Reading:** *Quis Cāseō Praeerit?* (a new cheese needs a guard, the family votes, Grandpa tries to bribe the dog, and for once somebody is allowed)
    - **Speaking:** say what you and your family like, obey, and believe; ask and give permission with *Licetne?*; say what someone ought to do with *oportet*

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep Chapter 11 close (the dative: *mihi, tibi, Avō, Aviae, canī, līberīs*), and Chapter 42's accusative and infinitive (*Crēdō Avum innocentem esse*).

## 1. The "to/for" case, recalled

:: Deō grātiās. || Thanks be to God. (Chapter 11)

Mihi, tibi, Avō, Aviae.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 11: *Dā mihi pānem.* Which case is *mihi*, and what job is it doing? | The **dative**, Chapter 11's "to/for" case: "Give the bread **to me**." *Mihi* is the receiver. |
| Chapter 11 gave the dative two more jobs: *Nōmen mihi est Iōannēs · Cēnam tibi parō.* | "**My** name is John" (a name is *to me*: the dative of possession) · "I'm making dinner **for you**" (the one you do it for). |
| Chant the pronoun datives: to me, to you, to him or her, to us, to y'all, to them. And Chapter 29's "to whom?" | ***Mihi, tibi, eī, nōbīs, vōbīs, eīs*** (or *iīs*). "To whom?": ***cui***. Nouns: *Avō, Aviae, canī, līberīs*. |
| Chapter 18's motto: *Nēmō potest duōbus dominīs servīre.* Chapter 18 said *servīre* takes the dative. Why is that odd for an English speaker? | In English, "serve two masters" makes the masters look like a plain **object**. Latin thinks "**be a servant to** two masters," so *duōbus dominīs* is dative. |
| The verse goes on, Matthew 6:24: *Nōn potestis Deō servīre et mammōnae.* (*Mammōna*: Mammon, money.) | "Y'all cannot serve God and money." *Deō* and *mammōnae*: dative, after *servīre*. Today the dative takes on new jobs, starting with more verbs like *serviō*. |

## 2. *Mihi placet*: verbs with a hidden "to"

:: Mihi placet. || It pleases me; I like it. (teaching line)

Cāseus mihi placet.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| ***Placeō, placēre, placuī***: please, be pleasing. *Cāseus mihi placet.* Word by word, then in natural English. | "The cheese **is pleasing to me**" = "**I like** the cheese." The cheese is the subject; *mihi* is dative. |
| "Grandpa likes the cheese." Is it *Cāseus Avum placet*? | **No:** ***Cāseus Avō placet.*** The one who is pleased goes in the **dative**: pleasing *to* Grandpa. |
| ***Pāreō, pārēre, pāruī***: obey. *Canis Aviae pāret.* | "The dog **obeys** Grandma." Latin thinks "is obedient **to** Grandma," so *Aviae* is dative. |
| ***Noceō, nocēre, nocuī***: harm (Chapter 38's *innocēns*, "harmless"). *Cāseus canī nocet.* | "Cheese **harms** the dog": "is harmful **to** the dog." *Canī*: the dative of *canis*. |
| ***Faveō, favēre, fāvī***: favor, be on someone's side. ***Serviō, servīre, servīvī***: serve. "Grandma favors the kids. The kids serve Grandma." | ***Avia līberīs favet. Līberī Aviae serviunt.*** Dative both times. (English *favor*, *servant*.) |
| So what's the trick with these verbs? | Hear a hidden "**to**": be pleasing **to**, be obedient **to**, be harmful **to**, be favorable **to**, be a servant **to**. English uses a plain object; Latin uses the dative. Dictionaries mark them "+ dat." |

## 3. Believe, forgive, persuade

:: Ignōsce mihi! || Forgive me! (teaching line)

Crēde mihi. Persuādē eī.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 42: *Crēdō Avum innocentem esse*, "I believe that Grandpa is innocent." Now: *Crēdō tibi.* | "I **believe you**, I trust you": I give my trust **to** you. The person believed goes in the **dative**. Believing a whole statement is Chapter 42's accusative and infinitive. |
| ***Ignōscō, ignōscere, ignōvī***: forgive (give pardon **to**). "Forgive me, Grandma!" "Grandma forgives Grandpa." | ***Ignōsce mihi, Avia! · Avia Avō ignōscit.*** |
| ***Imperō, imperāre***: give orders (**to**), command (English *imperative, emperor*). "Grandma gives orders to the kids." | ***Avia līberīs imperat.*** (What she orders them *to do* needs a construction Liber V will teach; for now, just whom she commands.) |
| Chapter 43: *Hortāre eum!* "Urge him!" Now ***persuādeō, persuādēre, persuāsī***: persuade. "Persuade him!" | ***Persuādē eī!*** *Hortor* takes the accusative (*eum*); *persuādeō* takes the dative (*eī*). Close in meaning, different cases: the dictionary decides. |
| ***Studeō, studēre, studuī***: be keen on, devote yourself to (English *student*). ***Parcō, parcere, pepercī***: spare. *Latīnae studeō.* "Spare the cheese!" | "I'm **keen on** Latin; I study Latin." ***Parce cāseō!*** Both take the dative. |
| ***Invideō, invidēre, invīdī***: envy (Chapter 43's *invidus*, envious). "Grandpa envies the dog." | ***Avus canī invidet.*** The one envied: dative. |

## 4. Real lines with a hidden "to"

:: Parcere subiectīs et dēbellāre superbōs. || To spare the conquered and to crush the proud in war. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 6.853)

Parcere subiectīs.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Vergil, *Aeneid* 6.852–853. In the underworld, Anchises tells the Roman of the future what his calling will be: *(hae tibi erunt artēs), pācīque impōnere mōrem, / parcere subiectīs et dēbellāre superbōs.* (*Ars, artis*: skill, art; *-que*: and; *pāx, pācis*: peace; *impōnō*: put on, impose; *mōs, mōris*: custom, way of life, Chapter 14's *Ō mōrēs*; *subiectus*: put under, conquered; *dēbellō*: crush in war; *superbus*: proud.) First: *hae tibi erunt artēs*? | "**These will be your arts**," "you'll have these skills": *tibi* + *erunt* is Chapter 11's dative of possession. |
| *Parcere subiectīs et dēbellāre superbōs.* Why is *subiectīs* dative but *superbōs* accusative? | ***Parcō*** takes the **dative**; ***dēbellō*** is an ordinary verb with an accusative object. "To **spare the conquered** and to **crush the proud** in war." Same sentence, two cases: each verb picks its own. |
| *Pācīque impōnere mōrem.* | "And to **set** a way of life **upon peace**": to give peace settled customs, so it lasts. *Mōrem* is what's put (accusative); *pācī* is what it's put on (dative). More on *impōnō* in section 5. |
| Chapter 42's Catullus 70.1–2: *Nūllī sē dīcit mulier mea nūbere mālle / quam mihi.* Chapter 42 said *nūbō*, "marry," takes the dative. Now you can see why *nūllī* and *mihi* are dative. | *Nūbō* is said of the bride, and dictionaries explain it as "veil oneself **for**" the groom: a hidden "to/for." "My woman says she'd rather marry **no one** than **me**." |
| Chapter 4's *Omnia vincit Amor* (Vergil, *Eclogues* 10.69): the line goes on to say we should give way to Love, ***Amōrī***. Which case, and why? | The **dative**: *cēdō* (Chapter 42's "go away," + ablative) also means "give way, yield," and then it takes the dative: yield **to** Love. (The verb form Vergil uses waits for Liber V.) |

## 5. Glued-on prepositions

:: Prīncipiīs obstā. || Resist the beginnings. (Ovid, *Remedies for Love* 91)

Adsum tibi.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Ad* (Chapter 8: to, toward) + *sum* = ***adsum***, "I'm here, I'm present." *Adsum tibi.* | "**I'm here for you**, I stand by you." Grammar books call *adsum* a **compound verb**: plain words, "a verb with a preposition glued on front." Many compounds take the **dative**. |
| ***Praesum, praeesse, praefuī*** (*prae*: in front, Chapter 38): be in charge of. *Avia culīnae praeest.* | "Grandma **is in charge of** the kitchen": she stands in front of it. *Culīnae*: dative. (Chapter 38's *praesēns* is this verb's participle.) |
| ***Obstō, obstāre, obstitī*** (*ob*: in the way; *stō*: stand): block, stand in the way of. *Canis iānuae obstat.* | "The dog **blocks** the door." *Iānuae*: dative. |
| Ovid, *Remedies for Love* (*Remedia Amōris*) 91: *Prīncipiīs obstā; sērō medicīna parātur.* (*Prīncipium*: beginning, Chapter 16's *In prīncipiō*; *sērō*: too late, Chapter 27; *medicīna*: medicine, cure.) | "**Resist the beginnings**: the cure is prepared too late." Stop trouble at the start. *Prīncipiīs*: dative after *obstā*; *parātur*: Chapter 30's passive. |
| ***Impōnō*** (*in* + *pōnō*): put on. *Avia cāseum mēnsae impōnit.* Two objects? | "Grandma **puts** the cheese **on the table**." *Cāseum*: accusative, what's put. *Mēnsae*: dative, what it's put on. A compound that already has an object can add a dative too. (Chapter 31's *pōnō* with *in* + ablative says the same: *in mēnsā pōnit*.) |
| ***Appropinquō*** (*ad* + *propinquus*, near): come near, approach. "The dog approaches the cheese." | ***Canis cāseō appropinquat.*** (*Ad cāseum* is fine too.) Not every compound takes the dative: the dictionary's "+ dat." tells you which. |

!!! info "Compounds that often take the dative"
    | prefix | meaning | example |
    |---|---|---|
    | *ad-* | to, at | *adsum tibi* I stand by you · *appropinquō cāseō* I approach the cheese |
    | *prae-* | in front | *praesum culīnae* I'm in charge of the kitchen |
    | *ob-* | in the way | *obstō iānuae* I block the door |
    | *in-* | on, in | *impōnō cāseum mēnsae* I put the cheese on the table |
    | *inter-* | between, among | *intersum cēnae* I take part in dinner |

    Others on the old grammar list (Allen and Greenough §370): *ante-, con-, post-, prō-, sub-, super-.*

## 6. Dear to me

:: Cāseus mihi cārus est. || Cheese is dear to me. (Grandpa's creed, teaching line)

Cārus mihi, fidēlis tibi.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| English says "dear **to** me." Latin? *Cāseus mihi cārus est.* (*Cārus*: dear, Chapter 43.) | "The cheese **is dear to me**." Adjectives that mean "___ to someone" take the **dative** too. |
| *Canis Aviae fidēlis est. Avus canī amīcus nōn est.* (*Fidēlis*: faithful; *amīcus*, as an adjective: friendly.) | "The dog is **faithful to Grandma**. Grandpa is **not friendly to the dog**." Opposite: ***inimīcus***, "unfriendly, hostile" (*in-* "not" + *amīcus*). |
| ***Grātus***: pleasing, welcome; ***ūtilis***: useful. "Dinner is welcome to everyone. Latin is useful to me." | ***Cēna omnibus grāta est. Latīna mihi ūtilis est.*** |
| Chapter 33's ***similis***: like. *Marcus Patrī similis est.* | "Marcus **is like** Dad." *Similis* takes the dative (and sometimes the genitive, *Patris*, especially with people). |

!!! success "The Law of the Hidden “To”"
    When a verb or adjective means "**be ___ to**" someone, the someone goes in the **dative**, even where English uses a plain object.

    1. **Verbs** with a hidden "to": please, obey, harm, favor, serve, believe, forgive, command, persuade, be keen on, spare, envy.
    2. **Compound verbs**, with a preposition glued on front: *adsum, praesum, obstō, impōnō, appropinquō.*
    3. **Adjectives** of "to": dear, friendly, hostile, welcome, useful, faithful, like.

    | Latin | English thinks | Latin thinks |
    |---|---|---|
    | *Cāseus Avō placet.* | Grandpa likes the cheese. | The cheese is pleasing **to** Grandpa. |
    | *Canis Aviae pāret.* | The dog obeys Grandma. | The dog is obedient **to** Grandma. |
    | *Crēdō tibi.* | I believe you. | I give trust **to** you. |
    | *Persuādē eī!* | Persuade him! | Make it agreeable **to** him! |
    | *Parce cāseō!* | Spare the cheese! | Be lenient **to** the cheese! |
    | *Avia culīnae praeest.* | Grandma runs the kitchen. | Grandma is set in front, **over** the kitchen (the prefix *prae-* brings the dative). |

    The dictionary marks each one "+ dat." When in doubt, look it up.

## 7. *Placet*: an "it" verb

:: Placetne tibi? Placet! || Is that all right with you? It is! (teaching line)

Mihi placet ambulāre.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Mihi placet ambulāre.* Who or what is doing the pleasing? | No person: **walking** (*ambulāre*) is. "Walking is pleasing to me" = "**I like to walk.**" English fills the gap with "it": "It pleases me to walk." |
| Grammar books call a verb like this, used with no person as its subject, an **impersonal verb**. Plain words? | "An **'it' verb**": no person or thing as its subject, just the "it" form, third person singular: *placet, placuit, placēbit*. *Placeō* can also be an ordinary verb (*Cāseus mihi placet*: the cheese is the subject); with an infinitive or on its own it's impersonal. *Licet* and *oportet* (sections 8 and 9) are "it" verbs only. (*In-* "not" + *persōna*, a person.) |
| *Placetne tibi? · Placet. · Nōn placet.* | "Is that all right with you?" (Do you agree?) · "**Yes**" (it pleases) · "**No**." Church councils and some old universities still vote with *Placet* and *Nōn placet*. And English *placebo* is Latin *placēbō*, "I will please." |
| The perfect, *placuit*: *Avō placuit cāseum edere.* And *placuit* can also mean "it was decided": *Aviae placuit novum cāseum emere.* | "Grandpa **liked** eating the cheese." · "Grandma **decided** to buy a new cheese" (it seemed good to Grandma). |
| Horace, *Epistles* 1.17.35: *prīncipibus placuisse virīs nōn ultima laus est.* (*Prīnceps, prīncipis*: leading man (related to Chapter 22's *prīmus*); *ultimus*: last, least; *laus*: praise.) | "To **have pleased** leading men is not the least praise." *Placuisse*: Chapter 41's perfect infinitive, the subject; *prīncipibus virīs*: dative, the ones pleased. |
| Horace's next line, 1.17.36: *Nōn cuivīs hominī contingit adīre Corinthum.* (*Contingit*: it falls to, it happens to; *cuivīs*: to any man at all, Chapter 29's *cui* + *vīs*; *adīre*: to go to; *Corinthus*: Corinth, the richest, priciest city in Greece.) | "**It isn't every man's luck** to get to Corinth." *Contingit* is another "it" verb with a dative. Romans quoted it about any privilege. |

## 8. *Licet*: it's allowed

:: Nōn licet tibi habēre eam. || It is not lawful for you to have her. (Matthew 14:4)

Licetne mihi?

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| ***Licet, licēre, licuit***: it is allowed. *Licet mihi īre.* | "It is allowed **to me** to go" = "**I may go**, I'm allowed to go." The pattern: *licet* + **dative** (who) + **infinitive** (what). |
| "May I eat the cheese?" "No, you may not!" | ***Licetne mihi cāseum edere? · Nōn licet!*** (English *license, illicit*.) |
| Matthew 14:4: John the Baptist tells King Herod, who had taken his brother's wife: *Nōn licet tibi habēre eam.* | "**It is not lawful for you** to have her." *Tibi*: dative; *habēre*: the infinitive. Telling a king "no" cost John his head. |
| Matthew 22:17, a trick question put to Jesus: *Licet cēnsum dare Caesarī an nōn?* (*Cēnsus*: tax; *an*: or.) Is *Caesarī* the person allowed? | **No.** "**Is it lawful** to pay the tax to Caesar, or not?" *Caesarī* is the receiver after *dare* (Chapter 11). No one is named as allowed: it's "allowed for anyone." |
| Chapter 38 glossed *libet*, "it pleases." *Mihi libet · Mihi licet.* | "**I feel like it**" · "**I'm allowed.**" One letter apart: *libet* is what you want; *licet* is what the rules permit. |

## 9. *Oportet*: one ought

:: Illum oportet crēscere, mē autem minuī. || He must increase, but I must decrease. (John 3:30)

Tē oportet dormīre.

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 4's very first motto: *Ēsse oportet ut vīvās, nōn vīvere ut edās.* Find the "it" verb. | ***Oportet***, "it is right, **one ought**." *Ēsse* (an old form of *edere*, "to eat") is its subject: "**One ought to eat**…" (The *ut vīvās* part, "so that you may live," waits for Liber V.) |
| *Oportet* puts the person in the **accusative**, with an infinitive: Chapter 42's pattern. *Tē oportet dormīre.* | "**You ought** to sleep" (that you sleep is right). *Tē*, not *tibi*: *oportet* is the odd one out. |
| "Grandpa ought to give a piece." | ***Avum oportet partem dare.*** *Avum*: accusative; *dare*: infinitive. |
| John 3:7, Jesus to Nicodemus: *Oportet vōs nāscī dēnuō.* (*Dēnuō*: anew, again; *nāscor*: be born, Chapter 43.) | "**Y'all must** be born again." *Vōs*: accusative; *nāscī*: Chapter 43's deponent infinitive. |
| John 3:30, John the Baptist about Jesus: *Illum oportet crēscere, mē autem minuī.* (*Crēscō*: grow; *minuō*: make smaller; *minuī*: to be made smaller.) | "**He must** increase, but **I must** decrease." Two accusatives with infinitives: *illum crēscere*, *mē minuī* (a passive infinitive, Chapter 30). |
| Chapter 42 told the end of Catullus 70 in English. Now the Latin, 70.3–4: *dīcit: sed mulier cupidō quod dīcit amantī, / in ventō et rapidā scrībere oportet aquā.* (*Cupidus*: eager; *amāns*: lover, Chapter 38; *quod*: what (= that which), Chapter 28's relative; *ventus*: wind; *rapidus*: swift; *scrībō*: write; *aqua*: water.) | "She says so: but what a woman says to an eager lover **ought to be written** on the wind and on swift water." *Cupidō amantī*: dative, the one she says it to. *Oportet* + *scrībere*, with no person: "one ought to write." |

!!! success "The Law of the Empty “It”"
    An **impersonal** verb has no person as its subject: it is used in the "it" form only, and English adds "it." (*Placeō* also has ordinary forms, *Cāseus mihi placet*; *licet* and *oportet* are "it" verbs only.)

    | verb | perfect | meaning | the person goes in the | example |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | *placet* | *placuit* | it pleases; it's agreed | **dative** | *Mihi placet ambulāre.* I like to walk. |
    | *licet* | *licuit* | it's allowed | **dative** | *Licet mihi īre.* I may go. |
    | *oportet* | *oportuit* | it's right, one ought | **accusative** | *Tē oportet dormīre.* You ought to sleep. |
    | *libet* | *libuit* | it pleases, one feels like | dative | *Mihi libet.* I feel like it. |
    | *decet* | *decuit* | it's fitting, it suits | accusative | *Tē decet.* It suits you. |

    What you like, may, or ought **to do** is an infinitive. (Chapter 20's *Dulce et decōrum est*: *decōrus*, "fitting," comes from *decet*.)

## 10. "As far as I'm concerned"

:: Lupus est homō hominī. || Man is a wolf to man. (Plautus, *The Comedy of Asses* 495)

Mihi vidētur…

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 30's chunk: *Mihi vidētur…*, "it seems to me." Whose point of view is *mihi*? | **Mine**: "as far as I'm concerned." Grammar books call this the **dative of reference**: plain words, "the 'as far as X is concerned' dative," the person something is true for. When it's for someone's good or harm, older grammars call it the dative of advantage or disadvantage. |
| *Avō cāseus vīta est.* | "**To Grandpa**, cheese is life." As far as Grandpa is concerned. |
| Plautus, *The Comedy of Asses* (*Asināria*) 495: *Lupus est homō hominī.* (*Lupus*: wolf.) | "Man is a wolf **to man**." (The line goes on: to a man he doesn't know.) *Hominī*: dative of reference. |
| Seneca grumbles in a letter (*Letters* 106.12): *nōn vītae sed scholae discimus.* (*Schola*: school; *discō*: learn.) | "We learn not **for life** but **for school**." Teachers later flipped it into a school motto: ***Nōn scholae sed vītae discimus***, "We learn not for school but for life." The dative of advantage: whom (or what) it's for. |
| Chapter 14's chunk: *Caput mihi dolet*, "my head hurts" (the head hurts **to me**). Now: *Stomachus canī dolet.* | "**The dog's** stomach hurts." Chapter 14's *mihi* was a dative of reference all along: the person it hurts **for**. It does the job English gives to "my" and "the dog's." Watch for it in the *Lectiō*. |

## 11. Two datives

:: Cui bonō? || Who stood to gain? (Cicero, *For Roscius of Ameria* 84; Chapter 29)

Hoc mihi cūrae est.

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Cūra*: care, worry (Chapter 35). *Hoc mihi cūrae est.* Word by word? | "This is **for a worry to me**" = "**This worries me**, I care about this." *Cūrae*: what it serves as. *Mihi*: whom it concerns. |
| Grammar books call *cūrae* a **dative of purpose**. Plain words? | "**The 'serves as' dative**": what something is *for*, or works as. Paired with a person's dative, it's the **double dative**. Older books also say "predicate dative." |
| *Cāseus Avō cūrae est.* | "**Grandpa cares about** the cheese" (the cheese is a concern to Grandpa). |
| *Auxilium*: help (Chapter 17). *Canis Aviae auxiliō vēnit.* | "The dog **came to help** Grandma": came **as a help to** Grandma. Caesar's soldiers are forever coming *auxiliō*. |
| Chapter 29's *Cui bonō?* Crack it now. | Two datives: ***cui***, "to whom" (reference), + ***bonō***, "as a benefit" (purpose). "**To whom** was it **a benefit**?" = "Who stood to gain?" |

!!! success "The Law of the Two Datives"
    A noun in the dative can say what something **serves as** (*cūrae*, *auxiliō*, *bonō*), and a second dative can say **for whom**.

    | Latin | word by word | natural English |
    |---|---|---|
    | *Hoc mihi cūrae est.* | This is for a worry to me. | This worries me. |
    | *Canis Aviae auxiliō vēnit.* | The dog came for a help to Grandma. | The dog came to help Grandma. |
    | *Cui bonō?* | To whom for a benefit? | Who stood to gain? |
    | *Avus līberīs exemplō est.* | Grandpa is for an example to the kids. | Grandpa sets the kids an example. |

    The "serves as" noun is usually singular, with no adjective. The "for whom" dative is the dative of reference.

## 12. Look-alikes and traps

:: Nōn "Avum placet," sed "Avō placet." || Not "Avum placet," but "Avō placet." (teaching line)

Pāret? Parat? Parit?

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Cāseus Avō placet · Avus cāseum amat.* | "Grandpa **likes** the cheese" · "Grandpa **loves** the cheese." With *placeō* the cheese is the subject and Grandpa is dative; with *amō* Grandpa is the subject and the cheese is accusative. |
| *Crēdō tibi · Crēdō tē venīre.* | "I **believe you**" (dative person) · "I believe **that you're coming**" (Chapter 42's accusative and infinitive). |
| *Hortāre eum! · Persuādē eī!* | "**Urge** him!" (accusative) · "**Persuade** him!" (dative). |
| *Licet mihi īre · Oportet mē īre.* | "**I may** go" (dative) · "**I ought** to go" (accusative). |
| *Pāret · parat · parit.* | "He **obeys**" (*pāreō*, long *ā*) · "he **prepares**" (*parō*) · "she **gives birth**" (*pariō*, Chapter 38's *pariēns*). |

## 13. Fix it

:: Ignōsce mihi: corrigam! || Forgive me: I'll fix it! (teaching line)

Aviam pāret? Aviae pāret!

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "The dog obeys Grandma": *Canis Aviam pāret.* | ***Canis Aviae pāret.*** *Pāreō* takes the dative. |
| "Grandpa likes the cheese": *Cāseus Avum placet.* | ***Cāseus Avō placet.*** The one pleased is dative. |
| "You ought to sleep": *Tibi oportet dormīre.* | ***Tē oportet dormīre.*** *Oportet* takes the accusative. |
| "Persuade him!": *Persuādē eum!* | ***Persuādē eī!*** *Persuādeō* takes the dative. |
| "Spare the dog!": *Parce canem!* | ***Parce canī!*** *Parcō* takes the dative. |

## 14. Lectiō: *Quis Cāseō Praeerit?*

:: Canis Aviae pāret. || The dog obeys Grandma. (today's reading)

Quis cāseō praeerit? Avus? Canis?

Diē duodecimā: the day after Chapter 43, when the dog followed the cheese and ate it. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. As you read, hunt the datives and ask: hidden "to," glued-on preposition, adjective, "it" verb, or "serves as"? New in passing: *miser*, poor, wretched; *nēminī*, to no one (Chapter 18's *nēmō*); *crustulum*, cookie; *moveor*, budge; *spectō*, look at; *diū*, for a long time; *suspīrō*, sigh. Old friends: *herī* (Chapter 16), *custōs* (Chapter 39), *saltem* (Chapter 43), *tangō* (Chapter 14), *tōtus* (Chapter 22).

::: dialogue lectio
Iōannēs | Diē duodecimā Avia novum cāseum mēnsae impōnit. Omnēs adsunt. || John | On the twelfth day Grandma puts a new cheese on the table. Everyone is here.
Avia | Herī cāseus canī placuit, sed canī nocuit! || Grandma | Yesterday the cheese pleased the dog, but it hurt the dog!
Quīntus | Miser canis! Stomachus eī dolet. || Quintus | Poor dog! His stomach hurts.
Avia | Hodiē canī cāseum edere nōn licet. Sed quis cāseō praeerit? || Grandma | Today the dog isn't allowed to eat cheese. But who will be in charge of the cheese?
Avus | Ego! Mihi crēde, cāra: cāseō fidēlis sum! || Grandpa | Me! Believe me, dear: I'm faithful to the cheese!
Cornēlia | Fidēlis? Ave, cāseō nōn fidēlis, sed inimīcus es! || Cornelia | Faithful? Grandpa, you're not faithful to the cheese, you're its enemy!
Avia | Placetne vōbīs, līberī? Avus cāseō praeerit. || Grandma | Is that all right with y'all, kids? Grandpa will be in charge of the cheese.
Līberī | Nōn placet! || Kids | No!
Marcus | Canis custōs erit! Canis Aviae semper pāret. || Marcus | The dog will be the guard! The dog always obeys Grandma.
Avia | Placet. Tū, canis, mēnsae obstā! Nēminī licet cāseō appropinquāre! || Grandma | Agreed. You, dog, block the table! No one is allowed to go near the cheese!
Iōannēs | Canis ante mēnsam sedet et mēnsae obstat. Avus canem spectat. || John | The dog sits in front of the table and blocks it. Grandpa looks at the dog.
Avus | Bone canis, mihi favē! Ego tibi amīcus sum. || Grandpa | Good dog, be on my side! I'm your friend.
Canis | Grrr… || Dog | Grrr…
Avus | Tibi crustulum dabō. Persuāsī tibi? || Grandpa | I'll give you a cookie. Have I persuaded you?
Iōannēs | Canis crustulum edit, sed nōn movētur. Aviae, nōn Avō, pāret. || John | The dog eats the cookie, but he doesn't budge. He obeys Grandma, not Grandpa.
Quīntus | Ave, canī nōn persuādēs! Canis Aviae servit. || Quintus | Grandpa, you're not persuading the dog! The dog serves Grandma.
Avus | Hoc mihi nōn placet! Licetne mihi saltem cāseum spectāre? || Grandpa | I don't like this! Am I at least allowed to look at the cheese?
Avia | Spectāre licet; edere nōn licet. || Grandma | Looking is allowed; eating isn't.
Pater | Licetne mihi cantāre? || Dad | Am I allowed to sing?
Omnēs | Nōn licet! || Everyone | No, you're not!
Iōannēs | Avus diū cāseum spectat et suspīrat. || John | Grandpa looks at the cheese for a long time and sighs.
Marcus | Avia, Avus nōbīs cūrae est. Miser est! || Marcus | Grandma, we're worried about Grandpa. He's miserable!
Cornēlia | Et hodiē cāseō nōn nocuit. Ignōsce eī! || Cornelia | And today he hasn't harmed the cheese. Forgive him!
Avia | Bene. Avō ignōscō. Partem tibi dō, Ave: hodiē tibi licet. || Grandma | All right. I forgive Grandpa. I'm giving you a piece, Grandpa: today you're allowed.
Avus | Mihi placet! Sed prīmum, canis, partem tibi dō: tibi amīcus sum! || Grandpa | That suits me! But first, dog, I'm giving you some: I'm your friend!
Iōannēs | Canis cāseum spectat, deinde Aviam. Cāseum nōn tangit. || John | The dog looks at the cheese, then at Grandma. He doesn't touch the cheese.
Quīntus | Vidēsne, Ave? Canis Aviae pāret, nōn stomachō! || Quintus | See, Grandpa? The dog obeys Grandma, not his stomach!
Avus | Ō canis fidēlis! Exemplō mihi es! || Grandpa | O faithful dog! You're an example to me!
Iōannēs | Tum Avus tōtam partem edit. || John | Then Grandpa eats the whole piece.
Omnēs | In stomachō… || Everyone | In the stomach of…
Avia | …Avī! Sed hodiē licuit. || Grandma | …Grandpa! But today it was allowed.
Avus | Mihi placuit! || Grandpa | And I liked it!
Pater | Nunc mihi licet cantāre? || Dad | Now may I sing?
Omnēs | Nōn licet! || Everyone | No, you may not!
:::

{#g-14}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Grandma: *Herī cāseus canī placuit, sed canī nocuit!* Why *canī* twice? | ***Placeō*** and ***noceō*** both take the **dative**: the cheese was pleasing **to** the dog, then harmful **to** the dog. |
| Grandpa: *Mihi crēde, cāra: cāseō fidēlis sum!* Cornelia: *cāseō nōn fidēlis, sed inimīcus es!* Find three datives and say why. | ***Mihi*** after *crēde* (a person believed); ***cāseō*** after *fidēlis* and again after *inimīcus* (adjectives of "to"). Faithful **to** the cheese, or its enemy? |
| Grandma: *Placetne vōbīs, līberī?* The kids: *Nōn placet!* What's going on? | A family **vote**, Church-council style. *Placet* is impersonal; *vōbīs* (dative) says whose agreement she wants. The kids vote **no**. |
| Grandma: *Spectāre licet; edere nōn licet.* | "**Looking is allowed; eating isn't.**" Each infinitive is the subject of *licet*, and no person is named: the rule is for everyone. |
| Marcus: *Avus nōbīs cūrae est.* What pattern? | The **double dative**: *cūrae* (serves as a worry) + *nōbīs* (to us). "**We're worried** about Grandpa." |
| Grandpa ends up eating the cheese, as usual. What's different this time? | **It was allowed:** *hodiē licuit*. Grandma forgave him (*Avō ignōscō*), and the dog, faithful to the end, obeyed Grandma, not his stomach: *Exemplō mihi es!* |

## Speak it aloud

:: Librō clausō, dīc mihi! || Book closed, tell me! (teaching line)

5. Say three things people ought to do with *oportet*: *Tē oportet dormīre · Avum oportet partem dare…*
