# Caput XLVIII: Recapitulātiō IV

*Liber IV, Verba Agentia · Chapter 48*

*In which nothing is new and every verb-adjective marches together. Book IV gave you the three participles (done, doing, about to do), the untied pair* urbe captā, *six infinitives, reported speech, the verbs in passive clothes, the hidden "to," the bare cases of place and time, the verbs with missing parts, and the "shall" command of Roman law. Today they all march together. Grandpa had no dinner last night, Grandma's laws still hang on the wall, and it's the sixteenth day. So he tells the oldest sixteen-day story in Rome: Cincinnatus, the farmer called from his field to save an army, who gave his power back on the sixteenth day. It's adapted from Livy, then lived (badly) by the family in the kitchen. Speaking goal: tell a piece of Roman history with participles, untied pairs, reports, and laws.*

## 1. *Laudātus*: the grown-up key

:: Rēs gestae dīvī Augustī. || The Deeds of the Divine Augustus. (the usual name of Augustus's own account of his deeds, AD 14; Chapter 37)

Lēgātī ā senātū missī vēnērunt.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Read the blurb. (*Lēgātus*: an envoy.) Law of the Grown-up Key: what is *missī*, and why that ending? | "The envoys, **sent** by the senate, came." *Missī* is the fourth key of *mittō* (*missum*) used as a plain *magnus*-family adjective: masculine, plural, nominative, like *lēgātī*. (Chapter 37.) |
| Law of the Done Deed: *Cāseus emptus in mēnsā est · erat · erit.* When was the cheese bought? | **Before** the main verb, every time: before it *is*, *was*, or *will be* on the table. The participle has no clock of its own; it borrows the main verb's and says "before that." |
| Law of the Many Englishes: *Cincinnātus, ā lēgātīs vocātus, togam induit.* (*Induō*: put on.) Give three Englishes. | "Cincinnatus, **called** by the envoys, put on his toga" · "Cincinnatus, **who had been called**…" · "**When he was called** by the envoys, Cincinnatus put on his toga." The Latin never changes; only your English does. |
| Chapter 37's motto: *Rēs gestae.* What is *gestae*, and who agrees with whom? | The fourth key of *gerō, gerere, gessī, gestum*, "carry on, do": "things **done**," deeds. *Gestae* is feminine plural to match *rēs*. In §12 Grandpa calls Livy's history the *rēs gestae* of the Roman people. |
| The old word: *dīcō* also means "name, appoint." Varro, *On the Latin Language* 5.82: *Dictātor, quod ā cōnsule dīcēbātur.* Why "dictator"? | "The dictator, because he **was named** by a consul." A Roman *dictātor* was "the named one": in an emergency a consul named one man to hold all the power, for six months at most. (Later the word was heard as "the one who dictates," and English "dictator" now means a tyrant. Rome's dictator was meant to give the power back.) |

## 2. *Laudāns*: doing it at the same time

:: Timeō Danaōs et dōna ferentīs. || I fear the Greeks, even bringing gifts. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 2.49; Chapter 38)

Cincinnātus, fossam fodiēns, lēgātōs vīdit.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of *-ns* and *-nt*: the present participle and its "of" form for *labōrō, sedeō, legō, veniō*, and for *fodiō*, "dig" (an *-iō* verb like *capiō*). | *Labōrāns, labōrantis · sedēns, sedentis · legēns, legentis · veniēns, venientis · fodiēns, fodientis.* The vowel is long before *-ns*, short before *-nt*. (Chapter 38.) |
| Law of One Shape: "The envoys found him **digging**." | ***Lēgātī eum fodientem invēnērunt.*** Accusative singular, to match *eum*. One shape for all three genders, declined like *ingēns*. |
| Law of the Same Moment: read the blurb. When was he digging? | **At the same time** as he saw them: "Cincinnatus, **digging** a ditch, saw the envoys," or "**while he was digging**…" Present participle = the same moment as the main verb, whatever its tense. |
| Ablative *-e* or *-ī*? *Patre cantante* · *ā virō sapientī*. | *-e* while it works as a participle (*cantante*: "while Dad sings," doing a verb's job); *-ī* once it's a plain adjective (*sapientī*, "wise"). (Chapter 38.) |
| Chapter 38's motto: *Timeō Danaōs et dōna ferentīs.* Which verb, which case, why *-īs*? | *Ferentīs* is the present participle of *ferō*, accusative plural, agreeing with *Danaōs*: "the Greeks **bringing** gifts." *-īs* is the old spelling of the accusative plural *-ēs* (Law of One Shape: *laudantēs, -īs*). |

## 3. *Laudātūrus*: about to

:: Avē, imperātor, moritūrī tē salūtant! || Hail, emperor! Those about to die salute you! (Suetonius, *Claudius* 21.6; Chapter 39)

Mandāta senātūs audītūrus es.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of *-ūrus*: from the fourth key, "about to fight, say, come, be." (*Pugnātum, dictum, ventum*; *sum* is a rule-breaker.) | ***Pugnātūrus, dictūrus, ventūrus, futūrus.*** Drop *-um*, add *-ūrus, -ūra, -ūrum*. The *ū* is always long and always stressed. (Chapter 39.) |
| Law of the Three Times: *missus · mittēns · missūrus*? | **Sent** (passive, before) · **sending** (active, same time) · **about to send** (active, after). Before, same, after: each borrows the main verb's clock. |
| Law of the Long Way Round: read the blurb. (*Mandātum*: an order.) | "**You are about to hear** the senate's orders." Future participle + *sum*: the active periphrastic. *Es* carries "you" and the present. |
| "The Aequi were about to attack the camp." (*Oppugnō*: attack; *Aequī*, a hill people east of Rome.) | ***Aequī castra oppugnātūrī erant.*** Masculine plural to match *Aequī*; *erant* makes it "were about to." |

## 4. *Urbe captā*: the untied pair

:: Cōnsule Plancō. || When Plancus was consul. (Horace, *Odes* 3.14.28; Chapter 40)

Signō datō, mīlitēs clāmōrem sustulērunt.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the Untied Pair: read the blurb. (*Clāmor*: a shout; *sustulērunt*: they raised, from *tollō*.) What's the pair, and when? | ***Signō datō***: noun + perfect participle, both ablative, standing loose: "**With the signal given**, the soldiers raised a shout," "**When the signal had been given**…" Perfect participle: **before** the main verb. (Chapter 40.) |
| "While the enemy were sleeping, the Romans came." Use the present participle. | ***Hostibus dormientibus, Rōmānī vēnērunt.*** Same time. Ablative plural *-ibus* (singular *-e*: *hoste dormiente*). |
| Law of the Unshared Noun: *Dictātor hostēs victōs sub iugum mīsit* · *Hostibus victīs, dictātor Rōmam rediit.* Why is only the second an ablative absolute? | In the first, *hostēs* is the object of *mīsit*, so the participle agrees with it (accusative): "sent the beaten enemy under the yoke." In the second, the enemy has no job in the main sentence: untied, so ablative. |
| Law of the Missing "Being": *Cincinnātō dictātōre · mē puerō · Avō invītō*? | "When Cincinnatus was dictator" · "when I was a boy" · "against Grandpa's will." *Sum* has no present participle, so Latin leaves "being" out. (Chapter 40.) |
| The Many Englishes, absolute edition: *Armīs relictīs, Aequī abiērunt.* Three ways. | "**With their weapons left behind**, the Aequi went away" · "**After leaving** their weapons…" · "The Aequi **left** their weapons **and** went away." (The "having ___ed" English works when the main subject did it.) |

## 5. *Sex īnfīnītīvī*: six ways to say "to"

:: Esse quam vidērī. || To be, rather than to seem. (North Carolina's state motto; the words are in Sallust and Cicero; Chapter 41)

Vocāre, vocārī, vocāvisse, vocātus esse, vocātūrus esse.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the Six: all six infinitives of *vocō* (*vocāvī, vocātum*). | Present: ***vocāre · vocārī*** · perfect: ***vocāvisse · vocātus esse*** · future: ***vocātūrus esse · vocātum īrī*** (rare). The one-word ones never change; the two-piece ones agree like *magnus*. (Chapter 41.) |
| And *sum*'s three? | ***Esse · fuisse · futūrus esse***, short form ***fore***. No passive. |
| Law of the Borrowed Clock: *Cincinnātus labōrāre · labōrāvisse · labōrātūrus esse vidēbātur.* | He seemed **to be working** (same time) · seemed **to have worked** (before) · seemed **about to work** (after). The clock is the main verb's. |
| Law of the Said and the Seen: "Cincinnatus is said to have been a farmer." | ***Cincinnātus agricola fuisse dīcitur.*** With *dīcitur* and *vidētur*, the **person** is the nominative subject, and *agricola* agrees with him in the nominative. |

## 6. *Dīcit sē venīre*: reported speech

:: Dīcit sē venīre. || He says that he is coming. (teaching line; Chapter 42's title)

Equitēs nūntiāvērunt exercitum obsidērī.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the Reported Word: *"Exercitus obsidētur,"* equitēs dīcunt. Report it. (*Obsideō*: besiege; *eques*: horseman.) | ***Equitēs dīcunt exercitum obsidērī.*** No word for "that"; the subject goes accusative (*exercitum*); the verb becomes an infinitive (present passive *obsidērī*). (Chapter 42.) |
| The Borrowed Clock, reported edition: read the blurb, then swap in *obsessum esse* (*obsideō, obsēdī, obsessum*). | *…exercitum obsidērī*: "announced that the army **was being** besieged" (same time). *…exercitum obsessum esse*: "that the army **had been** besieged" (before). The clock is the verb of saying's. |
| "The dictator said that he would come." | ***Dictātor dīxit sē ventūrum esse.*** After → future infinitive; *ventūrum* agrees with *sē* (accusative). |
| Law of the Speaker's *Sē*: *Cōnsul dīcit sē clausum esse* · *Cōnsul dīcit eum vēnisse.* | "The consul says that **he (the consul)** has been shut in" · "…that **he (someone else, say the dictator)** has come." *Sē* points to the one speaking; *eum* to somebody else. |
| *Negō*: "The dog denies that he ate the cheese." | ***Canis negat sē cāseum ēdisse.*** *Negō* = "say … not." |

## 7. *Loquor*: passive clothes, active meaning

:: Sequere mē. || Follow me. (Matthew 9:9; Chapter 43)

Mīlitēs dictātōrem secūtī sunt.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the Laid-Aside Voice: chant *sequor*, then read the blurb. | *Sequor, sequeris, sequitur, sequimur, sequiminī, sequuntur.* ***Secūtī sunt***: "they **followed**." Passive clothes, active meaning. *Deponent* comes from *dēpōnō*, "lay aside": these verbs laid aside their active endings. (Today a dictator lays something aside too: §12.) |
| Commands: "Follow, soldier!" "Speak, y'all!" | ***Sequere, mīles! Loquiminī!*** A deponent's command ends *-re* to one person and *-minī* to y'all. (Livy's own soldiers shout *Sequere, mīles!* on the night march, 3.27.8; you'll hear it in §12.) |
| Law of the Having-Done: *Mīlitēs, dictātōrem secūtī, vēnērunt* · *Hostēs, ā dictātōre victī, abiērunt.* Voice of each participle? | ***Secūtī***: active, "**having followed** the dictator" (a deponent). ***Victī***: passive, "**having been** beaten." Only deponents have an active "having done." (Chapter 43.) |
| Law of the Five Ablative Verbs: "The soldiers enjoy the booty; the dictator does his duty." (*Praeda*: booty.) | ***Mīlitēs praedā fruuntur; dictātor officiō fungitur.*** Ablative, not accusative: PUFFV (*potior, ūtor, fruor, fungor, vescor*). |
| Half-deponents: "The farmer dared; the senators rejoiced." (*Audeō, ausus sum · gaudeō, gāvīsus sum.*) | ***Agricola ausus est; senātōrēs gāvīsī sunt.*** Active in the present (*audet, gaudent*), passive-looking but active in the perfect. |

## 8. *Mihi placet*: the hidden "to"

:: Cui bonō? || Who stood to gain? (Cicero, *For Roscius of Ameria* 84; Chapter 44)

Dictātōrī omnēs pārent.

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the Hidden "To": read the blurb. Then "Spare us!" and "I believe you." | "**Everyone obeys the dictator**": *pāreō* + dative, "be obedient **to**." ***Parce nōbīs! Crēdō tibi.*** (Chapter 44.) |
| Compounds: "The dictator is in charge of the army. The soldiers come near the camp." (*Praesum, appropinquō.*) | ***Dictātor exercituī praeest. Mīlitēs castrīs appropinquant.*** The glued-on preposition brings the dative. |
| Law of the Empty "It": "The senate decided to name a dictator." "Y'all may go away." "You ought to obey." | ***Senātuī placuit dictātōrem dīcere. Licet vōbīs abīre. Tē oportet pārēre.*** *Placet* and *licet* take the person in the dative; *oportet* in the accusative. What you may or ought **to do** is an infinitive. |
| Law of the Two Datives: *Cincinnātus patriae auxiliō vēnit.* (*Patria*: country.) | Word by word, "came **for a help to** his country": "Cincinnatus **came to help** his country." One dative says what it serves as (*auxiliō*); the other, for whom (*patriae*). |
| *Cui bonō?* word by word, and Livy's *cui prope praedae fuistī* (§13). | "**To whom for a benefit?**" → "Who stood to gain?" · "**to whom** you almost were **for a booty**" → "whose prey you almost were." Two datives both times. |

## 9. *Domī mīlitiaeque*: place and time

:: Domī mīlitiaeque. || At home and in the field. (Roman saying; Chapter 45)

Rōmam, trāns Tiberim, mediā nocte, sex mēnsēs.

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of Home and Rome: to, at, from Rome; to, at, from home; to and in the country. | ***Rōmam, Rōmae, Rōmā · domum, domī, domō · rūs, rūrī.*** Towns, small islands, *domus* and *rūs*: no preposition. The middle column is the locative. (Chapter 45.) |
| Everything else takes a preposition: "into the forum," "across the Tiber," "on (Mount) Algidus." (*Tiberis*: the Tiber, Rome's river; its accusative is *Tiberim*, an old ending. *Algidus*: a mountain southeast of Rome.) | ***In forum · trāns Tiberim · in Algidō.*** Rooms, rivers, mountains and countries aren't towns, so they need *in, ad, ex, trāns*. |
| Law of the Point and the Stretch: "at midnight," "on the sixteenth day," "for six months," "within three days." | ***Mediā nocte · sextō decimō diē*** (point: ablative) · ***sex mēnsēs*** (stretch: accusative) · ***tribus diēbus*** (window: ablative). No preposition in any. |
| Space: "The soldiers marched twenty miles." | ***Mīlitēs vīgintī mīlia passuum ambulāvērunt.*** How far: accusative, like how long. |
| Law of Ago and Later: "three days later," "after three days," "three years ago." | ***Tribus diēbus post · post trēs diēs · abhinc trēs annōs.*** Alone, *post* is a how-word with a "by how much" ablative; with an accusative, it's a preposition. |

## 10. *Meminī, ōdī, coepī*: verbs with missing parts

:: Ōdī et amō. || I hate and I love. (Catullus 85; Chapter 46)

"Satin salvē?" inquit.

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the Perfect That Means Now: *meminī · memineram · meminerō*, and *ōdī, nōvī*? | "I remember · I remembered · I will remember"; "I hate"; "I know." Perfect forms, but every one slides one step toward now. (Chapter 46.) |
| "The soldiers began to dig." "The soldiers are beginning to dig." | ***Mīlitēs fodere coepērunt. Mīlitēs fodere incipiunt.*** *Coepī* for the perfect system, *incipiō* for the present system; both take an infinitive. |
| Law of the Slipped-In *Inquit*: read the blurb. (*Satin* = *satisne*: "Is it all right?") Where can *inquit* stand? | "'**Is everything all right?**' he said." Never first: it slips in after the first word or phrase (*"Satin," inquit, "salvē?"*), or follows a short quote. *Inquit* only quotes exact words. |
| *Aiō*: "They say that Cincinnatus was a farmer." | ***Aiunt Cincinnātum agricolam fuisse.*** *Ait* and *aiunt* may report with an accusative and infinitive. *Inquit* never does. |
| Law of the Genitive's Grip: "Remember me!" "The soldiers were mindful of the favor." "Grandpa is eager for cheese." (*Beneficium*: favor.) | ***Mementō meī! Mīlitēs beneficiī memorēs erant. Avus cāseī cupidus est.*** If English says "of" or "for" and there's no preposition, try the genitive. |

## 11. *Nōlī timēre*: commands for now and for later

:: Salūs populī suprēma lēx estō. || The welfare of the people shall be the highest law. (Cicero, *On the Laws* 3.8; Chapter 47)

Tabernae clausae suntō!

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the "Shall" Command: chant the "shall" command of *claudō*, "shut" (you, he, y'all, they), then *sum*'s. | ***Clauditō, clauditō, clauditōte, clauduntō · estō, estō, estōte, suntō.*** Third family *-itō*; "they" form + *-ō*. (Chapter 47.) |
| Law of Later and Forever: read the blurb, then "No one shall do private business." (*Prīvātus*: private.) Why the *-tō* form? | "**The shops shall be shut!**" · ***Nēmō rem prīvātam agitō!*** It's an **edict**, a law for everyone, so it takes the future imperative, like Cicero's *estō*. A plain *claude!* would be one person shutting one shop, now. |
| Law of the Five Don'ts: "Don't be afraid" (talk), "One shall not sing" (law), "Don't trust the horse, y'all" (poetry), "You shall not steal" (Bible). | ***Nōlī timēre · Nē cantātō · Equō nē crēdite · Nōn fūrtum faciēs.*** And the fifth, *Nē timeās*, a chunk for Liber V. |
| Cicero's law for the dictator, *On the Laws* 3.9: *Oenus nē amplius sex mēnsēs … idem iūris quod duo cōnsulēs tenētō.* (*Oenus*: old *ūnus*; *amplius*: more; *idem iūris*: the same power.) | "**One man**, for **not more than six months**, **shall hold** the same power as the two consuls." *Tenētō*: law-style "shall"; *sex mēnsēs*: a stretch, accusative. That's the office Cincinnatus gets in §12. |
| "Remember the cheese! Know that Grandpa is dictator!" | ***Mementō cāseī! Scītō Avum dictātōrem esse!*** *Mementō* with the genitive (Chapter 46), and *scītō* with an accusative and infinitive (Chapter 42). |

## 12. Lectiō: *Cincinnātus*

:: Operae pretium est audīre. || It's worth hearing. (Livy 3.26.7, opening the story of Cincinnatus)

Lūcius Quīnctius Cincinnātus trāns Tiberim agrum parvum colēbat.

Diē sextā decimā: the morning after Chapter 47, when Grandpa ate Grandma's cheese cake and went to bed without dinner. Grandpa tells the story; the kids interrupt. Scenes I and II are **adapted** from Livy, *From the Founding of the City* 3.26–29 (the year 458 BC in Livy's dating): the plot and a few phrases are Livy's, but the sentences are rebuilt in Book IV Latin (Livy's own words come in §13). Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. As you read, hunt participles, untied pairs, reports, deponents, and *-tō* laws. New words: *dictātor*, dictator; *lēgātus*, envoy; *ager*, field; *arātrum*, plow; *obsideō*, besiege; *iugum*, yoke; *hasta*, spear; *praeda*, booty; *triumphus*, triumph; *ēdictum*, edict; *dēpōnō*, lay down. Glossed: *pariēs, parietis*, wall; *pendeō*, hang; *Līvius*, Livy; *scrīptor*, writer; *Aequī*, the Aequi, a hill people east of Rome; *Algidus*, a mountain southeast of Rome; *castra*, camp (Chapter 45); *eques*, horseman; *ēmittō, ēmissum*, send out; *iūgerum* (plural *iūgera*), a Roman acre, about two-thirds of an English acre; *colō*, farm; *fodiō*, dig; *fossa*, ditch (Chapter 45); *mīrus*, amazing; *induō*, put on; *mandātum*, order; *admīrātus*, amazed (from *admīror*, a deponent); *casa*, hut; *pulvis, pulveris*, dust; *sūdor*, sweat; *abstergeō, abstersum*, wipe off; *prīvātus*, private; *cibus quīnque diērum*, five days' food; *vallus*, stake; *occāsus*, setting; *Campus Mārtius*, the Field of Mars, the army's drill ground; *senex, senis*, old man; *agmen*, column; *signifer*, standard-bearer; *accelerō*, hurry; *circumvectus*, having ridden around; *contemplor*, study, look over; *coniciō, coniectum*, throw together; *circumdō*, surround; *clāmor*, shout; *tollō, sustulī*, raise (Chapter 47); *ōrō*, beg; *sanguis*, blood; *fīgō, fīxum*, fix, plant; *trānsversus*, crosswise; *bōs, bovis*, ox; *currus*, chariot; *secō*, cut; *cauda*, tail; *dēmittō*, let down; *mīca*, crumb.

### I. *Ab agrō*

::: dialogue lectio-1
Iōannēs | Diē sextā decimā, māne, Avus ēsuriēns in culīnā sedet. Herī sine cēnā dormīvit. || John | On the sixteenth day, in the morning, Grandpa sits in the kitchen, hungry. Last night he went to bed without dinner.
Iōannēs | Lēgēs Aviae in pariete pendent. Māter cāseum novum, in forō emptum, in mēnsā pōnit. || John | Grandma's laws hang on the wall. Mom puts a new cheese, bought in the forum, on the table.
Avus | Ecce cāseus! Sed lēx prīma manet: cāseum nēmō tangitō. Ēheu! || Grandpa | Look, cheese! But law one still stands: no one shall touch the cheese. Alas!
Avia | Hic cāseus cēnae est, Ave. Exspectā! || Grandma | This cheese is for dinner, Grandpa. Wait!
Avus | Dum exspectāmus, fābulam vōbīs nārrābō, līberī: fābulam vēram, ā Līviō nārrātam. || Grandpa | While we wait, I'll tell y'all a story, kids: a true story, told by Livy.
Marcus | Quis est Līvius? || Marcus | Who is Livy?
Avus | Scrīptor Rōmānus, quī rēs gestās populī Rōmānī scrīpsit. Audīte! || Grandpa | A Roman writer, who wrote the deeds of the Roman people. Listen!

|---|---|
| Scene I: *in forō emptum · ad eum missī · fodientem · audītūrus es.* Name each participle and its time. | ***Emptum, missī***: perfect passive, **before** ("bought," "sent"). ***Fodientem***: present, **same time** ("digging"). ***Audītūrus es***: future, **after**, with *sum* (the long way round). Law of the Three Times. |
| Reports: *nūntiāvērunt cōnsulem exercitumque obsidērī · intellēxit auxilium adesse · dīxit sē sanguinem eōrum nōn cupere.* Translate. Who is *sē*, who are *eōrum*? | "Announced that the consul and the army **were being besieged**" · "understood that help **was there**" · "said that **he** didn't want **their** blood." ***Sē*** = the dictator, who is speaking; ***eōrum*** = the Aequi, somebody else. (Chapter 42.) |
| Untied pairs: *Pulvere et sūdōre abstersīs · Agmine factō · Sarcinīs … coniectīs · Signō datō · armīs relictīs.* Why ablative, and why is *abstersīs* plural? | None of these nouns has another job in its sentence (Law of the Unshared Noun). All perfect participles: done **before**. *Abstersīs* agrees with two nouns at once, dust **and** sweat. |
| The edict: *suntō · agitō · adsuntō · coquuntō*. Which mean "they shall," and why use *-tō* at all? | ***Suntō, adsuntō, coquuntō***: "they shall" (*-ntō*). ***Agitō***: "he shall" (*nēmō*, no one). An edict is a law for everyone, for later: Law of Later and Forever. |

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Dictātor imperium sex mēnsēs habēre poterat. Sed sextō decimō diē…* Which case is each time, and why? | ***Sex mēnsēs***: accusative, a **stretch** (how long). ***Sextō decimō diē***: ablative, a **point** (when). | Deponents: *contemplātus est · gāvīsus · sequentēs · officiō fūnctus*. Meanings? And why *officiō*? | "**Studied**" · "**overjoyed**" (half-deponent *gaudeō*) · "**following**" · "**having done** his duty." *Fungor* takes the ablative: one of the five ablative verbs. (Chapter 43.) |
| Grandpa's edicts: *Lēx prīma hodiē nē estō · Marcus cāseum secātō · līberī canem circumdantō · Clāmōrem tollite!* Which is the odd one out? | ***Tollite***: a plain "now" command, to the kids right in front of him. The rest are "shall" commands, laws. *Nē estō*, "shall not be," copies Cicero's *Reliquī magistrātūs nē suntō*, "the other offices shall not exist" (*On the Laws* 3.9, a few lines after §11's): under a dictator, the other offices stop. |

| *Iugum* and English: what does "to subjugate" mean, word by word? And John's last line: *Librō Quārtō lēctō*? | ***Sub iugum***: "to send **under the yoke**," to make someone admit defeat. · "**With Book Four read**," an ablative absolute. Careful: *lēctō* (long *ē*, "read") is not *lectō* (short *e*, Chapter 27's "bed"), where Grandpa is headed. |

## 13. Livy's own words

:: Spēs ūnica imperiī populī Rōmānī. || The one hope of the power of the Roman people. (Livy 3.26.7–8, of Cincinnatus)

Sub hōc iugō dictātor Aequōs mīsit.

The real story is in Livy's *Ab Urbe Conditā*, "From the Founding of the City," Book 3, chapters 26–29, written under Augustus more than four hundred years after the events, which were old legend even then. Livy writes long sentences full of subjunctives (Liber V), so §12 rebuilt them. But many of his lines are already yours.

| Livy 3.26.8: *L. Quīnctius trāns Tiberim … quattuor iūgerum colēbat agrum, quae prāta Quīnctia vocantur.* (*Prātum*: meadow. *Quattuor iūgerum*: "of four acres.") | "Lucius Quinctius **farmed** a field of four acres **across the Tiber**, which **are called** the Quinctian Meadows." A point in time and an ablative absolute. Grandpa's family has its own sixteenth day in §12. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Nārrandō discimus. || We learn by telling. (Chapter 26)

1. Say the three participles of *mittō, vocō, dūcō, audiō*: sent, sending, about to send.
2. Make five untied pairs from the story: *Signō datō · Armīs relictīs · Hostibus victīs · Cincinnātō dictātōre · Cāseō dīvīsō*, and say each in two Englishes.
3. Chant the six infinitives of *vocō*; then report three lines from the story with *dīcit* and three with *dīxit*.
4. Chant *sequor* and *loquor*; command one soldier and then y'all: *Sequere! Sequiminī!*
5. Five datives: *Dictātōrī pāret · Parce nōbīs · Licet vōbīs · exercituī praeest · patriae auxiliō*.
6. Where and when: *Rōmam, Rōmae, trāns Tiberim, in Algidō, mediā nocte, sextō decimō diē, sex mēnsēs*.
7. Give the dictator's edict in five *-tō* laws, two of them with *nē*; then "Don't be afraid" in talk, law, and poetry.
8. Tell the whole story of Cincinnatus in your own Latin in eight to ten lines (siege, envoys, toga, edict, night march, yoke, triumph, sixteenth day). Then read the *Lectiō* aloud, all three scenes, and hide the Latin and rebuild it.
