# Caput XXXIX: Laudātūrus

*Liber IV, Verba Agentia · Chapter 39*

*In which the verb looks ahead. Chapter 37's* laudātus *was done before the main verb. Chapter 38's* laudāns *is doing it at the same moment. Today's* laudātūrus *is **about to** do it: the third and last participle. "I'm about to eat the cheese." "Those about to die salute you." "From there he will come to judge the living and the dead." It's built on a key you've owned since Chapter 25, and it declines like Chapter 3's* magnus. *Stand it next to* sum *and you have a new way to talk about what's coming: the active periphrastic. Along the way: the Creed again, John the Baptist's "one who is to come," Caesar's Helvetians burning their grain, a sea battle on a lake, Judas, Horace's Dellius, and where "future" and "adventure" came from. Speaking goal: say what you're about to do, what you were about to do, and what you came to do.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Cāseum ēsūrus sum!* · *Quid factūrus es?* · *Cāseum ēsūrus eram, sed…* · *Avē, imperātor, moritūrī tē salūtant* · *Inde ventūrus est iūdicāre vīvōs et mortuōs* · *Tū es quī ventūrus es?*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** future active participle (the "about-to" verb-adjective; old-school "future participle") · active periphrastic (the "about-to" verb phrase: participle + *sum*; old-school "first periphrastic conjugation")
    - **Forms:** *laudātūrus, -a, -um* from the fourth key *laudātum* · *ēsūrus, ventūrus, itūrus, lātūrus, futūrus*; declined like *magnus* · *laudātūrus sum, laudātūrus eram*
    - **Laws:** the Law of *-ūrus* · the Law of the Three Times · the Law of the Long Way Round (plus the Many Englishes, future edition)
    - **Words:** *futūrus, ēsuriō, custōdiō, custōs, iūdicō, salūtō, vīvus, mortuus, saeculum, inde, trādō*
    - **Reading:** *Custōs Cāseī* (Grandpa volunteers to guard tomorrow's cheese overnight)
    - **Speaking:** say five things you're about to do, one plan that fell through, and why you came

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep three old friends close: Chapter 25's fourth key (*laudātum, ēsum*), Chapter 3's *magnus, -a, -um*, and Chapter 17's futures *laudābō, edam*.

## 1. About to praise

:: Laudātus, laudāns, laudātūrus. || Praised, praising, about to praise. (teaching line)

Avus laudātus rīdet. Avus laudāns rīdet. Avus laudātūrus rīdet.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapters 37 and 38: *Avus laudātus rīdet · Avus laudāns rīdet.* Who does the praising in each, and when? | *Laudātus*: **someone else** praised Grandpa, **before** he laughs. *Laudāns*: **Grandpa** praises, **while** he laughs. |
| The blurb's third line: *Avus laudātūrus rīdet.* Same verb *laudō*, new ending *-ūrus*. Guess. | "Grandpa laughs, **about to praise** (someone)." Grandpa will do the praising, but not yet: the praising comes **after** the laugh. |
| Grammar books call *laudātūrus* the **future active participle** (old-school just "future participle"): the "about-to" verb-adjective. Why "future," and why "active"? | "**Future**": the deed is still **ahead** of the main verb. "**Active**": the noun **does** it, just like *laudāns*. In meaning, *laudāns* and *laudātūrus* differ only in time. |
| *Cāseum ēsūrus sum!* (*Ēsūrus* comes from *edō*.) Guess. | "**I'm about to eat** the cheese!" or "I'm **going to** eat the cheese!" Grandpa's favorite sentence, from today on. |
| *Māter laudātūra rīdet.* Why *-a*? | It agrees with *Māter*: feminine, like *magna*. *Laudātūrus* has three shapes, *-us, -a, -um*, like Chapter 37's *laudātus*, not one shape like *laudāns*. |

## 2. Making it: the fourth key + *-ūrus*

:: Laudātum, laudātūrus. || The fourth key, and "about to praise." (teaching line)

Laudāt-um → laudāt-ūrus. Ēs-um → ēs-ūrus.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 25's four keys: *laudō, laudāre, laudāvī, laudātum.* Chapter 37 grew the fourth key into *laudātus*. Now take *laudātum*, drop *-um*, and add *-ūrus*. | ***laudātūrus.*** The same stem as *laudātus*: *laudāt-*. Only the ending differs: *-us* (done) vs *-ūrus* (about to do). |
| *Edō, edere, ēdī, ēsum.* "About to eat." | ***ēsūrus***: *ēs-* + *-ūrus*. A fourth key in *-sum* works the same as one in *-tum*. |
| *Veniō… ventum. Dīcō… dictum.* "About to come." "About to say." | ***ventūrus, dictūrus.*** |
| *Habeō… habitum · audiō… audītum · capiō… captum · faciō… factum.* | ***habitūrus, audītūrus, captūrus, factūrus.*** Chapter 38 sorted *-āns / -ēns / -iēns* by family. Today the family doesn't matter: the **fourth key** decides. |
| Look at the *ū*. Long or short? And where does the stress fall? | **Always long**: *-ūrus, -ūra, -ūrum*. A long next-to-last syllable takes the stress (Chapter 1): lau-dā-**TŪ**-rus, ē-**SŪ**-rus. |
| So what do you need in order to make it? | The **fourth key**. That's why Chapter 25 said "learn all four." Know *laudātum* and you own two participles: *laudātus* and *laudātūrus*. |

!!! success "The Law of *-ūrus*"
    Make the future active participle from the **fourth key**: drop *-um*, add ***-ūrus, -ūra, -ūrum***.

    | verb | fourth key | future participle |
    |---|---|---|
    | *laudō* | *laudātum* | *laudātūrus* |
    | *habeō* | *habitum* | *habitūrus* |
    | *legō* | *lēctum* | *lēctūrus* |
    | *audiō* | *audītum* | *audītūrus* |
    | *capiō* | *captum* | *captūrus* |
    | *edō* | *ēsum* | *ēsūrus* |

    The *ū* is **always long** and always stressed. (A handful of verbs build it a little differently; the dictionary prints those. You'll meet one in section 10.)

## 3. The rule-breakers fall in line

:: Quid sit futūrum crās, fuge quaerere. || Don't ask what tomorrow will bring. (Horace, *Odes* 1.9.13; Chapter 17)

Sum, esse, fuī, futūrus. Eō → itūrus. Ferō → lātūrus.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 25's dictionary listed *sum, esse, fuī, **futūrus***. A fourth key ending in *-ūrus*! What is *futūrus*? | The **future participle of *sum***: "**about to be, going to be**." *Sum* has no *-tum* key, so dictionaries print the participle in its place. (*Fu-*, as in *fuī*.) |
| English "future"? | From ***futūrus***, through French: "what is going to be." *Futūrum*, neuter, is "the future." |
| Chapter 17's motto: *Quid sit futūrum crās, fuge quaerere.* Chapter 17 said *futūrum* came "much later." Read it now. | *Futūrum* agrees with *quid*: "**what is going to be** tomorrow." Horace: "Don't ask what tomorrow will bring." (*Sit* is still waiting: Liber V.) |
| Chapter 32's rule-breakers: *eō, īre, iī, itum · ferō, ferre, tulī, lātum.* "About to go." "About to carry." | ***itūrus*** (short *i*) and ***lātūrus***. The present is wild, but the fourth key is a key: drop *-um*, add *-ūrus*. Compounds too: *abitūrus, exitūrus*. |
| *Possum, volō, nōlō*? | **None.** They have no fourth key (Chapter 25: *possum, posse, potuī*, full stop). For "I'm going to be able," use Chapter 18's plain future: ***poterō***. |

## 4. Declining *laudātūrus*

:: Bonus, bona, bonum. Ēsūrus, ēsūra, ēsūrum. || Good. About to eat. (teaching line)

Canis ēsūrus. Māter cantātūra. Animal ēsūrum.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Three genders, like *bonus*: "the dog about to eat," "Mom about to sing," "the animal about to eat." | ***canis ēsūrus · Māter cantātūra · animal ēsūrum.*** It follows the noun's gender, not its family: *canis* and *animal* are third family, but the participle stays *-us, -a, -um*. |
| Object: "I see the dog about to eat the cheese." | ***Canem cāseum ēsūrum videō.*** *Ēsūrum* agrees with *canem*. *Cāseum* is the object of *ēsūrum*, tucked in between, as in Chapter 38. |
| Genitive: "the voice of Dad about to sing." Compare Chapter 38's *vōx Patris cantantis*. | ***Vōx Patris cantātūrī.*** *-ī*, like *magnī*. (Uh-oh.) |
| Dative: "I give cheese to the girl about to leave." (*Abeō, abīre, abiī, abitum.*) | ***Puellae abitūrae cāseum dō.*** *-ae*, like *magnae*. |
| Plural: "the children about to play" and "I see the children about to play." (*Lūdō, lūdere, lūsī, lūsum*: play.) | ***Līberī lūsūrī. Līberōs lūsūrōs videō.*** *-ī* and *-ōs*, like *magnī, magnōs*. |

## 5. After the main verb

:: Pater cantātūrus venit, veniēbat, veniet. || Dad comes, was coming, will come, about to sing. (teaching line)

Pater cantātūrus venit. Pater cantātūrus veniēbat.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Pater cantātūrus venit.* When is the coming, and when is the singing? | The coming is **now**; the singing is **after**, any minute. "Dad comes in, **about to sing**." (Everyone reaches for their ears.) |
| Move the main verb into the past: *Pater cantātūrus veniēbat.* | "Dad was coming, **about to sing**." The singing was still **ahead** of that past moment. *Cantātūrus* didn't change: it borrows the main verb's clock, like the other two. |
| The three participles of *edō*. Give each, with its voice and its time. | ***ēsus***: eaten (passive, **before**). ***edēns***: eating (active, **same time**). ***ēsūrus***: about to eat (active, **after**). |
| So does Latin have a participle for "having eaten" (active, before)? | **No**, not for ordinary verbs (Chapter 38's trap). Nor one for "being eaten" (passive, same time). Two active, one passive, and that's the set. (Chapter 43's special verbs bend this.) |
| *Avus, cāseum ēsūrus, rīdet.* "Grandpa, having eaten the cheese, laughs"? | **No**: he hasn't eaten it yet. "Grandpa laughs, **about to eat** the cheese." *-ūrus* always looks ahead. |

!!! success "The Law of the Three Times"
    Latin's three participles split up time around the main verb. Each borrows the main verb's clock:

    | participle | voice | time | declined like |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | *laudātus* (Chapter 37) | passive: done **to** the noun | **before** the main verb | *magnus* |
    | *laudāns* (Chapter 38) | active: done **by** the noun | **same time** | *ingēns* |
    | *laudātūrus* (today) | active: done **by** the noun | **after** the main verb | *magnus* |

    *Ēsus, edēns, ēsūrus*: eaten, eating, about to eat.

## 6. *Laudātūrus sum*: the long way round

:: Cāseum ēsūrus sum! || I'm about to eat the cheese! (teaching line)

Ēsūrus sum, ēsūrus es, ēsūrus est. Ēsūrī sumus, ēsūrī estis, ēsūrī sunt.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 31 put *laudātus* next to *sum*: *laudātus sum*, "I have been praised." Put *laudātūrus* next to *sum*: *laudātūrus sum.* | "**I am about to praise**," "I'm **going to** praise," "I **mean to** praise." |
| Grammar books call this the **active periphrastic** (old-school "first periphrastic conjugation"). *Periphrasis* is Greek for "talking around": two words where one could do. Why "active"? | The subject **does** the deed, as with every *-ūrus*. (There is a passive one too, built differently. It waits for Liber V.) |
| Chant "about to eat," all six persons. | ***Ēsūrus sum, ēsūrus es, ēsūrus est, ēsūrī sumus, ēsūrī estis, ēsūrī sunt.*** *Sum* carries the person; the participle goes plural with plural subjects. |
| Mom: "I'm about to sing." The girls: "We're about to sing." | ***Cantātūra sum. Cantātūrae sumus.*** It agrees with the speaker, like Chapter 31's *amāta sum*. |
| "What are you going to do?" "What are y'all going to do?" | ***Quid factūrus es? Quid factūrī estis?*** The everyday "what's your plan?" (Ask a woman: *Quid factūra es?*) |
| Chapter 36's *cadō, cadere, cecidī, cāsum*. "The cheese is about to fall!" | ***Cāseus cāsūrus est!*** Falling is something the cheese **does** itself, so the active *-ūrus* fits. (*Cāsūrus*: *cāsum* + *-ūrus*.) |

## 7. Was about to

:: Frūmentum omne, praeter quod sēcum portātūrī erant, combūrunt. || They burn all the grain except what they were going to carry with them. (Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.5.3)

Cāseum ēsūrus eram… sed canis eum ēdit.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Swap *sum* for Chapter 16's *eram*: *Cāseum ēsūrus eram.* | "**I was about to** eat the cheese," "I **was going to** eat the cheese." |
| "I was going to eat the cheese, but the dog ate it." | ***Cāseum ēsūrus eram, sed canis eum ēdit.*** The classic use: a plan that something interrupted. |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.5.3: the Helvetians (Chapter 35) are leaving home for good. They burn their towns, and then: *frūmentum omne, praeter quod sēcum portātūrī erant, combūrunt.* (*Frūmentum*: grain; *praeter quod*: except what; *combūrō*: burn up; *sēcum*: Chapter 21.) Find the "was going to." | ***Portātūrī erant***: "**they were going to carry**," plural for the Helvetians. "They burn all the grain except what they were going to carry with them." Caesar adds why: so there'd be no hope of going home. |
| John 12:4: *Iūdās Scariōtis, quī erat eum trāditūrus.* (*Trādō, trādere, trādidī, trāditum*: hand over, betray.) What sits between *erat* and *trāditūrus*? | ***Eum***, the object. The two pieces can stand apart. "Judas Iscariot, **who was going to betray** him." (A *quī* clause, Chapter 28.) |
| Is there a "will be about to," with *erō*? | Yes, *laudātūrus erō*, but it's **rare**. To talk about the future plainly, Latin uses the plain future: *laudābō*. Just recognize *erō* when you see it. |

!!! success "The Law of the Long Way Round"
    The **active periphrastic** = future participle + a form of *sum*. *Sum* carries the person and the tense; the participle agrees with the subject:

    | Latin | English |
    |---|---|
    | *laudātūrus sum* | I am about to praise, I'm going to praise, I mean to praise |
    | *laudātūrus eram* | I was about to praise, I was going to praise |
    | *laudātūrus erō* | I will be about to praise (rare) |

    *Laudātūra sum* for a woman; *laudātūrī sumus* for "we."

## 8. *Edam* or *ēsūrus sum*?

:: Crās cāseum edam. Nunc cāseum ēsūrus sum! || Tomorrow I'll eat cheese. Right now I'm about to eat cheese! (teaching line)

Edam. Ēsūrus sum.

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 17 gave you *edam*, "I will eat." How is *ēsūrus sum* different? | *Edam* states a **plain future** fact or prediction. *Ēsūrus sum* says what's **coming up from where I stand now**: "I'm about to," "I'm set to," "I intend to." |
| Grandpa is at the table, knife in hand, cheese in front of him. Which one does he say? | ***Cāseum ēsūrus sum!*** He's on the point of it. (*Edam* sounds like a promise for later.) |
| "Tomorrow I'll read Caesar" vs "I'm going to read Caesar (that's my plan)." | ***Crās Caesarem legam*** (a plain future). ***Caesarem lēctūrus sum*** (a plan). English says "I'm going to" for both; Latin picks the shade. |
| *Quid faciēs?* vs *Quid factūrus es?* | *Quid faciēs?*: "What **will** you do?" *Quid factūrus es?*: "What are you **going to** do? What's your plan?" |
| *Laudātūrus sum* vs Chapter 31's *laudātus sum*. | ***Laudātus sum***: "I **have been praised**" (passive, done). ***Laudātūrus sum***: "I'm **about to praise**" (active, ahead). Two letters, *-ūr-*, flip both the voice and the time. |

## 9. Came to do it

:: Quī autem post mē ventūrus est, fortior mē est. || But the one who is coming after me is stronger than I. (Matthew 3:11)

Avus in culīnam vēnit cāseum ēsūrus.

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Avus in culīnam vēnit cāseum ēsūrus.* With a verb of coming or going, what's a good English? | "Grandpa came into the kitchen **to eat** the cheese" ("**intending to** eat it"). With motion verbs, *-ūrus* often tells **why** you came. |
| "Dad comes to sing." "The kids went out to play." | ***Pater cantātūrus venit. Līberī lūsūrī exiērunt.*** |
| Matthew 3:11, John the Baptist again (Chapter 38): *Quī autem post mē ventūrus est, fortior mē est.* (*Autem*: but, Chapter 33.) Find the periphrastic, and an old friend. | ***Ventūrus est***: "the one who **is coming** (who is to come) after me." ***Fortior mē***: Chapter 33's "than" ablative, "stronger **than I**." |
| Matthew 11:3: John, in prison, sends to ask Jesus: *Tū es quī ventūrus es, an alium exspectāmus?* (*An*: or; *alius*: another; *exspectō*: Chapter 18.) | "Are you the one **who is to come**, or are we waiting for another?" *Quī ventūrus es* had become almost a title: "the Coming One." |
| One participle, many Englishes again. *Avus, cāseum ēsūrus, in culīnam it.* Try four. | "Grandpa goes into the kitchen, **about to** eat the cheese" · "**going to** eat it" · "**intending to** eat it" · "**to** eat it." |

!!! success "The Many Englishes, future edition"
    One future participle can come into English many ways. Try them in order and keep the one that fits:

    1. **About to:** "about to eat."
    2. **Going to / set to:** "going to eat."
    3. **Intending to / meaning to:** "intending to eat."
    4. **To (purpose),** after verbs of coming and going: "came **to** eat."
    5. **Who will / who is to:** *quī ventūrus est*, "the one who is to come."

    The time is always **after** the main verb: "about to" with a present, "was about to / was going to" with a past.

## 10. The ones who will…

:: Avē, imperātor, moritūrī tē salūtant! || Hail, emperor! Those about to die salute you! (Suetonius, *Claudius* 21.6)

Ventūrī. Moritūrī. Futūrum.

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapters 37–38: a participle with no noun becomes a person (*victī, amantēs*). *Ventūrī* alone? | "**Those who will come**," "the people to come." *Futūrum* alone: "**the future**," "what is going to be." |
| The motto. *Avē* (Chapter 1), *imperātor* (Chapter 6), *salūtō*: greet. *Moritūrī* is from *morior*, "die," one of Chapter 43's special verbs; its future participle is *moritūrus* (one of the handful the dictionary prints). Who are *moritūrī*? | "**Those about to die**." A participle as people, the **subject** of *salūtant*: "Hail, emperor! Those about to die salute you!" (Many editions of Suetonius print *Have*: the same greeting with an *h*.) |
| Who said it? Suetonius tells it of the emperor Claudius in AD 52: before draining the Fucine Lake, he staged a sea battle on it, and the fighters shouted this. Claudius answered ***Aut nōn!***, "Or not!" What was he leaving out? | ***moritūrī***: "Or not (about to die)." The men took it as a pardon and refused to fight. Claudius, partly threatening and partly urging, drove them into battle anyway. (This is the only Latin source for the line, and it wasn't gladiators' everyday salute: just these men, that once.) |
| The Nicene Creed, sung at Mass, ends: *Et exspectō… vītam ventūrī saeculī.* (*Saeculum*: age, world.) | "And I look for… the life **of the world to come**." *Ventūrī* agrees with *saeculī*, genitive neuter: "the world **about to come**." |
| *Ēsuriō, ēsurīre*: "be hungry." Look at the front. Which fourth key? | *Edō*'s ***ēsum***: *ēs-* + a "want to" ending *-uriō*: "want to eat," so "**be hungry**." (Here the *u* is short: ay-SU-ri-oh.) And English "adventure" comes from *adventūrus*, "about to arrive," of *adveniō*: things about to happen to you. |

## 11. Real lines

:: Inde ventūrus est iūdicāre vīvōs et mortuōs. || From there he will come to judge the living and the dead. (the Apostles' Creed)

Ēmissūrus. Moriture.

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 38 opened the Apostles' Creed. Further on: *sedet ad dexteram Deī Patris omnipotentis, inde ventūrus est iūdicāre vīvōs et mortuōs.* (*Dextera*: right hand; *inde*: from there; *iūdicō*: judge; *vīvus*: living; *mortuus*: dead.) | "He sits at the right hand of God the Father almighty; from there he **will come** (**is to come**) to judge the living and the dead." *Ventūrus est* is the periphrastic. *Vīvōs* and *mortuōs* are adjectives used as people (Chapter 37). |
| Why is *iūdicāre* an infinitive? | It tells **why** he will come: "**to** judge." Church Latin often uses the infinitive this way after verbs of coming; classical prose usually doesn't. Read it as English "to." |
| The Nicene Creed says it again: *Et iterum ventūrus est cum glōriā iūdicāre vīvōs et mortuōs.* (*Iterum*: again.) | "And he **will come again** in glory to judge the living and the dead." *Cum glōriā*: Chapter 35's manner, "with glory." |
| Back to Suetonius, the sentence before the shout: *Quīn et ēmissūrus Fūcinum lacum naumachiam ante commīsit.* (*Quīn et*: what's more; *ēmittō, ēmittere, ēmīsī, ēmissum*: let out, drain; *lacus*: lake; *naumachia*: sea battle; *ante*: first, beforehand; *committō … commīsī*: stage.) | "What's more, **when he was about to drain** the Fucine Lake, he first staged a sea battle." *Ēmissūrus* agrees with Claudius, the subject: about to do something, after the main verb *commīsit*. |
| Horace, *Odes* 2.3.1–4, to his friend Dellius: *Aequam mementō rēbus in arduīs / servāre mentem… moriture Dellī.* (*Aequus*: level, calm; *mementō*: remember, a chunk whose form waits for Chapter 47; *arduus*: steep, hard; *mēns*: mind.) Which case is *moriture*? | **Vocative**: *-e*, like Chapter 9's *Quīnte*. Horace calls his friend "**Dellius, who will die**": "Remember to keep a calm mind when things are hard… Dellius, you who are going to die." Enjoy life; we're all *moritūrī*. |

## 12. Traps

:: Nōn "ēsus," sed "ēsūrus." || Not "eaten," but "about to eat." (teaching line)

Cāseus ēsus est. Avus ēsūrus est.

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Cāseus ēsus est* vs *Avus ēsūrus est.* | *Cāseus ēsus est*: "The cheese **has been eaten**" (passive, done). *Avus ēsūrus est*: "Grandpa **is about to eat**" (active, ahead). |
| *Cāseus ēsūrus est.* "The cheese is about to be eaten"? | **No!** *-ūrus* is **active**: "The cheese **is about to eat**." (A hungry cheese.) "About to be eaten" needs a different construction, later. |
| Spelling: *laudāturus* or *laudātūrus*? *venītūrus* or *ventūrus*? *ēssūrus* or *ēsūrus*? | ***laudātūrus*** (long *ū* always), ***ventūrus*** (from the fourth key *ventum*, not from *venīre*), ***ēsūrus*** (one *s*, from *ēsum*). |
| "Grandma is about to sing": *Avia cantātūrus est.* | ***Avia cantātūra est.*** The participle agrees with *Avia*: feminine *-a*. |
| "I'm going to be able to come": *Potentūrus sum venīre*? | No such word: *possum* has no fourth key. ***Venīre poterō***: Chapter 18's plain future. |

## 13. Fix it

:: Errāta corrēctūrī sumus. || We're about to fix mistakes. (teaching line; *corrigō … corrēctum*)

Cantātūra an cantātūrus? Ventūrus an venītūrus?

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Mom is about to sing": *Māter cantātūrus est.* | ***Māter cantātūra est.*** Feminine, to agree with *Māter*. |
| "We're going to eat": *Ēsūrus sumus.* | ***Ēsūrī sumus.*** Plural, to agree with "we." |
| "The one who is to come": *quī venītūrus est.* | ***quī ventūrus est.*** Fourth key *ventum* + *-ūrus*. |
| "I see the girls about to play": *Puellās lūsūrī videō.* | ***Puellās lūsūrās videō.*** Accusative feminine plural, to agree with *puellās*. |
| "I was going to read the book": *Librum legēns eram.* | ***Librum lēctūrus eram.*** *Legēns* is "reading" (same time) and doesn't build tenses (Chapter 38). "Was going to" = future participle + *eram*. |

## 14. Lectiō: *Custōs Cāseī*

:: Cāseum ēsūrī tē salūtant! || Those about to eat cheese salute you! (after Suetonius, *Claudius* 21.6)

Custōs cāseī. Avus custōdītūrus.

Diē septimā: the day after Chapter 38. Grandma has bought a new cheese for tomorrow's dinner. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. As you read, hunt the *-ūrus* forms and ask: who is about to do what? New in the story: *custōs, custōdis*, guard (from *custōdiō*, guard); *lupus*, wolf (Chapter 13); *vigilō*, stay awake; *pugnō*, fight (Chapter 34); *quaerō … quaesītum*, look for (Chapter 17); *culter*, knife (Chapter 37); *hesternus*, yesterday's (Chapter 23); *tantum*, only (Chapter 16); *intus*, inside; *tūtus*, safe.

{#g-14}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Grandma: *Crās eum in cēnā ēsūrī sumus.* Why *ēsūrī*, and why not *edēmus*? | ***Ēsūrī***: plural, agreeing with "we" (the whole family). The periphrastic makes it **the plan**: "we're going to eat it tomorrow." *Edēmus* would be a plain "we will eat." |
| John: *Lupus ovēs custōdītūrus est!* What's the joke? | "The **wolf** is going to guard the **sheep**!" Grandpa guarding cheese is like a wolf on sheep duty. (*Ovēs*: Chapter 15.) |
| Marcus: *Avē, Ave! Cāseum ēsūrī tē salūtant!* Which real line, and what changed? | Suetonius's ***Avē, imperātor, moritūrī tē salūtant***: *imperātor* → *Ave* (Chapter 9's *Avē, ave!*), *moritūrī* → *cāseum ēsūrī*. Grandpa answers like Claudius: ***Aut nōn!*** "Or not (about to eat it)!" |
| Grandma: *Īte in hortum lūsūrī!* Compare Chapter 38's *Īte in hortum lūdentēs!* | ***Lūsūrī***: "go to the garden **to play**" (purpose, after a verb of going). ***Lūdentēs***: "go **playing**" (same time). |
| Grandpa: *Cāseum… custōdītūrus eram.* What does *eram* add? | "I **was about to** guard the cheese": a plan from a moment ago, conveniently interrupted. Grandpa's excuse. |
| End: *Cāseus quem crās ēsūrī erāmus… in stomachō custōdis.* Whose stomach, and what's *custōdis*? | *Ēsūrī erāmus*: "we **were going to** eat it": a plan that will now never happen. ***Custōdis***: genitive of ***custōs***, "guard," from *custōdiō*: "in the stomach of **its guard**," Grandpa. |

## Vocābula: Words of this chapter

:: Verba lēctūrī sumus. || We're about to read the words. (teaching line)

New forms:

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| *laudātūrus, laudātūra, laudātūrum* | about to praise, going to praise (from the fourth key *laudātum*; declined like *magnus*) |
| *-ūrus, -ūra, -ūrum* | future active participle ending, added to the fourth key minus *-um* (*ēsum → ēsūrus*, *ventum → ventūrus*) |
| *laudātūrus sum* | I am about to praise, I'm going to praise (*laudātūrus eram*: I was going to praise) |

New words:

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| *futūrus, futūra, futūrum* | about to be, future (from *sum*); *futūrum*: the future |
| *ēsuriō, ēsurīre* | be hungry (short *u*; from *ēsum*, "want to eat") |
| *custōdiō, custōdīre, custōdīvī, custōdītum* | guard, keep watch over |
| *custōs, custōdis* | m./f., guard, keeper |
| *iūdicō, iūdicāre, iūdicāvī, iūdicātum* | judge |
| *salūtō, salūtāre, salūtāvī, salūtātum* | greet, salute |
| *vīvus, vīva, vīvum* | living, alive |
| *mortuus, mortua, mortuum* | dead |
| *saeculum, saeculī* | n., age, generation, world |
| *inde* | from there, from then |
| *trādō, trādere, trādidī, trāditum* | hand over, betray |

## Speak it aloud

:: Dictūrus, dīc! || About to speak? Speak! (teaching line)

1. Make the future participle of ten verbs from their fourth keys: *laudō, cantō, habeō, videō, legō, dīcō, audiō, capiō, edō, veniō*. Then *sum, eō, ferō*.
2. Chant *ēsūrus sum… ēsūrī sunt* and *cantātūra sum… cantātūrae sumus*, then *ēsūrus eram… ēsūrī erant*.
3. The three Laws in your own words: *-ūrus*, the Three Times, the Long Way Round. Then give the three participles of *edō* and *laudō*.
4. Say five things you're about to do today (*Cāseum ēsūrus sum. Librum lēctūrus sum…*), and ask someone *Quid factūrus es?*
5. Tell one plan that fell through: *… ēsūrus eram, sed …*
6. Say why you came, three ways: *In culīnam vēnī cāseum ēsūrus…*
7. The real lines: *Inde ventūrus est iūdicāre vīvōs et mortuōs · Avē, imperātor, moritūrī tē salūtant · Tū es quī ventūrus es? · vītam ventūrī saeculī · portātūrī erant.*
8. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part. Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
