# Caput XXXVII: Laudātus

*Liber IV, Verba Agentia · Chapter 37*

*In which the fourth key leaves home. Chapter 31 turned Chapter 25's fourth key into an adjective, but kept it on a leash: it only ever stood next to* sum *(*Cāseus ēsus est*). Chapter 36 promised "the fourth-key adjective all grown up." Here it is. Today* emptus, ēsus, laudātus *go anywhere a noun goes, in any case: "the cheese bought by Dad," "to John, praised by everyone," "a baby wrapped in cloths." They tell you what was done to something before the main verb happened, and one word can do the work of a whole English clause. Book IV is the book of* verba agentia, *"working verbs": verbs that work as adjectives and nouns. This is its first. Along the way: the first lines of the* Aeneid *again, Catullus at his brother's grave, the Christmas manger, what a Gaul said with his sword on the scales, and why Augustus called his life story* Rēs Gestae. *Speaking goal: describe things by what was done to them, and say in one word what English says in five.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Cāseus ā Patre emptus* · *pennae sparsae* · *Iōannī, ab omnibus laudātō* · *Canis, culpātus, nihilō minus edit.* · *Dictum, factum!* · *Vae victīs!*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** participle (a verb-adjective, from Chapter 31) · perfect passive participle (the fourth-key adjective; English "past participle") · participle used as a noun (old-school "substantive") · circumstantial participle (a participle standing for a whole clause)
    - **Forms:** none new! *laudātus, -a, -um* declined like *magnus* in every case
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Grown-up Key · the Law of the Done Deed · the Law of the Many Englishes
    - **Words:** *colligō, iungō, spargō, secō, involvō, impleō* with their four keys · *doctus, dictum, factum, rēs gestae, victī, pannus, pulvīnus*
    - **Reading:** *Laudātus!* (Grandma's empty pillow, a scolded dog, a present wrapped in cloth, and Grandpa wants to be praised too)
    - **Speaking:** describe five things by what was done to them, with *ā/ab*

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep three old friends close: Chapter 3's agreement (*magnus, magna, magnum*), Chapter 28's *quī, quae, quod*, and Chapter 31's fourth key (*amātus, ēsus, factus*).

## 1. The fourth key grows up

:: Cāseus ēsus! || Cheese eaten! (teaching line; Chapter 31's headline)

Cāseus ēsus est. Cāseus ēsus… in mēnsā nōn est!

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 31: *Cāseus ēsus est.* What are the two pieces, and what does it mean? | ***Ēsus*** (the fourth key of *edō*, an adjective agreeing with *cāseus*) + ***est*** (the time): "**The cheese has been eaten.**" |
| Chapter 31 also showed a headline with no *est*: *Cāseus ēsus!* What was that? | "**Cheese eaten!**" The *est* is just left out; you supply it. Chapter 31 promised: "Participles on their own, without *sum*, get their own chapter: Chapter 37." This is it. |
| Chapter 31's word **participle** means "a verb-adjective." Read the blurb's second sentence: *Cāseus ēsus… in mēnsā nōn est.* Which word is the verb now? | ***Est*** ("is"): "The eaten cheese… is not on the table." *Ēsus* is no longer half of the verb. It just **describes** the cheese, like *magnus*. |
| Grammar books call *ēsus, amātus, laudātus* the **perfect passive participle** (PPP for short). In English grammar, "eaten," "praised," "bought" are called the **past participle**. Why "perfect" and why "passive"? | "**Perfect**": the deed is finished, done. "**Passive**": the noun **received** the deed (Chapter 30's voice). *Cāseus ēsus*: the cheese didn't eat; it got eaten. |
| The title: *laudō, laudāre, laudāvī, laudātum* (Chapter 30's "praise"). What is *laudātus*? | "**Praised**," "having been praised." *Puer laudātus*, "the praised boy." |
| So how many ways can a fourth-key adjective work, so far? | Two. **With *sum*** it makes Chapter 31's finished passive (*laudātus est*, "he has been praised"). **Without *sum*** it's a describing word (*puer laudātus*, "the praised boy"). Today is all about the second. |

## 2. Any noun, any case

:: Hic est Fīlius meus dīlēctus. || This is my beloved Son. (Matthew 3:17; Chapter 19)

Pennae sparsae. Pennās sparsās videō. In capsā apertā nihil est.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| In Chapter 31 the fourth key always agreed with the **subject**, so it was always nominative. Now it's free. *Spargō, spargere, sparsī, sparsum*: scatter. "The scattered feathers" (*pennae*, f. pl.). | ***Pennae sparsae.*** Feminine plural nominative, like *magnae*. |
| "I see the scattered feathers." Now the feathers are the **object**. | ***Pennās sparsās videō.*** Accusative plural: *-ās* on both. The participle follows its noun into any case, like *magnās*. |
| *Aperiō, aperīre, aperuī, apertum* (Chapter 31). "There is nothing in the opened box." (*In* + where: Chapter 7.) | ***In capsā apertā nihil est.*** Ablative: *capsā apertā*. ("In the open box": *apertus* often just means "open.") |
| Genitive: "the smell of the cheese bought by Dad." (*Odor, odōris*: smell, Chapter 35; *emō … emptum*.) | ***Odor cāseī ā Patre emptī.*** *Emptī* agrees with *cāseī*: masculine genitive singular. |
| Dative: "We give a gift to John, praised by everyone." | ***Iōannī, ab omnibus laudātō, dōnum damus.*** *Laudātō* agrees with *Iōannī* (dative, Chapter 13). Same endings as *bonō*. |
| The motto, Chapter 19's Matthew 3:17. *Dīligō … dīlēctum*: love, cherish. Which case is *dīlēctus*, and why? | **Nominative** masculine singular, agreeing with *Fīlius* (*est* links them). "My **beloved** Son," word for word "my loved Son." Chapter 31 called it "a fourth-key adjective used plainly as an adjective." It was a participle all along. |

!!! success "The Law of the Grown-up Key"
    Without *sum*, the fourth key is a plain **adjective** of the *magnus, -a, -um* family. It agrees with its noun in **gender, number, and case**, wherever that noun stands in the sentence:

    | | masculine | feminine | neuter |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | subject | *cāseus emptus* | *capsa aperta* | *dōnum emptum* |
    | object | *cāseum emptum* | *capsam apertam* | *dōnum emptum* |
    | of | *cāseī emptī* | *capsae apertae* | *dōnī emptī* |
    | to / for | *cāseō emptō* | *capsae apertae* | *dōnō emptō* |
    | by / with / in | *cāseō emptō* | *capsā apertā* | *dōnō emptō* |

    Plural: *-ī, -ae, -a · -ōs, -ās, -a · -ōrum, -ārum, -ōrum · -īs* (all genders). Nothing new to learn: it's Chapter 3's *magnus*, Chapter 10's genitive, Chapter 11's dative.

## 3. Two pieces or one?

:: Gallia est omnis dīvīsa in partēs trēs. || Gaul, as a whole, is divided into three parts. (Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.1)

Capsa aperta est. Capsa aperta in mēnsā est.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Capsa aperta est.* · *Capsa aperta in mēnsā est.* Which one is Chapter 31's finished passive? | The first: ***aperta est***, "the box **has been opened**" (or "is open"). In the second, *est* already has a job (*in mēnsā est*, "is on the table"), so *aperta* is just a describer: "**The opened box is on the table.**" |
| So the test is: find the **main verb** first. *Pater cāseum emptum portat.* | Main verb ***portat***. *Emptum* agrees with *cāseum* (object): "Dad is carrying the **bought** cheese," "the cheese he bought." |
| *Avus laudātus rīdet.* | Main verb ***rīdet***: "**The praised Grandpa laughs**," better English: "Grandpa, **praised**, laughs," "Grandpa laughs **at being praised**." |
| Chapter 31's Caesar again: *Gallia est omnis dīvīsa in partēs trēs.* Chapter 31 said "you can also read *dīvīsa* as a plain adjective." What are the two readings? | Two pieces: *est … dīvīsa*, "Gaul **has been divided**" (so "is divided"). One piece: *est* = "is" and *dīvīsa* describes: "Gaul, as a whole, **is a divided (land)**." The meaning comes out the same: the dividing is done and the result stands. |
| *Iacta ālea est* (Chapter 31). Now make it a headline, and then a describer: "Caesar sees the thrown die." | Headline: ***Ālea iacta!*** "Die cast!" Describer: ***Caesar āleam iactam videt.*** *Iactam* agrees with *āleam* (accusative). |

## 4. Done before

:: Factum est illud: fierī īnfectum nōn potest. || That's been done: it can't be made undone. (Plautus, *Aulularia* 741)

Cāseus ā Patre emptus in mēnsā est. Erat. Erit.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Cāseus ā Patre emptus in mēnsā est.* When did the buying happen, and when is the cheese on the table? | The buying happened **first** (yesterday, at the market). The cheese is on the table **now**. The participle reports what had **already** been done to the cheese. |
| Move the main verb into the past: *Cāseus ā Patre emptus in mēnsā erat.* Still "bought" first? | Yes. "The cheese **bought** by Dad **was** on the table": the buying came before the "was." |
| And the future: *Crās cāseus ā Patre emptus in mēnsā erit.* | "Tomorrow the cheese bought by Dad will be on the table": by then it will **have been** bought. Still earlier than the main verb. |
| So the fourth key doesn't tell time on its own clock. Whose clock does it use? | The **main verb's**. The participle says "**done before** that." English often makes you choose ("bought," "which had been bought," "which will have been bought"); Latin uses one word. |
| *Pennae ā Quīntō collēctae in pulvīnō nōn sunt.* (*Colligō, colligere, collēgī, collēctum*: gather; Chapter 36 glossed it.) In what order did things happen? | First Quīntus **gathered** the feathers (out of the pillow, Chapter 36). Now they **are not** in the pillow. "The feathers gathered by Quīntus aren't in the pillow." |
| The motto, from Plautus's comedy *The Pot of Gold* (*Aulularia* 741): a young man owns up to a wrong already done. *Factum est illud: fierī īnfectum nōn potest.* (*Īnfectum*: "un-done," *in-* "not" + *factum*. *Fierī*: "to become, be made," Chapter 32.) Find the two-piece verb and the one-piece participle. | ***Factum est***: two pieces, "that **has been done**." ***Īnfectum***: one piece, a describer with *fierī*: "it can't become **undone**." A deed already done stays done: that is the participle's whole sense of time. |

!!! success "The Law of the Done Deed"
    The perfect passive participle shows a deed **done to its noun before** the main verb, whatever the main verb's tense:

    | main verb | Latin | English |
    |---|---|---|
    | present | *Cāseus emptus in mēnsā **est**.* | The cheese (that was) bought **is** on the table. |
    | past | *Cāseus emptus in mēnsā **erat**.* | The cheese (that had been) bought **was** on the table. |
    | future | *Cāseus emptus in mēnsā **erit**.* | The cheese (that will have been) bought **will be** on the table. |

    The participle has no clock of its own: it borrows the main verb's and says "**before** that." (Almost always. A few verbs, used loosely, slide toward "at the same time," but "before" is the rule to read by.)

## 5. By whom? With what?

:: Ā Patre emptus, ab Avō ēsus. || Bought by Dad, eaten by Grandpa. (teaching line)

Cāseus ab Iōanne sectus. Cāseus cultrō sectus.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 30's Law of the Doer and the Tool still works on a participle. *Secō, secāre, secuī, sectum*: cut. "The cheese, cut by John." | ***Cāseus ab Iōanne sectus.*** The doer: *ā/ab* + ablative. |
| "The cheese, cut with a knife." (*Culter, cultrī*, m.: knife, like *liber*.) | ***Cāseus cultrō sectus.*** The tool: plain ablative, no preposition. |
| The blurb has both: *Ā Patre emptus, ab Avō ēsus.* Two participles, two doers. Who did what? | Dad **bought** it; Grandpa **ate** it. "Bought by Dad, eaten by Grandpa." The life story of every cheese in this book. |
| Chapter 30's motto, Horace: *Laudātur ab hīs, culpātur ab illīs.* Turn it into describers: "a man praised by these, blamed by those" (*vir*). | ***Vir laudātus ab hīs, culpātus ab illīs.*** Every famous person ever. |
| *Iōannēs cāseum ab omnibus laudātum edit.* Who praised, who eats, what's praised? | **Everyone** praised the cheese (*ab omnibus*); **John** eats it (subject); *laudātum* agrees with *cāseum* (object). "John eats the cheese praised by everyone." |

## 6. From *quī* to the participle

:: Cāseus quem Pater ēmit · cāseus ā Patre emptus. || The cheese which Dad bought · the cheese bought by Dad. (teaching line; Chapter 28)

Cāseus, quī ā Patre emptus est… Cāseus ā Patre emptus.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 28's title chapter had *Cāseus quem Pater ēmit.* Say it with a participle and no *quem*. | ***Cāseus ā Patre emptus.*** Dad goes into *ā Patre*; *ēmit* becomes *emptus*, agreeing with *cāseus*. Same meaning, shorter. |
| The blurb's middle step: *cāseus quī ā Patre emptus est*, "the cheese which was bought by Dad." What do you cross out to get the participle? | Cross out ***quī*** and ***est***. What's left is *cāseus ā Patre emptus*. A participle is a relative clause with the scaffolding taken down. |
| Shrink it: *Pennae quās Quīntus collēgit ubīque sunt.* | ***Pennae ā Quīntō collēctae ubīque sunt.*** "The feathers (which were) gathered by Quīntus are everywhere." |
| Now grow one back: *Iōannēs partem ā sē sectam Avō dat.* (*Ā sē*: by himself, Chapter 21's *sē*.) | ***Iōannēs partem quam sēcuit Avō dat.*** "John gives Grandpa the piece he cut." (*Sēcuit* is *secō*'s key 3.) |
| Why would a Roman pick the participle over the *quī* clause? | It's **shorter** and it **flows**: Latin writers stack participles where English stacks "which… who… that." Recognizing them is half of reading Caesar and Vergil. |

## 7. When, because, although

:: Rēgnī cupiditāte inductus coniūrātiōnem fēcit. || Led on by desire for the kingship, he made a conspiracy. (Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.2; Chapter 35)

Quīntus, ab Avō captus, rīsit.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Quīntus, ab Avō captus, rīsit.* Word for word, then good English. | Word for word: "Quīntus, **caught** by Grandpa, laughed." Good English: "**When he was caught** by Grandpa, Quīntus laughed." |
| *Avus, ab Aviā culpātus, trīstis erat.* Try "because." | "**Because he had been blamed** by Grandma, Grandpa was sad." (Or just "Grandpa, blamed by Grandma, was sad.") |
| *Canis, ab Aviā culpātus, nihilō minus cāseum edit.* (Chapter 35's *nihilō minus*.) What does *nihilō minus* tell you about the participle? | "**Although he's been scolded** by Grandma, the dog eats the cheese all the same." *Nihilō minus* (or *tamen*, "still," a word you'll meet soon) signals "although." |
| Some grammar books call this a **circumstantial participle**: it tells the circumstances (when, why, in spite of what) of the main verb. Where does Latin say "when," "because," or "although"? | **Nowhere.** The participle just says "caught," "blamed." **You** pick the English word that fits the story. That's a reader's job, not a grammar rule. |
| Chapter 35's Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.2, about Orgetorix: *rēgnī cupiditāte inductus coniūrātiōnem nōbilitātis fēcit.* (*Indūcō … inductum*: lead on; *coniūrātiō*: conspiracy; *nōbilitās*: the nobles. Caesar's sentence opens with the year, which we skip.) Chapter 35 glossed *inductus*. Now read it fully. | "**Led on** by desire for the kingship, he made a conspiracy of the nobles," so "**Because he was** led on…" *Inductus* agrees with the subject (he, Orgetorix) and gives the **reason** for *fēcit*. |
| One more English for the same Latin: *Pater cāseum emptum domum portāvit.* | "Dad carried the bought cheese home," so "Dad **bought** the cheese **and** carried it home." Latin often packs the first of two deeds into a participle; English uses "and." |

!!! success "The Law of the Many Englishes"
    One perfect passive participle can come into English many ways. Try them in this order, and keep the one that fits the story:

    1. **Plain:** "bought," "caught," "praised" (*cāseus emptus*, "the bought cheese").
    2. **Who / which:** "which was bought," "who had been caught" (Chapter 28's relative).
    3. **When / after:** "when he was caught," "after it was bought."
    4. **Because:** "because he had been blamed."
    5. **Although:** "though he'd been scolded" (look for *nihilō minus* or *tamen*).
    6. **And:** "he bought it and carried it" (when the participle sits on the object).

    The Latin never changes. Only your English does.

## 8. Things done, things said

:: Rēs gestae dīvī Augustī. || The Deeds of the Divine Augustus. (the usual name of Augustus's own account of his deeds, AD 14)

Dictum, factum! Dicta et facta. Vae victīs!

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 31's *Dictum, factum!* "Said and done!" Now look at the forms: *dictum* (from *dīcō … dictum*), *factum* (from *faciō … factum*). With no noun to describe, what are they? | **Nouns**: "a thing said" and "a thing done." A neuter fourth-key adjective standing alone becomes a **thing**: ***dictum, dictī***, "a saying"; ***factum, factī***, "a deed." English got "dictum" and "fact." |
| Grammar books call a participle (or adjective) used as a noun a **substantive** (old-school word; "standing on its own"). *Dicta et facta*? | Neuter plurals: "**words and deeds**," "things said and things done." |
| *Datum* (from *dō … datum*) is "a thing given." What's the plural, and where have you seen it? | ***Data***, "things given": English "data" is the Latin plural. Likewise ***scrīptum*** "a thing written," and *postscrīptum*, "written after" (modern Latin; P.S.). |
| The motto. *Gerō, gerere, gessī, gestum*: carry on, do. *Rēs gestae*? | "**Things carried out**": deeds, achievements. Augustus left his *Rēs gestae* to be cut on bronze tablets in front of his tomb (a stone copy survives in Ankara). *Gestae* agrees with *rēs* (feminine plural, Chapter 23). |
| A masculine participle alone is a **person**. *Vincō … victum*: conquer. *Victī*? | "**The conquered**" (people who have been conquered). Like Chapter 20's *fortēs*, "the brave." |
| Livy 5.48 tells how the Gauls captured Rome (390 BC). The Romans were weighing out gold to buy them off and complained that the Gauls' weights were false. A Gaul (later writers say their chief, Brennus) threw his sword onto the scales, and the Romans heard: *Vae victīs!* (*Vae*: "woe," + dative.) | "**Woe to the conquered!**" *Victīs* is dative plural, "to the conquered (people)": a participle as a noun, in the dative. |

## 9. Real lines

:: Multās per gentēs et multa per aequora vectus. || Carried through many nations and over many seas. (Catullus 101.1)

Īnfantem pannīs involūtum et positum in praesēpiō.

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Luke 2:12, the angel to the shepherds: *Et hoc vōbīs signum: inveniētis īnfantem pannīs involūtum et positum in praesēpiō.* (*Īnfāns, īnfantis*: baby; *pannus*: cloth; *involvō … involūtum*: wrap; *praesēpium*: manger; *signum*: sign.) Find the two participles and their noun. | ***Involūtum*** and ***positum***, both accusative masculine singular with ***īnfantem***, the object of *inveniētis* (Chapter 17's future). "And this (will be) a sign for y'all: you'll find a baby **wrapped** in cloths and **laid** in a manger." *Pannīs*: the tool. |
| Chapter 28's first line of the *Aeneid* continues (1.3) about Aeneas: *multum ille et terrīs iactātus et altō.* (*Iactō … iactātum*: toss, a cousin of *iaciō*; *altum*, the deep sea.) | "**Much tossed**, that man, both on lands and on the deep." *Iactātus* agrees with *ille*. (*Terrīs, altō*: place where, without *in*, as poets often do.) |
| The motto: Catullus 101.1, his journey to his brother's grave (Chapter 13's *Frāter, avē atque valē* is line 10). *Vehō … vectum*: carry. Who is *vectus*? | Catullus himself (the next word is *adveniō*, "I arrive"): "**Carried** through many nations and over many seas, I arrive…" Masculine singular: the speaker. |
| Chapter 36 cut Ovid's participles. Here is one: Daedalus, after kissing Icarus, *pennīsque levātus / ante volat* (*Metamorphoses* 8.212–213). (*Levō*: lift, Chapter 23; *ante*: ahead.) | "And, **lifted** on his wings, he flies ahead." *Levātus* agrees with Daedalus; *pennīs* is the tool. |
| Chapter 35 Lectiō: *Cāseus ablātus est!* (Chapter 32's *auferō … ablātum*.) Now say: "The doctor ate the carried-off cheese in the street." | ***Medicus cāseum ablātum in viā ēdit.*** *Ablātum* agrees with *cāseum*. In good English: "The doctor carried the cheese off and ate it in the street." |
| Chapter 14: *Catulus doctus est!* "The puppy is clever!" *Doceō, docēre, docuī, doctum*: teach. What is *doctus*, really? | The fourth key of *doceō*: "**taught**," so "well taught, learned, clever." Many everyday Latin adjectives are participles in disguise: *doctus, apertus* "open," *dīlēctus* "beloved." |

## 10. Old friends, new eyes

:: Iacta ālea est. || The die has been cast. (Caesar at the Rubicon, in Suetonius, *Julius* 32)

Liber lēctus. Cāseus ēsus. Avus laudātus!

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 31 Lectiō: *Canis in hortum ductus est.* Rebuild it as a describer: "The dog, taken into the garden, barked." (*Dūcō … ductum*; *lātrō*: bark.) | ***Canis in hortum ductus lātrāvit.*** Main verb *lātrāvit*; *ductus* describes the dog. |
| Chapter 36's myth: *ālae factae sunt.* "Daedalus gave Icarus the wings he'd made." (*Ālās factās*.) | ***Daedalus Īcarō ālās factās dedit.*** *Factās* agrees with *ālās* (accusative feminine plural). |
| Chapter 36's *Ā mē servātur* (Grandma, keeping the cheese). "Grandpa found the cheese kept by Grandma." | ***Avus cāseum ab Aviā servātum invēnit.*** |
| Chapter 30 told the deponent *loquor* to wait for Chapter 43. Its participle waits too. For now: does every fourth-key adjective mean "having been …ed"? | In this chapter, **yes**: received, passive, done to the noun. (Chapter 43 shows a family of verbs whose participles break this rule.) |
| A peek ahead. In Chapter 40 the participle and its noun go off together in the **ablative**, on their own: *urbe captā*, "the city having been captured." Do you need it today? | **No.** Just notice it when you meet it. Today the participle always hangs on a noun that has a job in the sentence. |

## 11. Traps

:: Nōn laudātus, sed amātus. || Not praised, but loved. (teaching line)

Laudātus ≠ "having praised."

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Does *Pater laudātus* mean "Dad, having praised"? | **No.** "Dad, **having been praised**." The perfect passive participle is always passive: Dad received the praise. |
| Then how does Latin say "Having bought the cheese, Dad carried it home"? | It flips the participle onto the **thing**: ***Pater cāseum emptum domum portāvit.*** "Dad carried home the bought cheese." (Section 7's "and" English.) |
| *Liber lātus* (Chapter 31). Carried or wide? | Either! *Lātus* (fourth key of *ferō*) = "**carried**"; the adjective *lātus* = "**wide**." Same letters; the story decides. |
| *Dictum est* vs *dictum*. Which is "it has been said," which is "a saying"? | ***Dictum est***: two pieces, the finished passive. ***Dictum*** alone, as a subject or object: "a saying." *Amō hoc dictum*: "I love this saying" (one piece, the object of *amō*). Find the main verb first; if *est* could pair with the participle, read it as two pieces. |
| *Laudātus sum* (Chapter 31) vs *laudātus* (today). | ***Laudātus sum***: "**I have been praised**" (with *sum*, the verb). ***Laudātus*** alone: "**praised**," describing someone. Grandpa in the Lectiō uses the first. |

## 12. Fix it

:: Errāta corrēcta! || Mistakes corrected! (teaching line; Chapter 31)

Emptus an emptum? Ā Patre an Patre?

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Dad is carrying the bought cheese": *Pater cāseum emptus portat.* | ***Pater cāseum emptum portat.*** The participle follows *cāseum* into the accusative. |
| "There's nothing in the opened box": *In capsā apertus nihil est.* | ***In capsā apertā nihil est.*** Feminine ablative, like *capsā*. |
| "The feathers gathered by Quīntus": *Pennae Quīntō collēctae.* | ***Pennae ā Quīntō collēctae.*** A person doing it needs *ā/ab*. |
| "To John, praised by everyone": *Iōannī, ab omnibus laudātus.* | ***Iōannī, ab omnibus laudātō.*** Dative, to agree with *Iōannī*. |
| "Dad, having praised John, laughed": *Pater, Iōannem laudātus, rīsit.* | Can't be done that way: *laudātus* is passive. ***Pater Iōannem laudāvit et rīsit.*** "Dad praised John and laughed." |

## 13. Lectiō: *Laudātus!*

:: Laudātus ab hīs, culpātus ab illīs. || Praised by these, blamed by those. (after Horace, *Satires* 1.2.11)

Pulvīnus apertus. Pennae sparsae. Dōnum emptum.

Diē quīntā, the morning after Chapter 36. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. As you read, hunt the participles and ask: which noun does it agree with? Done by whom? Which English fits best? New in the story: *pulvīnus*, pillow (Chapter 36); *pannus*, cloth; *impleō*, fill; *coquō … coctum*, cook, bake (Chapter 34's *Avia pulchrē coquit*); *satur*, full (of food).

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Grandma's first two lines: *pulvīnus… ā Quīntō apertus* · *pennās… sparsās*. Which nouns, which cases? | ***Apertus*** with *pulvīnus* (nominative, the subject); ***sparsās*** with *pennās* (accusative, the object of *videō*). Two participles, two cases. |
| Grandpa: *Canis cāseum meum in āere captum ēdit.* Why does the English say "caught … and ate it"? | *Captum* sits on the **object** (*cāseum*) and tells the earlier deed: the cheese was caught first, then eaten. Section 7's "and" English. |
| Cornēlia: *Canis, heri ab omnibus culpātus, hodiē pennās colligit.* Which of the Many Englishes fits? | "**Although** he was blamed by everyone yesterday" (or plain "blamed by everyone yesterday"): the blaming came before, and he helps anyway. |
| Mom's *Rēs gestae Iōannis* and the cloth: *Iōannī doctō, ab omnibus amātō.* Name the uses and the case of *doctō* and *amātō*. | *Rēs gestae*: *gestae* describes *rēs*, "things carried out," so "deeds" (Augustus's title). *Lēctī, servātī*: describers without *sunt* (headline style). *Doctō, amātō*: **dative** singular, agreeing with *Iōannī*: "**to** John, learned, loved by all." |
| Grandpa: *Ego numquam laudātus sum!* vs Grandma: *Canis, ā mē culpātus, nunc ā mē laudātur.* Which is two pieces, which one piece? | *Laudātus sum*: two pieces, Chapter 31's finished passive ("I have been praised"). *Culpātus*: one piece, a describer ("scolded by me"); *laudātur* is Chapter 30's one-word present. |
| *Pulvīnum meum, ā tē vacuum factum.* Why *factum*, and why "which you emptied"? | *Factum* (from *faciō*) agrees with *pulvīnum* (accusative masculine): "made empty by you." Who / which English (Law of the Many Englishes) is the smoothest. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Dictum, factum! || Said and done! (teaching line)

3. Describe five things in the room by what was done to them, with a doer: *liber ā mē lēctus · cāseus ā Patre emptus · pennae ā Quīntō collēctae…*
