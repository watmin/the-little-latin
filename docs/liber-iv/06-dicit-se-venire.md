# Caput XLII: Dīcit Sē Venīre

*Liber IV, Verba Agentia · Chapter 42*

*In which Latin learns to gossip. Marcus runs in: "Grandpa is sleeping!" You can pass that on two ways: with his exact words in quotation marks, or in your own words: "Marcus says **that** Grandpa is sleeping." English needs a "that." Latin doesn't have one. It puts the reported subject in the accusative and the reported verb in the infinitive, and Chapter 41's six infinitives tell the time. Today you report what people say, think, know, hear, and deny, and you learn which "he" is which: Chapter 21's* sē *comes back to point at the speaker. This pays off Chapter 41's "they say that…" promise and Chapter 34's psalm. Along the way: a message that changes as it's passed along, a poet's girlfriend who'd marry no one but him, a question in the Gospel, the rumor that Idomeneus has fled Crete, Juno's bad news, and an old man who finds nothing human foreign to him. Speaking goal: report what someone said, and give your own opinion with* putō.

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Marcus dīcit Avum dormīre* · *Dīcit sē venīre* · *Dīcit sē ventūrum esse* · *Negat sē cāseum ēdisse* · *Quid putās? Putō…*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** direct statement (the exact words, in quotes; *ōrātiō rēcta*) · indirect statement (reported speech; *ōrātiō oblīqua*) · accusative and infinitive (the reported-speech pattern) · verbs of saying and thinking (the verbs that open reported speech)
    - **Forms:** no new endings: Chapter 41's six infinitives with an accusative subject · *sē ventūrum esse, sē ventūram esse, sē ventūrōs esse* · *cāseum ēsum esse* · *fore*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Reported Word · the Law of the Borrowed Clock, reported edition · the Law of the Speaker's *Sē*
    - **Words:** *putō, crēdō, negō, spērō, sentiō, nūntiō, nūgae, vērus, falsus, Quid putās?*
    - **Reading:** *Quis Hoc Dīxit?* (a message about the new cheese is passed from kid to kid and comes out wrong; the shopkeeper won't sell to Mom; Grandma goes instead)
    - **Speaking:** report what someone says, said, or will do; deny something with *negō*; ask *Quid putās?* and give your opinion with *putō*

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep three old friends close: Chapter 41's six infinitives (*laudāre, laudāvisse, laudātūrus esse…*), Chapter 21's Law of Looking Back (*sē* vs *eum*), and your accusatives.

## 1. Two ways to tell it

:: Dīcit sē venīre. || He says that he is coming. (teaching line; the chapter's title)

"Avus dormit." Marcus dīcit Avum dormīre.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Marcus runs in: *"Avus dormit!"* Now you tell Grandma what Marcus said. Two ways, in English? | **"Marcus says, 'Grandpa is sleeping.'"** (his exact words, in quotation marks) or **"Marcus says that Grandpa is sleeping."** (his words, retold in yours). |
| Grammar books name the two. Plain words? | The exact words in quotes are a **direct statement** (direct speech; old-school ***ōrātiō rēcta***, "straight speech"). The retold version is an **indirect statement**: **reported speech** (old-school ***ōrātiō oblīqua***, "slanted speech," or "indirect discourse"). |
| Direct speech in Latin is easy: *Marcus dīcit: "Avus dormit."* Now the reported version: ***Marcus dīcit Avum dormīre.*** What two things changed? | *Avus* became ***Avum*** (accusative) and *dormit* became ***dormīre*** (infinitive). Word for word: "Marcus says Grandpa to be sleeping." |
| Where's the Latin word for "that"? | **There isn't one.** The accusative and the infinitive do the work of "that." Don't add a word for it. |
| Chapter 41 promised: "English's 'they say that…' has a Latin twin with a different case." You know *Avus cāseum ēdisse dīcitur*. Now: "They say that Grandpa ate the cheese." | ***Dīcunt Avum cāseum ēdisse.*** Same *ēdisse*, but now Grandpa is ***Avum***, accusative: the subject of *dīcunt* is "they," and Grandpa is only being reported. |

## 2. Accusative + infinitive

:: Dīcit canem lātrāre. || He says that the dog is barking. (teaching line)

Canis lātrat → canem lātrāre.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Direct: *"Canis lātrat."* Report it after *Marcus dīcit…* | ***Marcus dīcit canem lātrāre.*** "Marcus says that the dog is barking." *Canis* → *canem*; *lātrat* → *lātrāre*. |
| Grammar books call this the **accusative and infinitive** ("subject accusative" in some books). A plain-words name? | "The **reported-speech pattern**": the subject of the reported sentence goes into the **accusative**, and its verb becomes an **infinitive**. |
| Does English ever do this? "I believe **him** to be honest." "I know **her** to be a thief." | **Yes!** *Him* and *her* are English's leftover accusatives, followed by an infinitive, "to be." English usually prefers "that he is honest"; Latin almost always uses the accusative and infinitive. |
| The object stays an object. *"Avus cāseum edit."* Report it after *Cornēlia dīcit…* | ***Cornēlia dīcit Avum cāseum edere.*** Two accusatives now: *Avum* (who eats) and *cāseum* (what gets eaten). Common sense and word order tell them apart: the eater usually comes first. |
| Plural: *"Līberī lūdunt."* After *Avia dīcit…* | ***Avia dīcit līberōs lūdere.*** *Līberī* → *līberōs*. The infinitive doesn't change for number. |
| *"Pater cantat."* After *Audiō…*, "I hear…" | ***Audiō Patrem cantāre.*** "I hear that Dad is singing." Hearing opens the door just like saying. |

!!! success "The Law of the Reported Word"
    When you report what someone says, thinks, knows, or perceives:

    1. **No word for "that."**
    2. The reported subject goes into the **accusative**.
    3. The reported verb becomes an **infinitive**.

    | direct (the exact words) | reported (*Marcus dīcit…*) |
    |---|---|
    | *"Avus dormit."* | *Marcus dīcit **Avum dormīre**.* |
    | *"Canis lātrat."* | *Marcus dīcit **canem lātrāre**.* |
    | *"Līberī lūdunt."* | *Marcus dīcit **līberōs lūdere**.* |

## 3. Me, you, us, y'all

:: Marcus dīcit mē dormīre! || Marcus says that I'm sleeping! (teaching line)

Mē, tē, nōs, vōs.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Grandpa protests: "Marcus says that **I'm** sleeping!" (*Ego* → ?) | ***Marcus dīcit mē dormīre!*** *Ego* becomes ***mē***, accusative. |
| "Marcus says that **you're** sleeping." "…that **we're** singing." "…that **y'all** are playing." | ***Marcus dīcit tē dormīre · nōs cantāre · vōs lūdere.*** (*Vōs*: you all, y'all.) |
| And the person you tell? "I tell **you** that the dog is hungry." (*Ēsuriō*: be hungry, Chapter 39.) | ***Tibi dīcō canem ēsurīre.*** The listener goes in the dative (*tibi*, "to you"); the reported subject goes in the accusative (*canem*). |
| Grandpa grumbles: "They say I'm a thief." | ***Dīcunt mē fūrem esse.*** *Fūrem* is accusative too, because it describes *mē* (section 6 has more of these). |

## 4. The verbs that open the door

:: Sciō tē cāseum ēdisse. || I know that you ate the cheese. (teaching line)

Dīcō, putō, sciō, audiō, videō.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Which verbs can open the door to reported speech? | Verbs of **saying** (*dīcō*, *nārrō* tell, *nūntiō* announce, *negō* deny), **thinking** (*putō* think, *crēdō* believe, *spērō* hope), **knowing** (*sciō, nesciō*), and **perceiving** (*audiō, videō, sentiō* feel, realize). Grammar books lump them together as "**verbs of saying and thinking**." |
| *Sciō tē cāseum ēdisse.* | "I know that you ate the cheese." *Sciō* (Chapter 6) + *tē* + *ēdisse*. |
| *Crēdō Avum innocentem esse.* (*Crēdō*: believe, from Chapter 18's motto *Quae volumus, et crēdimus libenter*.) | "I believe that Grandpa is innocent." *Innocentem* agrees with *Avum*. |
| *Vīcīnus nūntiat Mātrem venīre.* (*Vīcīnus*: neighbor, Chapter 41.) | "The neighbor **sends word** that Mom is coming." *Nūntiō*: announce, report. |
| Chapter 38's participle: *Canem lātrantem audiō.* Now ***Canem lātrāre audiō.*** What's the difference? | *Lātrantem*: "I hear the dog **barking**" (the sound itself). *Lātrāre*: "I hear **that** the dog is barking" (a fact someone told you, or you worked out). *Sentiō canem ēsurīre*: "I **sense** that the dog is hungry." |

## 5. The clock, borrowed again

:: Marcus dīxit Avum dormīvisse. || Marcus said that Grandpa had slept. (teaching line)

Dormīre, dormīvisse, dormītūrum esse.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 41's Law of the Borrowed Clock still works. *Marcus dīcit Avum dormīre.* | "Marcus says that Grandpa **is sleeping**." The present infinitive means **same time** as the saying. |
| *Marcus dīcit Avum dormīvisse.* | "Marcus says that Grandpa **slept**" (has slept). The perfect infinitive means **before** the saying. |
| *Marcus dīcit Avum dormītūrum esse.* Why *-um*? | "Marcus says that Grandpa **will sleep**." The future means **after**. *Dormītūrum* is accusative to agree with *Avum*. |
| Push the main verb into the past: *Marcus dīxit Avum dormīre.* | "Marcus said that Grandpa **was sleeping**." Same time as the saying, and the saying is now in the past. |
| *Marcus dīxit Avum dormīvisse · dormītūrum esse.* | "Marcus said that Grandpa **had slept**" · "that Grandpa **would sleep**." |
| English changed "is" to "was," "slept" to "had slept," "will" to "would." Did the Latin infinitives change? | **No.** English shifts its verbs back after a past "said" (English grammar books call it **backshift**). Latin keeps the same three infinitives; they borrow the time from *dīcit* or *dīxit*. |

!!! success "The Law of the Borrowed Clock, reported edition"
    In reported speech, the infinitive tells time **compared with the verb of saying**:

    | infinitive | time | *Marcus dīcit…* (says) | *Marcus dīxit…* (said) |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | *Avum dormīre* | **same time** | that Grandpa is sleeping | that Grandpa was sleeping |
    | *Avum dormīvisse* | **before** | that Grandpa slept | that Grandpa had slept |
    | *Avum dormītūrum esse* | **after** | that Grandpa will sleep | that Grandpa would sleep |

## 6. Everything in the accusative

:: Putō cēnam bonam fore. || I think dinner will be good. (teaching line)

Avus fessus est → Avum fessum esse.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *"Avus fessus est."* Report it after *Avia dīcit…* | ***Avia dīcit Avum fessum esse.*** The describing word follows its noun into the accusative: *fessus* → *fessum*. |
| Chapter 41: *Cāseus ēsus esse dīcitur.* Now: "They say that the cheese has been eaten." | ***Dīcunt cāseum ēsum esse.*** The participle agrees with ***cāseum***, accusative. In Chapter 41 *ēsus* was nominative because *cāseus* was the subject of *dīcitur*. |
| "Marcus says that Grandma will sing." "…that the kids will play." | ***Marcus dīcit Aviam cantātūram esse · līberōs lūsūrōs esse.*** The *-ūrus* piece agrees: feminine accusative *-am*, masculine plural accusative *-ōs*. |
| Chapter 41's *fore* never changes. "I think dinner will be good." (*Putō*: I think; more in section 10.) | ***Putō cēnam bonam fore.*** *Bonam* agrees with *cēnam*; *fore* (= *futūram esse*) stays the same. |
| Latin often leaves out *esse*, as Chapter 41 showed with *Vidēris fessus*. *Putō Avum fessum.* | "I think Grandpa is tired." Same with participles: *Dīcunt cāseum ēsum*, "They say the cheese has been eaten." The accusative shows it's reported. |

## 7. *Sē* or *eum*?

:: Marcus dīcit sē ēsurīre. || Marcus says that he (Marcus) is hungry. (teaching line)

Sē: ipse. Eum: alius.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 21's Law of Looking Back: *Marcus sē lavat* vs *Marcus eum lavat.* Remind me. | ***Sē*** points back to the subject: Marcus washes **himself**. ***Eum*** points to someone else: Marcus washes **him**. |
| Marcus says, *"Ēsuriō!"* Report it: "Marcus says that he is hungry." | ***Marcus dīcit sē ēsurīre.*** *Ego* becomes ***sē***, pointing back to the one who says it: Marcus. |
| *Marcus dīcit eum ēsurīre.* | "Marcus says that **he** (someone else, maybe Quintus) is hungry." English "he" could mean either; Latin can't be misread. |
| Cornelia: *"Fessa sum."* Report it. | ***Cornēlia dīcit sē fessam esse.*** Here *sē* means Cornelia, so the describing word is feminine: *fessam*. |
| The kids: *"Lūdimus!"* | ***Līberī dīcunt sē lūdere.*** *Sē* is the same in singular and plural; *dīcunt* tells you it's "they." |
| Grandpa: *"Cāseus meus est."* Report it with Chapter 21's *suus*. | ***Avus dīcit cāseum suum esse.*** "Grandpa says that the cheese is **his** (his own)." *Cāseum eius esse* would make it somebody else's. |

!!! success "The Law of the Speaker's *Sē*"
    Inside reported speech, ***sē*** and ***suus*** point back to **the one doing the saying or thinking** (the subject of *dīcit, putat, negat…*). ***Eum, eam, eius*** point to **somebody else**. It's Chapter 21's Law of Looking Back, reaching into the reported words.

    | Latin | English |
    |---|---|
    | *Marcus dīcit **sē** ēsurīre.* | Marcus says that he (Marcus) is hungry. |
    | *Marcus dīcit **eum** ēsurīre.* | Marcus says that he (someone else) is hungry. |
    | *Avus dīcit cāseum **suum** esse.* | Grandpa says that the cheese is his (own). |

## 8. *Dīcit sē ventūrum esse*

:: Dīcit sē ventūrum esse. || He says that he will come. (teaching line)

Veniō. Vēnī. Veniam.

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The title: *Dīcit sē venīre.* | "He says that he **is coming**." *Sē* = the speaker himself; *venīre* = same time. |
| "He says that he came." "He says that he will come." (Chapter 39: *ventūrus*.) | ***Dīcit sē vēnisse · Dīcit sē ventūrum esse.*** *Vēnī* → *vēnisse*; *ventūrus* → ***ventūrum***, agreeing with *sē*. |
| Grandma: *"Veniam."* Report it. And the kids: *"Veniēmus."* | ***Avia dīcit sē ventūram esse · Līberī dīcunt sē ventūrōs esse.*** *Sē* looks the same for him, her, and them, but the participle shows who it means: *-am*, *-ōs*. |
| *Spērō*, "I hope," looks ahead, so it likes the future infinitive. "I hope that Mom will come with the cheese." | ***Spērō Mātrem cum cāseō ventūram esse.*** (*Spērō* is from Chapter 5's motto, *Dum spīrō, spērō*: "While I breathe, I hope.") |
| Dad: *"Cantābō!"* Report it, then react. | ***Pater dīcit sē cantātūrum esse.*** And all together: ***Nōlī, Pater!*** |

## 9. Saying no: *negō*

:: Negō mē cāseum ēdisse! || I deny that I ate the cheese! (teaching line)

Dīcit… nōn? Negat!

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Grandpa: *"Cāseum nōn ēdī!"* Romans report a "not" with ***negō***, "say … not, deny." | ***Avus negat sē cāseum ēdisse.*** "Grandpa says that he did **not** eat the cheese," "denies that he ate it." The *nōn* hides inside *negat*. |
| Could you say *Avus dīcit sē cāseum nōn ēdisse*? | You could, but Romans much preferred ***negat***. When the report says "not," reach for *negō*. |
| "Grandpa said that he wouldn't eat the cheese." | ***Avus negāvit sē cāseum ēsūrum esse.*** *Negāvit*: past saying; *ēsūrum esse*: after. |
| Psalm 14:1 (13:1 in the Vulgate): *Dīxit īnsipiēns in corde suō: "Nōn est Deus."* (*Īnsipiēns*: fool, Chapter 41; *cor, cordis*: heart.) Direct or reported? | **Direct**: "The fool has said in his heart, 'There is no God.'" After the colon come his exact words. |
| Report the fool with *negō*. | ***Īnsipiēns negat Deum esse.*** "The fool says that there is no God." *Deus* → *Deum*, *est* → *esse*, and the *nōn* goes into *negat*. |

## 10. What do you think? *Putō*

:: Homō sum: hūmānī nīl ā mē aliēnum putō. || I am a human being: I think nothing human is foreign to me. (Terence, *The Self-Tormentor* 77)

Quid putās? Putō…

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 30 gave you *Mihi vidētur…* for opinions. The everyday verb is ***putō***, "I think." *Putō Avum fūrem esse.* | "I think that Grandpa is a thief." *Putō* + accusative and infinitive. |
| Compare Chapter 41's pattern: *Avus mihi fūr esse vidētur.* Same opinion? Which case is Grandpa in each time? | The same opinion: "Grandpa seems to me to be a thief." With *vidētur*, Grandpa is the **subject** (nominative *Avus … fūr*); with *putō*, I'm the subject and Grandpa is **reported** (accusative *Avum … fūrem*). |
| Ask a friend's opinion. Then answer it. | ***Quid putās?*** "What do you think?" ***Putō cēnam bonam fore***, "I think dinner will be good." Or ***Nōn putō***, "I don't think so." |
| The motto, Terence, *The Self-Tormentor* 77: old Chremes explains why he pokes into his neighbor's business. *Homō sum: hūmānī nīl ā mē aliēnum putō.* (*Hūmānī*: "of human things"; *nīl* = *nihil*; *aliēnus*: belonging to another, foreign, Chapter 21.) | "I am a human being: I think **nothing human** (is) **foreign** to me." *Nīl* is the accusative subject, *aliēnum* agrees with it, and *esse* is left out. |
| Catullus 1.3–4, giving his little book to his friend Cornelius: *namque tū solēbās / meās esse aliquid putāre nūgās.* (*Namque*: for; *solēbās*: you used to; *aliquid*: something, Chapter 29; *nūgae*: trifles.) | "For you used to **think** that my trifles **were** something." *Putāre* + the accusative *meās nūgās* + *esse aliquid*. (A Cornelius, like our Cornelia.) |

## 11. Real people reporting

:: Fāma volat. || Rumor flies. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 3.121; Chapter 41)

Catullus, Matthaeus, Vergilius.

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Catullus 70.1–2: *Nūllī sē dīcit mulier mea nūbere mālle / quam mihi.* (*Nūllī*: to no one, Chapter 22's *nūllus*; *nūbō*: marry, said of the bride, + dative; *mālō … quam*: Chapter 32.) | "My woman says that **she** would rather marry no one than me." ***Sē dīcit***: the title's pattern, with *sē* = the woman. (Line 2 adds "not even if Jupiter himself courted her," and the poem ends: what a woman tells her eager lover should be written on the wind and running water.) |
| Matthew 16:13: *Quem dīcunt hominēs esse Fīlium hominis?* (*Fīlius hominis*: the Son of Man.) Who is the accusative subject? | ***Fīlium hominis***: "Who do people say **that the Son of Man is**?" *Hominēs* is the nominative subject of *dīcunt*; *quem* matches *Fīlium* in the accusative. |
| Matthew 16:15: *Vōs autem quem mē esse dīcitis?* (*Autem*: but, Chapter 40.) | "But **y'all**, who do y'all say **that I am**?" ***Mē*** is the accusative subject; *quem* agrees with it. |
| Chapter 41 stopped after two words. Here's more of Vergil, *Aeneid* 3.121–122: *Fāma volat pulsum rēgnīs cessisse paternīs / Īdomenēa ducem.* (*Pellō, pulsus*: drive out; *rēgnum*: kingdom; *cēdō, cessī*: go away, leave (+ ablative, "from"); *paternus*: a father's; *Īdomenēa*: Greek-style accusative of *Īdomeneus*, a king of Crete; *dux*: leader, Chapter 13.) | "Rumor flies **that** the leader **Idomeneus** has been driven out and **has left** his father's kingdom." *Fāma volat* works like "people say"; *Īdomenēa* is the accusative subject; *cessisse* is perfect, so before. |
| *Aeneid* 1.19–20, about Juno: *Prōgeniem sed enim Trōiānō ā sanguine dūcī / audierat.* (*Prōgeniēs*: offspring, a race; *sed enim*: but in fact; *sanguis, sanguinis*: blood; *audierat* = *audīverat*.) | "But she **had heard** that **a race was springing** from Trojan blood" (literally "was being drawn"). *Prōgeniem* is the accusative subject; *dūcī* is a present passive infinitive, so same time as her hearing. (That race would one day overthrow her Carthage: Rome.) |
| Chapter 34's Psalm 133:1: *Ecce quam bonum et quam iūcundum habitāre frātrēs in ūnum!* Chapter 34 said *frātrēs* "says who does the living, a pattern Chapter 42 explains." Explain. | ***Frātrēs*** is the **accusative subject** of *habitāre*: "for brothers **to live** together." An accusative and infinitive can even be the subject of *est*: *Bonum est līberōs lūdere*, "It's good for the kids to play." |

## 12. Look-alikes and traps

:: Nōn "eum," sed "sē." || Not "him," but "himself." (teaching line)

Sē? Eum?

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Dīcit sē venīre* · *Dīcit eum venīre.* | ***Sē***: "He says that **he himself** is coming." ***Eum***: "He says that **he** (someone else) is coming." |
| *Avus cāseum ēdisse dīcitur* · *Dīcunt Avum cāseum ēdisse.* | Same news: "Grandpa is said to have eaten the cheese" · "They say Grandpa ate the cheese." **Nominative** with *dīcitur* (Chapter 41's Law of the Said and the Seen); **accusative** with *dīcunt*. |
| *Cāseus ēsus esse vidētur* · *Putō cāseum ēsum esse.* | "The cheese seems to have been eaten" (nominative, *ēsus*) · "I think the cheese has been eaten" (accusative, *ēsum*). The participle copies its subject's case. |
| *Dīxit sē venīre* · *Dīcit sē vēnisse.* | "He **said** he **was coming**" (same time, in the past) · "He **says** he **came**" (before). Check which verb carries the past. And listen: *venīre* has a short *e*, *vēnisse* a long *ē*. |
| *Dīcit quod Avus dormit*? | **Not classical Latin.** After *dīcō*, classical Latin uses the accusative and infinitive: ***Dīcit Avum dormīre.*** (Much later Latin, the Vulgate included, sometimes uses *quod* or *quia* for "that." Recognize it; don't copy it yet.) |

## 13. Fix it

:: Errāvisse mē negō; corrēxisse mē dīcō. || I deny that I made a mistake; I say that I fixed it. (teaching line)

Dīcit canis…? Dīcit canem!

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "He says that the dog is hungry": *Dīcit canis ēsurīre.* | ***Dīcit canem ēsurīre.*** The reported subject goes into the accusative. |
| "Marcus says that he (Marcus himself) is coming": *Marcus dīcit eum venīre.* | ***Marcus dīcit sē venīre.*** It points back to the speaker, so *sē*. |
| "Grandma says that she will sing": *Avia dīcit sē cantātūrum esse.* | ***Avia dīcit sē cantātūram esse.*** *Sē* is Grandma here, so the participle is feminine. |
| "He says that the cheese has been eaten": *Dīcit quod cāseus ēsus est.* | ***Dīcit cāseum ēsum esse.*** No *quod*; accusative + infinitive, and the participle agrees with *cāseum*. |
| "I think dinner will be good": *Putō cēna bona fore.* | ***Putō cēnam bonam fore.*** *Cēnam* is the accusative subject, and *bonam* agrees with it. |

## 14. Lectiō: *Quis Hoc Dīxit?*

:: Dīxī Mātrem vestram cāseum emere! || I said that your mother was buying a cheese! (the neighbor, in today's reading)

Vīcīnus dīcit… Cornēlia dīcit… Marcus dīcit…

Diē decimā: the day after Chapter 41, when Mom confessed to eating the cheese and promised to buy a new one. Today a message gets passed along, and changes on the way. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. As you read, hunt the accusatives and infinitives and ask: who is *sē*, and before, same time, or after? Old friends in the story: *maximus, minimus*, biggest, smallest (Chapter 33); *familia*, family (Chapter 9); *quoque*, also (Chapter 5).

::: dialogue lectio
Iōannēs | Diē decimā Māter ad tabernam it. Nōs cāseum novum exspectāmus. || John | On the tenth day Mom goes to the shop. We wait for the new cheese.
Vīcīnus | Salvēte! Māter vestra nūntiat sē cāseum novum emere. || Neighbor | Hello, y'all! Your mother sends word that she's buying a new cheese.
Iōannēs | Audīsne, Cornēlia? Vīcīnus dīcit Mātrem cāseum novum emere. || John | Do you hear, Cornelia? The neighbor says that Mom is buying a new cheese.
Cornēlia | Marce! Vīcīnus dīcit Mātrem cāseum novum ēmisse! || Cornelia | Marcus! The neighbor says that Mom has bought a new cheese!
Marcus | Quīnte! Cornēlia dīcit Mātrem cāseum novum ēdisse! || Marcus | Quintus! Cornelia says that Mom has eaten a new cheese!
Quīntus | Ave! Marcus dīcit tē cāseum novum ēdisse! || Quīntus | Grandpa! Marcus says that you've eaten a new cheese!
Avus | Mē? Negō mē cāseum ēdisse! Fāma iterum volat! || Grandpa | Me? I deny that I ate any cheese! The rumor's flying again!
Avia | Crēdō Avum vērum dīcere. Putō vōs omnēs errāre. || Grandma | I believe Grandpa is telling the truth. I think y'all are all wrong.
Vīcīnus | Falsum! Dīxī Mātrem vestram cāseum emere. Emere: nōn ēmisse, nōn ēdisse! || Neighbor | Wrong! I said that your mother was buying a cheese. Buying: not "has bought," not "has eaten"!
Cornēlia | Ego "ēmisse" audīvī… || Cornelia | I heard "has bought"…
Marcus | Et ego "ēdisse," et Quīntus "tē"! || Marcus | And I heard "has eaten," and Quintus heard "you"!
Avus | Vidētisne? Sciēbam mē innocentem esse! || Grandpa | See, y'all? I knew I was innocent!
Pater | Et ego putō mē nunc cantātūrum esse! || Dad | And I think I'm going to sing now!
Omnēs | Nōlī, Pater! || Everyone | Don't, Dad!
Iōannēs | Subitō Māter venit… sine cāseō. || John | Suddenly Mom arrives… without cheese.
Māter | Tabernārius negāvit sē mihi cāseum venditūrum esse! || Mom | The shopkeeper said he wouldn't sell me any cheese!
Māter | Dīxit mē cāseum omnium ēdisse, et Avum fūrem maximum esse, et canem fūrem minimum! || Mom | He said that I had eaten everyone's cheese, that Grandpa was the biggest thief, and the dog the smallest!
Iōannēs | Herī dīxī fāmam volātūram esse! || John | Yesterday I said the rumor would fly!
Avus | Negō mē fūrem esse! Ego bonus sum! || Grandpa | I deny that I'm a thief! I'm good!
Avia | Ego ībō. Tabernārius mē bonam esse scit. || Grandma | I'll go. The shopkeeper knows that I'm good.
Iōannēs | Avia abit et mox redit cum cāseō magnō. || John | Grandma goes off and soon comes back with a big cheese.
Avia | Tabernārius dīxit mē sōlam in hāc familiā bonam esse! || Grandma | The shopkeeper said that I was the only good one in this family!
Avus | Ego quoque bonus sum! Negō mē cāseum ēsūrum esse! || Grandpa | I'm good too! I say I won't eat the cheese!
Iōannēs | Avia cāseum in mēnsā pōnit et in culīnam it. Deinde redit: cāseus minor esse vidētur. || John | Grandma puts the cheese on the table and goes into the kitchen. Then she comes back: the cheese seems smaller.
Avia | Ave! Negāvistī tē cāseum ēsūrum esse! || Grandma | Grandpa! You said you wouldn't eat the cheese!
Avus | Vērum dīxī! Nōn cāseum ēdī, sed partem! || Grandpa | I told the truth! I didn't eat the cheese, just a piece!
Omnēs | In stomachō… || Everyone | In the stomach of…
Avia | …Avī! Iterum! || Grandma | …Grandpa! Again!
Pater | Nunc putō mē cantātūrum esse! || Dad | Now I think I'm going to sing!
Omnēs | Nōlī, Pater! || Everyone | Don't, Dad!

{#g-14}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The neighbor: *Māter vestra nūntiat sē cāseum novum emere.* Who is *sē*? | **Mom**, the subject of *nūntiat*: she sends word about herself. *Emere* is same time: she's buying it right now. |
| The message goes from Cornelia to Marcus to Quintus. What changes each time? | *Emere* (is buying, same time) → ***ēmisse*** (has bought, before) → ***ēdisse*** (has eaten: one letter off) → ***tē*** instead of *Mātrem*. A tense, a letter, a pronoun: that's how rumors fly. |
| The neighbor: *Dīxī Mātrem vestram cāseum emere.* Why "was buying" in the English? | *Emere* is same time as *dīxī*, and *dīxī* is past, so English backshifts "is buying" to "was buying." |
| Mom: *Tabernārius negāvit sē mihi cāseum venditūrum esse.* Who is *sē*, who is *mihi*, and when is the selling? | *Sē* = **the shopkeeper** (subject of *negāvit*); *mihi* = Mom. *Venditūrum esse* is after: "said he **wouldn't sell**." The "not" is inside *negāvit*. |
| Grandma: *Tabernārius mē bonam esse scit.* Why *bonam*? | It agrees with ***mē***, and *mē* is Grandma: feminine accusative. (Chapter 41's *Esse quam vidērī*: the shopkeeper knows she **is** good.) |
| Grandma: *Negāvistī tē cāseum ēsūrum esse!* What's Grandpa's defense? | "I told the truth! Not the cheese, just a piece." By Grandpa's logic he only denied eating **the cheese**, so a piece doesn't count. *In stomachō… Avī! Iterum!* |

## Vocābula: Words of this chapter

:: Dīcunt mē verba nova didicisse. || They say I've learned new words. (teaching line)

New patterns:

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| *Dīcit sē venīre.* | He says that he (himself) is coming (*sē* = the speaker). |
| *Dīcit eum venīre.* | He says that he (someone else) is coming. |
| *sē ventūrum esse* | that he will come (the future piece agrees with *sē*: *ventūram, ventūrōs*) |

New words:

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| *putō, putāre, putāvī, putātum* | think, suppose; + acc. + inf.: *Putō Avum dormīre*, I think Grandpa is sleeping |
| *crēdō, crēdere, crēdidī, crēditum* | believe; + acc. + inf.: *Crēdō Avum innocentem esse*, I believe Grandpa is innocent |
| *negō, negāre, negāvī, negātum* | say … not, deny: *Negat sē cāseum ēdisse*, he says he didn't eat the cheese |
| *spērō, spērāre, spērāvī, spērātum* | hope; + acc. + future inf.: *Spērō Mātrem ventūram esse*, I hope Mom will come |
| *sentiō, sentīre, sēnsī, sēnsum* | feel, sense, realize |
| *nūntiō, nūntiāre, nūntiāvī, nūntiātum* | announce, report, send word |
| *nūgae, nūgārum* | f. pl., trifles, nonsense |
| *vērus, vēra, vērum* | true; *vērum dīcere*, to tell the truth |
| *falsus, falsa, falsum* | false, wrong; *Falsum!* Wrong! |
| *Quid putās?* | What do you think? |
| *indirect statement* | reported speech: what someone says, thinks, or knows, retold without quotation marks (*ōrātiō oblīqua*) |
| *accusative and infinitive* | the reported-speech pattern: subject in the accusative, verb in the infinitive: *Dīcit Avum dormīre* |
| *verbs of saying and thinking* | the verbs that open reported speech: *dīcō, nūntiō, negō, putō, crēdō, spērō, sciō, audiō, videō, sentiō* |

Glossed in passing (no card): *ōrātiō rēcta* direct speech, *ōrātiō oblīqua* indirect speech, backshift (English tense shift after "said"), *cor, cordis* heart, *Deus* God, *hūmānī* of human things, *nīl* = *nihil*, *namque* for, *solēbās* you used to, *Cornēlius* Cornelius, *nūllī* to no one, *nūbō* marry (of the bride), *Iuppiter* Jupiter, *Fīlius hominis* the Son of Man, *autem* but, *pellō, pulsus* drive out, *rēgnum* kingdom, *cēdō, cessī* go away, *paternus* a father's, *Īdomeneus, Īdomenēa* a king of Crete, *prōgeniēs* offspring, *sed enim* but in fact, *Trōiānus* Trojan, *sanguis* blood, *audierat* = *audīverat*, *iūcundus* pleasant, *ipse* himself, *alius* someone else, *Matthaeus* Matthew, *Vergilius* Vergil, *didicisse* to have learned, *corrēxisse* to have corrected.

## Speak it aloud

:: Librō clausō, dīc tē posse. || Book closed, say that you can. (teaching line)

1. Report five direct sentences after *Marcus dīcit…*: *"Avus dormit." "Canis lātrat." "Līberī lūdunt." "Pater cantat." "Māter cāseum emit."*
2. Report one sentence in all three times, then again after *Marcus dīxit…*: *Avum dormīre / dormīvisse / dormītūrum esse.*
3. The three Laws in your own words: the Reported Word, the Borrowed Clock (reported edition), the Speaker's *Sē*.
4. Say "Marcus says that he is hungry" both ways, *sē* and *eum*, and explain the difference.
5. Three people promise to come: *Dīcit sē ventūrum esse · Avia dīcit sē ventūram esse · Līberī dīcunt sē ventūrōs esse.*
6. Deny three things with *negō*: *Negō mē cāseum ēdisse…*
7. Ask *Quid putās?* and give three opinions with *Putō…*, one with *fore*.
8. The real lines: *Nūllī sē dīcit mulier mea nūbere mālle · Quem dīcunt hominēs esse Fīlium hominis? · Homō sum: hūmānī nīl ā mē aliēnum putō.* Then read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part, and rebuild it from the English.
