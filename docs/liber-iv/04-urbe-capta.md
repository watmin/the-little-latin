# Caput XL: Urbe Captā

*Liber IV, Verba Agentia · Chapter 40*

*In which a noun and its participle walk off together. Chapter 37 promised it:* urbe captā, *"the city having been captured." Chapter 38 promised a tool for "after." Here they are, one and the same: the ablative absolute, a little two-word clause set loose in the ablative, untied from the rest of the sentence. "With the cheese eaten, Grandpa sleeps." "While Dad sings, everyone runs." "When I was a boy…" "In Cicero's consulship…" It's Caesar's favorite way to move a story along, and the Gospels' favorite way to say "when evening came." Along the way: how Romans named their years, a joke about Julius and Caesar, Horace remembering his youth, Caesar's Helvetians one last time, Jesus coming through locked doors, Livy's title (a look-alike), and "God willing." Speaking goal: say what happened first, what was going on, and when you were a boy, without a single "when."*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Urbe captā, mīlitēs clāmant* · *Cāseō ēsō, Avus dormit* · *Patre cantante, nēmō dormit* · *Mē puerō…* · *Avō invītō* · *Hīs rēbus cognitīs* · *Deō volente*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** ablative absolute (the "loose-standing ablative": noun + participle in the ablative, untied from the main sentence)
    - **Forms:** no new endings. Chapter 37's *captus* and Chapter 38's *cantāns*, put in the ablative with their noun: *urbe captā, Patre cantante, līberīs lūdentibus* · with no participle at all: *Cicerōne cōnsule, mē puerō*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Untied Pair · the Law of the Unshared Noun · the Law of the Missing "Being" (plus the Many Englishes, absolute edition)
    - **Words:** *cōnsul, invītus, cognōscō, cōnficiō, dīmittō, āmittō, iānua, rēgnō, vesper, hīs rēbus cognitīs, mē puerō, Deō volente*
    - **Reading:** *Urbe Captā* (the kids build a pillow city to guard the dinner cheese; Grandpa, banned from dinner, joins the Gauls)
    - **Speaking:** tell three things in order with ablative absolutes, say what was going on while something happened, and one thing from when you were a child

## 1. The city captured

:: Urbe captā, mīlitēs clāmant. || With the city captured, the soldiers shout. (teaching line)

Urbe captā. Cāseō ēsō. Patre cantante.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 37 gave you a peek: *urbe captā*, "the city having been captured." Which case are *urbe* and *captā*? | Both **ablative**: *urbe* from *urbs* (third family, Chapter 15), *captā* from *captus, -a, -um*, feminine to agree with *urbs*. A noun and its participle, together. |
| *Urbe captā, mīlitēs clāmant.* Who shouts? And what job does *urbe* do for *clāmant*? | ***Mīlitēs*** shout (subject). *Urbe* does **no** job for *clāmant*: it isn't the subject, it isn't the object, and there's no *in* or *cum* tying it on. It just stands there, with its participle, telling the **situation**: "the city captured." |
| Grammar books call this the **ablative absolute**. *Absolūtus* is the participle of *absolvō … absolūtum*, "untie, set loose" (English *absolve*). Why "absolute"? | It's **untied**: grammatically loose from the main sentence. A plain-words name: "the **loose-standing ablative**." The noun and participle lean on **each other**, not on the main verb. |
| English has one too: "**Weather permitting**, we'll go." "**The city captured**, the soldiers cheered." What case does English use? | English uses the plain subject form ("the city," "weather"); old grammars call it the "nominative absolute." Latin puts the pair in the **ablative**. Same idea, different case. |
| Chapter 38 said: "For 'after,' Latin needs other tools (Chapter 40 brings one)." *Cāseō ēsō, Avus dormit.* | "**After the cheese is eaten**, Grandpa sleeps." Literally "the cheese having been eaten." Here's the tool: a perfect participle in the ablative absolute gives you "after." |

## 2. Building one

:: Cāseō emptō, domum venīmus. || With the cheese bought, we come home. (teaching line)

Cāseus emptus est. Pater domum venit. → Cāseō emptō, Pater domum venit.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Two sentences: *Cāseus emptus est. Pater domum venit.* Squeeze the first into an ablative absolute: put *cāseus* in the ablative, and make the participle agree. | ***Cāseō emptō, Pater domum venit.*** *Cāseus → cāseō* (Chapter 7), *emptus → emptō*. Drop the *est*: the participle carries the meaning. |
| English for *Cāseō emptō, Pater domum venit*? Start literal, then make it sound like English. | Literal: "The cheese having been bought, Dad comes home." Better: "**When the cheese has been bought**, Dad comes home," or "**After buying the cheese**, Dad comes home" (section 4 explains that one). |
| Feminine. *Iānua clausa est. Canis lātrat.* (*Iānua*: door; *claudō … clausum*, Chapter 31.) | ***Iānuā clausā, canis lātrat.*** "With the door shut, the dog barks." *-ā* on both, like Chapter 7's *in culīnā*. |
| Plural. Chapter 37's feathers: *Pennae collēctae sunt. Quīntus rīdet.* | ***Pennīs collēctīs, Quīntus rīdet.*** "With the feathers gathered, Quintus laughs." Ablative plural *-īs* on both. |
| Add the doer, as in Chapter 37: "With the cheese bought **by Dad**, everyone laughs." | ***Cāseō ā Patre emptō, omnēs rīdent.*** *Ā Patre* tucks in between the noun and its participle, just as in *cāseus ā Patre emptus*. |
| Where does the pair sit in the sentence? | Usually **first**, or tucked in after the subject, fenced off by **commas**: *Pater, cāseō emptō, domum venit.* The commas are a hint: "loose piece here." |

## 3. Before, or at the same time

:: Patre cantante, canis fugit. || While Dad sings, the dog runs away. (teaching line)

Cāseō ēsō… Avō dormiente…

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Cāseō ēsō, Avus dormit.* Which comes first, the eating or the sleeping? | The **eating**. Chapter 37's Law of the Done Deed still holds: a perfect participle is **before** the main verb. "Once the cheese has been eaten, Grandpa sleeps." |
| Now Chapter 38's participle. *Avō dormiente, līberī cāseum edunt.* Which ending does *dormiente* have, and when is the eating? | ***-e***: Chapter 38's working-participle ablative (not *-ī*). The eating happens **at the same time** as the sleeping: "**While Grandpa sleeps**, the kids eat the cheese." |
| Move the main verb into the past: *Avō dormiente, līberī cāseum ēdērunt.* | "**While Grandpa was sleeping**, the kids ate the cheese." *Dormiente* didn't change: it borrows the main verb's clock (Chapter 39's Three Times). |
| Plural: "While the children play, Grandpa sleeps." | ***Līberīs lūdentibus, Avus dormit.*** *Lūdentibus*: Chapter 38's *-ibus*, agreeing with *līberīs*. |
| Chapter 39's *-ūrus* in an ablative absolute? | **Rare**, and mostly in later writers. Just recognize it if you meet it. The everyday pair is perfect participle (before) and present participle (same time). |

!!! success "The Law of the Untied Pair"
    An **ablative absolute** is a noun (or pronoun) + a participle, **both in the ablative**, agreeing like any adjective. It stands loose from the main sentence, usually fenced off by commas, and tells the situation:

    | Latin | participle | time | plain English |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | *Cāseō ēsō, Avus dormit.* | perfect (Chapter 37) | **before** the main verb | After the cheese is eaten, Grandpa sleeps. |
    | *Avō dormiente, līberī edunt.* | present (Chapter 38) | **same time** | While Grandpa sleeps, the kids eat. |

    The present participle's ablative here ends in ***-e***: *cantante, dormiente, lūdentibus*.

## 4. Many Englishes, again

:: Cāseō ēsō, Avus dormit. || With the cheese eaten, Grandpa sleeps. (teaching line)

Duo verba Latīna, multa Anglica.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 37's Law of the Many Englishes: one participle, many ways into English. The literal "the cheese having been eaten" is clunky. What's the first thing to try? | **When / after**: "**After** the cheese was eaten, Grandpa slept." Time is the most common meaning. |
| *Cāseō ēsō, Avus trīstis est.* | "**Because** the cheese has been eaten, Grandpa is sad." Same pair, now a reason. Latin doesn't say which: you pick from the sense. |
| *Iānuā clausā, canis tamen intrāvit.* (*Intrō, intrāre*: go in; *tamen*: Chapter 38.) | "**Although** the door was shut, the dog got in anyway." *Tamen* in the main clause flags "although." |
| *Patre cantante, nēmō dormit.* | "**With Dad singing**, nobody sleeps," or "**While** Dad sings." "With" is a handy all-purpose try. |
| Chapter 37's trap: Latin has no participle for "having bought" (active, before). Yet: *Cāseō emptō, Pater domum vēnit.* Who did the buying? | The sentence doesn't say, but the sense does: Dad. So English can say "**Having bought the cheese**, Dad came home." This is Latin's way round the missing "having ___ed": put the **thing** in the ablative with a passive participle. |
| Why is the ablative absolute so popular with Caesar? | One pair of words replaces a whole "when / after / because" clause. Caesar can stack event on event: this done, that done, then the main verb. |

!!! success "The Many Englishes, absolute edition"
    One ablative absolute, many ways into English. Try them in order and keep the one that fits:

    1. **When / after** (perfect) or **while** (present): "after the cheese was eaten," "while Dad sings."
    2. **Because / since:** "since the cheese was eaten."
    3. **Although:** "although the door was shut" (look for *tamen*).
    4. **With:** "with the door shut," "with Dad singing."
    5. **Having ___ed:** "having bought the cheese," when the main subject clearly did it.

    And to see the bones: the literal "the cheese having been eaten."

## 5. The noun can't do two jobs

:: Pater cāseum emptum portat. || Dad carries the bought cheese. (teaching line)

Cāseum emptum. Cāseō emptō.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 37: *Pater cāseum emptum portat.* What job does *cāseum* have, and so what case is *emptum*? | *Cāseum* is the **object** of *portat*, so it's accusative, and *emptum* agrees with it. The participle hangs on a noun with a job: no ablative absolute. |
| *Cāseō emptō, Pater domum venit.* Is the cheese anywhere in the main sentence? | **No.** *Pater domum venit* doesn't mention the cheese. Only then does the pair go loose in the ablative. |
| *Cāseō emptō, Pater eum portat.* (*Eum* = the cheese.) What's off? | The cheese is now in **both** places: loose in the ablative **and** the object *eum*. Latin avoids that. Say it Chapter 37's way: ***Pater cāseum emptum portat.*** (Writers very occasionally bend this; you shouldn't.) |
| "Grandpa, praised, laughs." *Avō laudātō, Avus rīdet*? | **No**: the same Grandpa can't be loose and the subject. ***Avus laudātus rīdet*** (Chapter 37). But ***Avō laudātō, Avia rīdet*** is fine: "When **Grandpa** is praised, **Grandma** laughs." Different people. |
| *Patre cantante, līberī fugiunt* vs *Līberī Patrem cantantem fugiunt.* Both good? | **Both good.** In the first, Dad isn't in the main sentence: "While Dad sings, the kids run away." In the second, he's the object: "The kids run away from Dad (as he's) singing." |
| So what's the quick test? | **Look for the noun in the main sentence.** If it's there, the participle agrees with it in that case (Chapters 37–38). If it's nowhere else, the pair goes loose in the ablative. |

!!! success "The Law of the Unshared Noun"
    The noun of an ablative absolute has **no other job** in the sentence: it isn't the subject, the object, or anything else of the main verb.

    | if the noun… | then | example |
    |---|---|---|
    | has a job in the main sentence | participle agrees in that case | *Pater **cāseum emptum** portat.* |
    | appears nowhere else | ablative absolute | ***Cāseō emptō**, Pater domum venit.* |

## 6. Two sentences, one sentence

:: Duae sententiae, ūna sententia. || Two sentences, one sentence. (teaching line)

Capsa aperta est. Canis cāseum videt.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Capsa aperta est. Canis cāseum videt.* Is *capsa* in the second sentence? Then join them. | Not there, so it goes loose: ***Capsā apertā, canis cāseum videt.*** "With the box open (once the box is opened), the dog sees the cheese." |
| *Canis lātrat. Avus dormīre nōn potest.* | ***Cane lātrante, Avus dormīre nōn potest.*** "With the dog barking, Grandpa can't sleep." Present participle, same time, *-e*. |
| Chapter 37: *Quīntus, ab Avō captus, rīsit.* Can *captus* become an ablative absolute? | **No**: Quintus is the subject of *rīsit*, so *captus* stays nominative. But change the main verb's subject: ***Quīntō ab Avō captō, omnēs rīsērunt.*** "When Quintus was caught by Grandpa, everybody laughed." |
| Chapter 37's feathers again: *Pennae ā Quīntō collēctae sunt. Avia pulvīnum implet.* (*Impleō*: fill, Chapter 37.) | ***Pennīs ā Quīntō collēctīs, Avia pulvīnum implet.*** "With the feathers gathered by Quintus, Grandma fills the pillow." |
| *Pater cāseum ēmit. Pater cāseum domum portāvit.* One sentence. Which tool? | The cheese is the object of *portāvit*, so not an ablative absolute: ***Pater cāseum emptum domum portāvit*** (Chapter 37). The Unshared Noun decides. |

## 7. No participle at all

:: Cōnsule Plancō. || When Plancus was consul. (Horace, *Odes* 3.14.28)

Cicerōne cōnsule. Mē puerō. Avō invītō.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 38: *sum* has no present participle; there's no Latin word for "being." So how would Latin say "Cicero being consul"? | Leave "being" out: ***Cicerōne cōnsule.*** Two nouns, both ablative: "with Cicero (as) consul," "**when Cicero was consul**," "in Cicero's consulship." (*Cōnsul, cōnsulis*: consul.) |
| *Mē puerō.* | "**When I was a boy**": literally "me (being) a boy." *Mē*, ablative of *ego* (Chapter 21); *puerō*, ablative of *puer*. Grandpa's favorite way to start a story. |
| *Invītus, -a, -um*: unwilling. *Avō invītō, līberī cāseum edunt.* | "**Against Grandpa's will**, the kids eat the cheese." Literally "Grandpa (being) unwilling." A noun and an adjective work too. ***Mē invītō***: against my will. |
| *Caesare duce.* (*Dux*: Chapter 13.) | "**With Caesar as leader**," "under Caesar's command." *Mē duce*: "with me in the lead." |
| Chapter 38's *absēns, praesēns*. *Patre absente, nēmō cantat.* | "**With Dad away**, nobody sings" (and everyone is secretly relieved). *Absente* ends in *-e*, like any working participle in an ablative absolute. |

!!! success "The Law of the Missing ‘Being’"
    *Sum* has no present participle, so Latin simply **leaves "being" out**. Two nouns, or a noun and an adjective, both in the ablative, make an ablative absolute too:

    | Latin | literal | plain English |
    |---|---|---|
    | *Cicerōne cōnsule* | Cicero (being) consul | in Cicero's consulship |
    | *mē puerō* | me (being) a boy | when I was a boy |
    | *Avō invītō* | Grandpa (being) unwilling | against Grandpa's will |
    | *Caesare duce* | Caesar (being) leader | under Caesar's command |

## 8. Naming the year

:: Nōn Caesare et Bibulō, sed Iūliō et Caesare cōnsulibus. || Not in the consulship of Caesar and Bibulus, but of Julius and Caesar. (Suetonius, *Julius* 20)

M. Messallā, M. Pīsōne cōnsulibus.

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Rome had no year numbers in daily life. Each year two consuls took office, and the year was named after them: *Cicerōne et Antōniō cōnsulibus*. What's that, and which case? | "**In the consulship of Cicero and Antonius**" (63 BC, the year of Chapter 19's speech against Catiline). Ablative plural *cōnsulibus*: an ablative absolute with no participle. |
| Chapter 37 read Caesar's *rēgnī cupiditāte inductus* and skipped the date. Here's the whole line, *Gallic War* 1.2.1: *Is M. Messallā, M. Pīsōne cōnsulibus rēgnī cupiditāte inductus coniūrātiōnem nōbilitātis fēcit.* (*M.* = *Marcus*; some editions spell it *Messala*, and some add *et* between the two names.) | "He [Orgetorix], **in the consulship of Marcus Messalla and Marcus Piso**, led on by a desire for kingship, formed a conspiracy of the nobles." 61 BC. One ablative absolute (two names + *cōnsulibus*), then a PPP in the nominative (*inductus*), because it belongs to the subject. |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.6, gives the Helvetians' departure date: *Is diēs erat a. d. V. Kal. Apr. L. Pīsōne, A. Gabīniō cōnsulibus.* (*a. d. V. Kal. Apr.*: the fifth day before the Kalends of April, Chapter 22.) | "That day was March 28, **in the consulship of Lucius Piso and Aulus Gabinius**": 58 BC. Year by consuls, day by Kalends. |
| Suetonius, *Julius* 20: in 59 BC Caesar's fellow consul Bibulus was pushed aside and stayed home. So some city wits, signing papers for fun, dated them "**not** *Caesare et Bibulō*, but *Iūliō et Caesare cōnsulibus*." What's the joke? | "In the consulship of **Julius** and **Caesar**": one man, *Gaius Iūlius Caesar*, counted twice, as if he were both consuls. |
| Horace, *Odes* 3.14.25–28, his hair now turning white, says he'd let a snub pass these days, but wouldn't have put up with it "hot with youth, *cōnsule Plancō*." | "**When Plancus was consul**": 42 BC, when Horace was in his early twenties. A poet dating his own youth by the consul's name, as we'd say "back in '42." |

## 9. Caesar's favorite link

:: Hīs rēbus cognitīs. || When these things were learned. (Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.33.1)

Hīs rēbus cognitīs, Caesar…

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Cognōscō, cognōscere, cognōvī, cognitum*: get to know, learn (Chapter 17's *cognōscētis*). *Hīs rēbus cognitīs.* Parse it. | *Hīs rēbus*: ablative plural of *haec rēs* (Chapters 19, 23); *cognitīs*: agreeing. "**These things having been learned**": "**On learning this**," "When he had learned these things." |
| *Gallic War* 1.33.1: *Hīs rēbus cognitīs Caesar Gallōrum animōs verbīs cōnfirmāvit.* (*Animus*: spirit, courage; *cōnfirmō*: strengthen.) | "**On learning this**, Caesar strengthened the Gauls' spirits with words." Caesar often opens a chapter with a pair like this (*Quibus rēbus cognitīs* is another): something learned, then the main deed. |
| *Gallic War* 1.6, the Helvetians (Chapters 35 and 39) ready to leave: *Omnibus rēbus ad profectiōnem comparātīs, diem dīcunt.* (*Profectiō*: departure; *comparō* here: get ready, a look-alike of Chapter 33's "compare.") | "**With everything made ready** for the departure, they name a day." Chapter 39's line (1.5.3) burned the grain they couldn't carry; now the packing is done. |
| *Gallic War* 1.30.1, the war over: *Bellō Helvētiōrum cōnfectō … ad Caesarem convēnērunt.* (*Cōnficiō, cōnfectum*: finish; envoys from almost all Gaul are the subject.) | "**When the war with the Helvetians was finished**, (envoys) came to Caesar." Goodbye, Helvetians: they burned their grain in Chapter 39's line, and this one closes their war. |
| Livy 1.34.1, on Rome's kings: *Ancō rēgnante Lucumō … Rōmam commigrāvit.* (*Rēgnō*: reign; *Ancus*: Rome's fourth king; *commigrō*: move.) | "**While Ancus was reigning**, Lucumo moved to Rome": "**In the reign of** Ancus." A present participle, same time. (Lucumo later became king himself, as Tarquinius Priscus.) |

## 10. When evening came

:: Venit Iēsus iānuīs clausīs. || Jesus came, though the doors were shut. (John 20:26)

Vespere factō. Edentibus illīs.

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Matthew 26:20: *Vespere autem factō, discumbēbat cum duodecim…* (*Vesper*: evening, Chapter 16's *vesperī*; *discumbō*: recline at table; *duodecim*: twelve, *duo* + *decem* (Chapter 22); some editions add *discipulīs suīs*.) What's *factō*? | Chapter 32's *fīō, fierī, factus sum*: "**Evening having come**," "**When evening came**, he was reclining at table with the twelve." Matthew uses exactly *Vespere autem factō* four times. |
| The next verse, 26:21: *Et edentibus illīs, dīxit: … Ūnus vestrum mē trāditūrus est.* Find the ablative absolute, and an old friend from Chapter 39. | ***Edentibus illīs***: "**while they were eating**," present participle + pronoun, ablative plural. "One of you is going to betray me": Chapter 39's ***trāditūrus est***, about Judas again. |
| Matthew 14:23, after the loaves and fishes: *Et dīmissā turbā, ascendit in montem sōlus…* (newer editions: *dīmissīs turbīs*) (*Dīmittō … dīmissum*: send away; *turba*: crowd; *ascendō*: climb.) | "And **having sent the crowd away**, he went up the mountain alone." Who sent them? Jesus, the main subject: section 4's "having ___ed" English. |
| John 20:26, a week after Easter, with Thomas there: *Venit Iēsus iānuīs clausīs et stetit in mediō et dīxit: "Pāx vōbīs."* (*Venit*: present, "comes," as in the Greek; English tells it as "came." *Stō … stetī*: stand.) Which English for *iānuīs clausīs*? | "Jesus came, **though the doors were shut**, and stood in the middle and said: 'Peace to y'all.'" "With the doors shut" works too, but the point is "although." (*Vōbīs*: plural.) |
| Matthew 27:1: *Māne autem factō…* (*Māne*: morning, Chapter 16.) | "**When morning came**…" Same pattern as *vespere factō*. Two pairs bracket the story of the last night. |

## 11. Phrases you'll meet

:: Deō volente. || God willing. (later usage)

Deō volente. Cēterīs paribus. Ab urbe conditā?

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Deō volente.* (*Volēns*: Chapter 38.) | "**God willing**": literally "God (being) willing." A later Latin phrase, still used in letters, sometimes shortened to *D.V.* |
| The Bible says the idea in a *sī* clause. James 4:15: *Sī Dominus voluerit… faciēmus hoc aut illud.* Which tense is *voluerit*? | Chapter 27's **future perfect**: "If the Lord **wills** (will have willed)… we will do this or that." *Deō volente* packs the same thought into two words. |
| *Cēterīs paribus.* (*Cēterī*: the rest; *pār*: equal, Chapter 20.) | "**Other things being equal**": no participle, the Law of the Missing "Being." A later phrase, much loved by economists. |
| *Sēde vacante.* (*Sēdēs*: seat; *vacō*: be empty, Chapter 23's *vacuus*.) | "**With the seat empty**": the Church's name for the time between one pope and the next. A present participle, *-e*. |
| Livy's history is called *Ab urbe conditā* (*condō … conditum*: found). Ablative absolute? | **No!** There's a preposition, *ab*, so the ablative is tied to it. "**From the city founded**," that is, "from the founding of the city." (Later historians counted years this way: *A.U.C.*) An ablative absolute never has a preposition. |

## 12. Traps

:: Nōn "urbs capta," sed "urbe captā." || Not "the city captured," but "with the city captured." (teaching line)

Urbs capta? Urbe captā.

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "With the city captured, the soldiers shout": *Urbs capta, mīlitēs clāmant.* | ***Urbe captā, mīlitēs clāmant.*** English puts the loose pair in the subject form; Latin wants the **ablative**. |
| "While Dad sings": *Patre cantantī*? | ***Patre cantante.*** In an ablative absolute the participle is working, so Chapter 38's ablative is *-e*. |
| *Cum Patre cantante* vs *Patre cantante.* | *Cum Patre cantante* (Chapter 38): "**with** Dad (as he) sings": Dad is company, tied by *cum*. *Patre cantante*: "**while** Dad sings," loose. A preposition means it's not absolute. |
| "Having bought the cheese, Dad came home": *Pater, cāseum emptus, domum vēnit.* | ***Cāseō emptō, Pater domum vēnit.*** *Emptus* is passive: *Pater emptus* is "Dad, **bought**." Put the cheese in the ablative instead. |
| "Grandpa snores while sleeping": *Avō dormiente, Avus stertit.* | ***Avus dormiēns stertit*** (Chapter 38). Grandpa is the subject, so the participle agrees with him: the Unshared Noun. |

## 13. Fix it

:: Errātīs corrēctīs, rīdēmus. || With the mistakes fixed, we laugh. (teaching line)

Capsa apertā? Capsā apertā.

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "With the box open, the dog found the cheese": *Capsa apertā, canis cāseum invēnit.* | ***Capsā apertā, canis cāseum invēnit.*** Both words ablative: *capsā* needs its long *-ā*. |
| "After the cheese was eaten, Grandpa slept": *Cāseō ēsus, Avus dormīvit.* | ***Cāseō ēsō, Avus dormīvit.*** The participle agrees with *cāseō*: ablative *-ō*. |
| "While the children play, Grandpa sleeps": *Līberīs lūdentēs, Avus dormit.* | ***Līberīs lūdentibus, Avus dormit.*** Ablative plural *-ibus*. |

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Grandma: *Avō absente cēnābimus.* Find the pair. Is Grandpa anywhere else in the sentence? | ***Avō absente***: "with Grandpa away." *Cēnābimus* is "we": Grandpa isn't in it, so the pair goes loose. *Absente*: working-participle *-e*. |
| *Mē invītō?! — Tē invītō.* Where's the participle? | There isn't one: pronoun + adjective, the **Missing "Being."** "Me (being) unwilling?!" "You (being) unwilling." Grandma isn't moved. |
| John: *Mē cōnsule, urbs tūta erit.* Cornelia: *Mē duce, urbem capiēmus.* Which real Roman habit is John borrowing? | Naming things by the **consul**: *Cicerōne cōnsule* → *mē cōnsule*, "with **me** as consul." Both pairs borrow the main verb's future: "the city **will** be safe," "we **will** take it." |
| *Signō datō, Gallī currunt. Avō clāmante, sellae cadunt.* Which is before, which same time? | ***Signō datō***: perfect, **before**: "the signal given," so "at the signal." ***Avō clāmante***: present, **same time**: "while Grandpa yells." |
| Grandpa's alibi. Why does John say *Avum pugnantem semper vidēbam*, not *Avō pugnante, eum semper vidēbam*? | Grandpa is the **object** of *vidēbam*, so the participle agrees with him in the accusative: *Avum pugnantem*. With *eum* in the main sentence, *Avō pugnante* would break the **Unshared Noun**. |
| The end: *Mē puerō… — Tē puerō, Ave…* What does Grandma turn around, and whose stomach is it this time? | Grandpa's "**when I was a boy**" becomes "**when you were a boy**": same pair, new pronoun. The cheese is ***in stomachō Cāseī***, the dog's. For once, Grandpa really is innocent. |
