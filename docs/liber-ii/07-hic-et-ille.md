# Caput XIX: Hic et Ille

*Liber II, Rēs Cotīdiānae · Chapter 19*

*In which you learn to point. Latin has a word for "this one, near me," another for "that one, over there," a third for "that one, near you" (often with a sneer), a plain one for "he, she, it," and one for "the same." You've been saying some of them for chapters as chunks:* Hoc est corpus meum*,* In hōc signō vincēs*,* Docēbat eōs*. Today they open up, and they share one big surprise in the genitive and dative. Then Grandma, who stayed home from Rome, goes shopping for a cheese of her own.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Quid est hoc?* · *Hic cāseus!* · *Hunc an illum?* · *Eum nōn videō.* · *Id est…* · *Semper eadem!*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** demonstrative (a "pointing word": this, that)
    - **Forms:** *hic, haec, hoc*; *ille, illa, illud*; *iste, ista, istud*; *is, ea, id*; *īdem, eadem, idem*; the genitive in *-īus* (*huius, illīus, eius*) and the dative in *-ī* (*huic, illī, eī*)
    - **Laws:** the Law of Near and Far, the Law of the Pointing Words
    - **Words:** *hic, ille, iste, is, īdem, dēmōnstrō, iubeō, obsecrō, sīc, atque, id est*
    - **Reading:** *Hunc an illum?* (Grandma at the market)
    - **Speaking:** point at things and choose: this one, that one, the same one

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep Chapter 3's *magnus, magna, magnum* handy: most of today's forms are its endings with a new front. And **point** with your finger while you say them. That's what these words are for.

---

## 1. *Hic, haec, hoc*

:: Hic est Fīlius meus dīlēctus. || This is my beloved Son. (Matthew 3:17)

Hic puer. Haec puella. Hoc mālum.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 14's motto, *Hoc est corpus meum.* What did it mean, and which word was left as a chunk? | "This is my body." ***Hoc***, "this," was a chunk. Today it opens up. |
| Read the blurb: *Hic puer. Haec puella. Hoc mālum.* Three words for "this." Why three? | Three genders: ***hic*** *puer* (masculine), ***haec*** *puella* (feminine), ***hoc*** *mālum* (neuter). "This" agrees with its noun, like an adjective: *magnus puer, magna puella, magnum mālum*. |
| Words like "this" and "that" point at things. Their grammar name? | **Demonstrative**: a "pointing word." From Latin *dēmōnstrāre*, "point out, show" (English *demonstrate*). Point while you say it: *hic cāseus!* |
| "This" for *liber*? For *mēnsa*? For *corpus*? | *Hic liber, haec mēnsa, hoc corpus.* Gender, not letters: *corpus* is neuter (Chapter 14's Disguise), so ***hoc***. |
| "This boy is small. This girl is big." | *Hic puer parvus est. Haec puella magna est.* |
| *Hic* can stand alone: *Hic est Fīlius meus dīlēctus.* (*Dīlēctus*: beloved.) Who's speaking, and how do you know *hic* is a man? | The voice from heaven at Jesus's baptism: "This is my beloved Son." Masculine *hic* alone = "this man, this one." *Haec* alone = "this woman"; *hoc* alone = "this thing." |
| Chapter 16 read *In prīncipiō erat Verbum* (John 1:1). The next verse: *Hoc erat in prīncipiō apud Deum.* (*Apud*: with.) Why *hoc*, not *hic*? | It points back at *Verbum*, the Word, which is neuter: "This (Word) was in the beginning with God." In "this is…" Latin's pointer takes the gender of the thing: *Hic est cāseus. Haec est pila. Hoc est mālum.* (*Quid est hoc?* "What is this?" is neuter because you don't know yet.) |
| Careful! Chapter 5's *Hīc habitō.* Same word? | No. ***Hīc***, long ī, is "here," a place word (Chapter 5). ***Hic***, short i, is "this" (masculine). *Hic puer hīc est*: "This boy is here." The macron is the whole difference. |
| *Hīc et nunc.* (*Nunc*: now, Chapter 16.) Which one? | The long one: "here and now." You'll often see it printed without the macron, but it's the place word. |

## 2. *Hunc cāseum!*

:: In hōc signō vincēs. || In this sign you will conquer. (Constantine's vision, as Latin tradition tells it)

Hunc cāseum volō. Hanc pilam volō. Hoc mālum volō.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Read the blurb: *Hunc cāseum volō. Hanc pilam volō. Hoc mālum volō.* What job do *hunc, hanc, hoc* do? | Object (accusative, Chapter 4): "I want this cheese, this ball, this apple." Chapter 18's *volō* with a plain object. |
| Compare *bonum cāseum, bonam pilam*. Where's their *-m* in *hunc* and *hanc*? | Hiding. Underneath sit old *hum* and *ham* plus a little pointing *-c* ("see!"). Before a *c*, the *m* turned into *n*: ***hunc, hanc***. |
| And the neuter object? | ***Hoc***, the same as the subject: Chapter 4's Law of the Neuter Twins (subject = object). |
| Chapter 17's motto, *In hōc signō vincēs.* Which case is *hōc*, and what does the macron tell you? | Ablative after *in* (place where, Chapter 7): "in this sign." The ablative has a long ***ō***, like *bonō signō*. Feminine: *in hāc viā*, long ā like *bonā*. |
| *Hoc* or *hōc*? Then: "with this girl," "in this garden." | Short ***hoc***: "this" (neuter subject or object). Long ***hōc***: "by, with, in this" (ablative, masculine or neuter). *Cum hāc puellā. In hōc hortō.* |
| Now the surprise. Chapter 10's genitive: "of the good boy" is *bonī puerī*. Guess "of this boy." | Not "*hī*." It's ***huius puerī***, HOO-yus. And the same for all three genders: *huius puellae, huius mālī*. |
| What old question word does *huius* rhyme with? | Chapter 10's ***cuius?***, "whose?" (*Cuius est?*) Pointing words and question words share this genitive. |
| The dative, "to/for this boy"? Another surprise. | ***Huic*** *puerō*, *huic puellae*, *huic mālō*: again one form for all three genders. It's one syllable: HOO-ik, squeezed into a single beat. (*Cuius*'s own dative, *cui*, "to whom," rhymes: Chapter 29.) |
| Chapter 10 said nouns like *fīlius* have the genitive *fīliī*. Is *huius* one of those? | No. *Fīlius* is a noun whose naming form ends in *-ius*. ***Huius*** is a genitive **ending** on a pointing word. Put them together: *huius fīliī*, "of this son." |
| "I give this cheese to this girl." | *Huic puellae hunc cāseum dō.* Receiver in the dative (Chapter 11), object in the accusative. |

## 3. *Hī, hae, haec*

:: Forsan et haec ōlim meminisse iuvābit. || Maybe someday it will be a pleasure to remember even this. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 1.203)

Hī puerī. Hae puellae. Haec māla.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Read the blurb: *Hī puerī. Hae puellae. Haec māla.* Compare *bonī, bonae, bona*. | Same vowels with an *h-* in front: ***hī, hae***. The neuter plural is ***haec***, with the little *-c* again. "These boys, these girls, these apples." |
| Wait: *haec* was "this" (feminine) in §1. How do you tell *haec puella* from *haec māla*? | By the company. *Puella* is feminine singular; *māla* is neuter plural. And the verb: *Haec est…* (one) vs *Haec sunt…* (many). |
| Chapter 16's Lectiō motto, *Forsan et haec ōlim meminisse iuvābit.* Which *haec*? | Neuter plural, standing alone: "these things." Aeneas, shipwrecked, tells his men that one day even **these** troubles will be good to remember. English says "even this." |
| Objects: *hōs, hās, haec*. "I want these cheeses." | *Hōs cāseōs volō.* Like *bonōs, bonās, bona*. |
| "Of these"? Is there a surprise? | No. ***Hōrum, hārum, hōrum***, like *bonōrum, bonārum*. The surprise is only in the singular. |
| "To these, with these" is one word for all genders: ***hīs***. Chapter 16's *Dabit deus hīs quoque fīnem.* (*Quoque*: also; *fīnis*: end.) | "God will give an end **to these** (troubles) too": "God will put an end to these as well." *Hīs* is the receiver, in the dative. Aeneas again, four lines earlier. |
| *Hīs*, *hīc*, *hic*: sort them. | ***Hīs***: to, for, with these. ***Hīc***: here. ***Hic***: this (masculine). |

!!! success "*Hic, haec, hoc*: this"
    | | masc. | fem. | neut. | | masc. | fem. | neut. |
    |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
    | **subject** | *hic* | *haec* | *hoc* | | *hī* | *hae* | *haec* |
    | **of** | ***huius*** | ***huius*** | ***huius*** | | *hōrum* | *hārum* | *hōrum* |
    | **to/for** | ***huic*** | ***huic*** | ***huic*** | | *hīs* | *hīs* | *hīs* |
    | **object** | *hunc* | *hanc* | *hoc* | | *hōs* | *hās* | *haec* |
    | **by/with/in** | *hōc* | *hāc* | *hōc* | | *hīs* | *hīs* | *hīs* |

    Singular on the left, plural on the right. The bold forms are the surprise; almost everything else is *bonus, bona, bonum* with *h-*.

## 4. *Ille, illa, illud*

:: Ille mī pār esse deō vidētur. || That man seems to me to be equal to a god. (Catullus 51.1)

Hic cāseus parvus est. Ille cāseus magnus est!

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Read the blurb. Which cheese is near you, and which is over there? | ***Hic*** *cāseus*, "this cheese," is near: small. ***Ille*** *cāseus*, "that cheese," is over there: big. Point at each. |
| The three genders: *ille, illa, illud*. Compare *magnus, magna, magnum*. What's odd? | The masculine ends in ***-e*** (*ille*), and the neuter in ***-ud*** (*illud*), not *-um*. The feminine *illa* is normal. |
| "That girl." "That apple." | *Illa puella. Illud mālum.* |
| The rest is mostly *magnus*: *illum, illam, illud; illō, illā, illō; illī, illae, illa*… "I want that cheese." | *Illum cāseum volō.* |
| Genitive and dative? Remember *huius, huic*. | ***Illīus*** (il-LEE-us), "of that," and ***illī***, "to/for that," the same for all three genders. The *-ius* genitive and *-ī* dative again. |
| *Illī* is also the plural "those (men)." How do you tell *Illī puerō cāseum dō* from *Illī puerī currunt*? | By the company, like Chapter 11's Look-alikes. *Puerō* is dative singular: "I give cheese **to that** boy." *Puerī currunt* is plural: "**Those** boys are running." |
| The shopkeeper's question: *Hunc cāseum an illum vīs?* (Chapter 17's *an*, "or," in a question.) | "Do you want this cheese or that one?" Latin doesn't repeat *cāseum*: *illum* alone is "that one." |
| Spanish *el*, French *le* and *la*, Italian *il*: where did they come from? | From ***ille, illa***. Late Latin speakers said "that" so often, it wore down into the word "the." Classical Latin has no "the." |
| The motto, *Ille mī pār esse deō vidētur.* (*Mī*: a poet's short *mihi*; *pār*: equal, Chapter 20's family; *vidētur*: "seems," Chapter 18's *videntur*.) Who is *ille*? | "**That** man seems to me to be equal to a god." Catullus, after the Greek poet Sappho, watches a man sitting across from the woman he loves. *Ille* alone: "that man (over there)." |

!!! success "The Law of the Pointing Words"
    ***Ille, iste, is, īdem*** **decline almost like *magnus, magna, magnum*, with three surprises.** (*Hic* has extra quirks of its own, the little *-c*, but shares surprises 1 and 2.)

    1. **Genitive singular *-īus*,** the same for all three genders: *huius, illīus, istīus, eius, eiusdem* (like Chapter 10's *cuius?*).
    2. **Dative singular *-ī*,** the same for all three genders: *huic, illī, istī, eī, eīdem*.
    3. **Neuter subject and object end in *-d*:** *illud, istud, id* (and *idem* is *id* + *-dem*).

    In the plural, no surprises. (A few ordinary adjectives borrow surprises 1 and 2, like *ūnus*, "one": Chapter 22.)

## 5. *Iste*

:: Nōlī, obsecrō, istum disturbāre! || Please, don't mess up that (dust by you)! (Archimedes, in Valerius Maximus 8.7 ext. 7; *inquit*, "he said," left out)

Hic cāseus meus est. Iste cāseus tuus est. Ille cāseus Marcī est.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Read the blurb. *Hic* is mine, *ille* is Marcus's. Where is *iste cāseus*? | Near **you**: "that cheese of yours, that one by you." Three distances: *hic* (near me), *iste* (near you), *ille* (over there). |
| Guess the forms: *iste, ista, istud*… | Exactly like *ille*: *istum, istam, istud*; *istīus*; *istī*; plural *istī, istae, ista*… |
| *Iste* often sneers. Cicero, attacking a traitor in the Senate (*Against Catiline* 1.1): *Quam diū etiam furor iste tuus nōs ēlūdet?* (*Quam diū*: how long; *etiam*: still; *furor*: madness; *ēlūdō, ēlūdere*: mock.) | "How long will that madness of yours keep mocking us?" ***Iste*** + *tuus*: "that … of yours," and Cicero means it nastily. *Ēlūdet* is a future (Chapter 17). |
| Chapter 18's motto, *Nōlī turbāre circulōs meōs*, was "later tradition." The oldest Latin telling (Valerius Maximus) has: *"Nōlī," inquit, "obsecrō, istum disturbāre."* (*Inquit*: he said; *obsecrō*: please, I beg you; *disturbāre*: knock apart, mess up.) What is *istum*? | "That (one by you)": the dust (*pulvis*, masculine) where Archimedes was drawing, right at the soldier's feet. He shielded it with his hands: "Please, don't mess up **that**!" |
| "Give me that cheese (the one you're holding)!" | *Dā mihi istum cāseum!* Near you, so *iste*. |
| Is *iste* always nasty? | No. Often it just means "that … of yours, that … by you." The tone comes from the speaker. When Cicero says it, duck. |

!!! success "The Law of Near and Far"
    **Point with the right word:**

    - ***hic, haec, hoc***: **this**, near me (*hīc*, "here," is its place word)
    - ***iste, ista, istud***: **that**, near you; "that … of yours" (often scornful)
    - ***ille, illa, illud***: **that**, over there, away from us both
    - ***is, ea, id***: just **points back** to someone already mentioned: he, she, it, that (§6)

## 6. *Is, ea, id*

:: Id est. || That is. (Latin's "i.e.")

Ubi est Marcus? Eum nōn videō. Ubi est Cornēlia? Eam nōn videō.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Read the blurb: *Eum nōn videō. Eam nōn videō.* | "I don't see **him**. I don't see **her**." ***Eum***, him; ***eam***, her. |
| Chapter 16's motto *Docēbat eōs*, and Chapter 17's *Libenter eum audiēbat*. Crack them. | "He was teaching **them**" (Jesus and the crowd: *eōs*, plural object). "He liked to listen to **him**" (Herod and John the Baptist: *eum*). |
| *Is, ea, id* is the plain pointer: no near or far. Guess the objects from *bonum, bonam, bonum*… | *Eum, eam,* ***id*** (that *-d* again). Plural *eōs, eās, ea*. Ablative *eō, eā, eō*. Plural subject *eī* (also *iī*), *eae, ea*. |
| And the genitive and dative? Law of the Pointing Words. | ***Eius*** (EH-yus), "of him, of her, of it," and ***eī***, "to/for him, her, it." One form for all three genders. |
| *Ubi est cāseus? Eum nōn videō.* Why *eum*, if a cheese isn't a "him"? | *Cāseus* is masculine, so the pointer is masculine. English says "it." *Ubi est mālum? Id nōn videō. Ubi est pila? Eam nōn videō.* |
| *Liber eius.* | "His book" or "her book": literally "the book of him/her." Someone else's. (When it's the subject's **own** book, Latin uses a different word: Chapter 21.) |
| Why do you rarely see *is* as a subject? | The verb already says "he": *venit*, "he comes." Latin adds *is* only to point: *Is venit*, "**that** man is coming, the one we mentioned." |
| *Ea*: how many jobs? | ***Ea***: "she" (subject); ***ea***: "those things" (neuter plural); ***eā***: "by/with her" (ablative, long ā). Company and macron decide. |
| The motto, *Id est.* | "That is": English writes it *i.e.* *Id* points at what you just said. |

!!! success "*Ille, illa, illud* and *is, ea, id*"
    | | *ille* m. | f. | n. | *is* m. | f. | n. |
    |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
    | **subject** | *ille* | *illa* | *illud* | *is* | *ea* | *id* |
    | **of** | ***illīus*** | ***illīus*** | ***illīus*** | ***eius*** | ***eius*** | ***eius*** |
    | **to/for** | ***illī*** | ***illī*** | ***illī*** | ***eī*** | ***eī*** | ***eī*** |
    | **object** | *illum* | *illam* | *illud* | *eum* | *eam* | *id* |
    | **by/with/in** | *illō* | *illā* | *illō* | *eō* | *eā* | *eō* |
    | **pl. subject** | *illī* | *illae* | *illa* | *eī, iī* | *eae* | *ea* |
    | **pl. of** | *illōrum* | *illārum* | *illōrum* | *eōrum* | *eārum* | *eōrum* |
    | **pl. to/for, by/with** | *illīs* | *illīs* | *illīs* | *eīs, iīs* | *eīs, iīs* | *eīs, iīs* |
    | **pl. object** | *illōs* | *illās* | *illa* | *eōs* | *eās* | *ea* |

    *Iste* goes exactly like *ille*.

## 7. *Īdem, eadem, idem*

:: Idem velle atque idem nōlle, ea dēmum firma amīcitia est. || To want the same things and not want the same things: that, in the end, is firm friendship. (Sallust, *Catiline* 20.4)

Eundem cāseum volō. Eandem pilam volō. Idem mālum volō!

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Read the blurb. *Eundem, eandem, idem*: which old word is inside? | ***Is, ea, id*** with ***-dem*** ("same") stuck on: "the same cheese, the same ball, the same apple." |
| The subject forms are ***īdem, eadem, idem***. Why is the masculine *īdem* long and the neuter *idem* short? | *Is* + *-dem*: the *s* drops out and the *i* stretches to make up for it: ***īdem***. *Id* + *-dem*: one *d* is lost: ***idem***, short. The macron tells masculine from neuter. |
| *Eum* + *-dem* = *eundem*. Why the *n*? | Same trick as *hunc*: an *m* turns to *n* before *d*. *Eundem, eandem*, and plural *eōrundem, eārundem*. |
| "The same girl is here." "With the same boy." | *Eadem puella hīc est. Cum eōdem puerō.* |
| The motto, *Idem velle atque idem nōlle, ea dēmum firma amīcitia est.* (*Atque*: and; *dēmum*: at last, in the end; *firma*: firm; *amīcitia*: friendship.) Find Chapter 18's verbs. | ***Velle*** and ***nōlle***: "to want **the same** (*idem*, neuter, "the same thing") and not want the same." And ***ea***, feminine, points at *amīcitia*: "**that** is firm friendship." The rebel Catiline, in Sallust, to his fellow plotters. |
| Quīntus asks for cookies again. Cornēlia: *Semper eadem vīs!* Which *eadem*? | Neuter plural object: "You always want **the same things**!" |
| Quīntus: *Īdem semper sum!* | "I'm always **the same**!" Masculine subject: long ***ī***. |

## 8. *Hunc an illum?*

:: Hīc Rhodus, hīc saltā! || Here is Rhodes: jump here! (proverb, from Aesop's fable of the boaster)

Hunc vīs an illum? Nōn istum, illum!

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "This book is mine; that one is yours." | *Hic liber meus est, ille tuus.* |
| *Hic mālum bonum est.* What's wrong? | *Mālum* is neuter: ***Hoc*** *mālum bonum est.* |
| *Illum mālum volō.* What's wrong? | The neuter object is ***illud***: *Illud mālum volō.* |
| *Huius puerō librum dō.* ("I give the book to this boy.") What's wrong? | *Huius* is "of this." "To this" is ***huic***: *Huic puerō librum dō.* |
| *Hic puer hic est.* ("This boy is here.") What's wrong? | The second one is the place word, long ī: *Hic puer* ***hīc*** *est.* |
| "I see Marcus and give him the cheese." | *Marcum videō et eī cāseum dō.* |
| "Do you want the same cheese?" | *Eundemne cāseum vīs?* The *-ne* sticks to the first word. |
| The motto, *Hīc Rhodus, hīc saltā!* (*Saltō, saltāre*: jump.) Which *hic*? | Both are ***hīc***, "here." In Aesop's fable, a boaster claims he once made a huge jump on the island of Rhodes. Someone answers: "Here is Rhodes. Jump here!" Prove it now. |

## 9. Lectiō: *Hunc an illum?*

:: Hoc volō, sīc iubeō. || This I want; so I order. (Juvenal, *Satires* 6.223)

Domī sunt. Avia cāseum quaerit. Ubi est ille cāseus?

The morning after Rome, at home, then at the market in town, then home again. Grandma stayed home yesterday, so she never got any of the big cheese. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. New words: *sīc*, "so, like this," and *iubeō, iubēre*, "order"; *vidēbimus* is just *videō* in the future, "we'll see."

::: dialogue lectio

Iōannēs | Eum nōn habēmus, Avia. || John | We don't have it, Grandma.
Avia | Quid? Ubi est? || Grandma | What? Where is it?
Marcus | In stomachō Avī est! || Marcus | It's in Grandpa's stomach!
Avia | Ave! Iste stomachus tuus! || Grandma | Grandpa! That stomach of yours!
Avus | Hic stomachus meus cāseum amat! || Grandpa | This stomach of mine loves cheese!
Avia | Hodiē in forum ībō. Cāseum meum emam! || Grandma | Today I'll go to the market. I'll buy my own cheese!
Cornēlia | Tēcum venīmus, Avia! || Cornēlia | We're coming with you, Grandma!
Quīntus | Et crustula emēmus? || Quīntus | And will we buy cookies?
Avia | Vidēbimus. || Grandma | We'll see.
Mercātor | Salvēte! Quid vultis? || Merchant | Hello, all of you! What do y'all want?
Avia | Cāseum volō. || Grandma | I want a cheese.
Mercātor | Hunc cāseum an illum vīs? || Merchant | Do you want this cheese or that one?
Avia | Quid est hic? || Grandma | What's this one?
Mercātor | Hic cāseus novus est et albus. Ille antīquus et flāvus. || Merchant | This cheese is new and white. That one's old and yellow.
Avia | Illum! Antīquus cāseus optimus est. || Grandma | That one! Old cheese is the best.
Iōannēs | Illum, Avia? Ille parvus est. Hic magnus est! || John | That one, Grandma? That one's small. This one's big!
Avia | Nōn hunc, Iōannēs. Illum volō. Hoc volō, sīc iubeō! || Grandma | Not this one, John. I want that one. This I want; so I order!
Mercātor | Ecce ille cāseus. || Merchant | Here's that cheese.
Marcus | Mercātor, quid est istud? || Marcus | Sir, what's that thing by you?
Mercātor | Hoc? Hoc est mel. || Merchant | This? This is honey.
Quīntus | Et haec? Haec sunt crustula! Haec crustula volō! || Quīntus | And these? These are cookies! I want these cookies!
Cornēlia | Semper eadem vīs, Quīnte! || Cornēlia | You always want the same things, Quīntus!
Quīntus | Īdem semper sum! || Quīntus | I'm always the same!
Avia | Bene. Haec crustula tibi emō. Et huic puellae mel. || Grandma | All right. I'm buying these cookies for you. And honey for this girl.
Marcus | Et mihi, Avia? || Marcus | And for me, Grandma?
Avia | Tibi hoc mālum. || Grandma | For you, this apple.
Marcus | Mālum? Ēheu! || Marcus | An apple? Oh, no!
Avus | Salvēte! Quid est illud? Estne cāseus? || Grandpa | Hello, all of you! What's that? Is it a cheese?
Avia | Est. Sed nōn tuus est. Hic cāseus meus est! || Grandma | It is. But it's not yours. This cheese is mine!
Avus | Sed eundem cāseum ego quoque amō… || Grandpa | But I love the same cheese too…
Iōannēs | Nōn eundem, Ave. Ille cāseus in stomachō tuō est! || John | Not the same one, Grandpa. That cheese is in your stomach!
Avus | Ita… Sed huius cāseī parvam partem volō… || Grandpa | True… But I want a little piece of this cheese…
Avia | Nōlī, Ave! Hunc cāseum nunc edō! || Grandma | Don't, Grandpa! I'm eating this cheese now!
Marcus | Ecce! Avia eum edit! || Marcus | Look! Grandma's eating it!
Cornēlia | Hodiē cāseus est in stomachō… || Cornēlia | Today the cheese is in the stomach of…
Omnēs | …Aviae! || Everyone | …Grandma!
Avus | Ēheu! Nōn īdem stomachus… et hic stomachus meus vacuus est! || Grandpa | Oh, no! Not the same stomach… and this stomach of mine is empty!
:::

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Ubi est ille cāseus magnus?* Why *ille*, not *hic*? | The cheese isn't here. Grandma points far away, to the famous cheese everyone talked about. *Ille* is "that one, over there (or back then)." |
| *Eum nōn habēmus.* What is *eum*? | "Him," pointing back at *cāseus* (masculine), so English says "it." The plain pointer. |
| *Iste stomachus tuus!* Tone? | Scornful: *iste* + *tuus*, "that stomach of yours!" Cicero's *furor iste tuus* in a kitchen. |
| *Marcus: Quid est istud? · Mercātor: Hoc?* Why two different words for the same thing? | It's by the merchant. Marcus points at it near the merchant: ***istud***. The merchant, holding it: ***hoc***. Near you vs near me. |
| *Hoc est mel* · *Haec sunt crustula.* | One thing, neuter singular: *hoc est*. Many things, neuter plural: *haec sunt*. |
| *Et huic puellae mel.* What's left out? | *Emō*: "And (I'm buying) honey for this girl." *Huic*: dative, for all genders. |
| *Huius cāseī parvam partem volō.* | "I want a small part **of this** cheese." *Huius* is the genitive: of this. (*Pars, partis*: Chapter 15.) |
| *Nōn eundem, Ave.* · *Nōn īdem stomachus!* | *Eundem*: object, "not the same (cheese)." *Īdem*: masculine subject, long ī. The cheese story repeats, but this time the stomach is Grandma's, and Grandpa's is *vacuus* (Chapter 3). |

## 10. Sound check

:: Hīc et nunc. || Here and now.

Audī et dīc.

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *hic · haec · hoc · hīc · hīs*? | HIK · HIKE · HOK · HEEK · HEES. Classical *ae* is "eye"; church Latin says HECK for *haec*. |
| *huius · huic · eius · cuius*? | HOO-yus · HOO-ik (one beat) · EH-yus · KOO-yus. The *i* between vowels is a *y* sound, and Romans doubled it: really hooy-yus, ey-yus. |
| *ille · illa · illud · illīus · illī*? | IL-le · IL-la · IL-lud · il-LEE-us · IL-lee. Hold the double *l*. In *illīus* the long ī takes the stress (emphasis). |
| *iste · istīus · is · ea · id · eum · eōrum*? | IS-te · is-TEE-us · IS · E-a · ID · E-um · e-OH-rum. |
| *īdem · idem · eadem · eundem · eōdem*? | EE-dem · I-dem · E-a-dem · e-UN-dem · e-OH-dem. |
| *dēmōnstrāre · obsecrō · disturbāre · iubeō*? | day-mohn-STRAH-re · OB-se-kroh · dis-tur-BAH-re · YU-be-oh. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking.

1. Chant *hic, haec, hoc* across, case by case: *hic, haec, hoc · huius, huius, huius · huic, huic, huic · hunc, hanc, hoc · hōc, hāc, hōc*. Then the plural.
2. Do the same for *ille, illa, illud* and *is, ea, id*.
3. Point at three things in your room and say *Hic…, haec…, hoc…* with the right noun. Then point at three things across the room: *Ille…, illa…, illud…*
4. Play shopkeeper: *Hunc cāseum an illum vīs? · Illum! · Nōn istum, illum!*
5. Answer about someone you can't see: *Ubi est Marcus? Eum nōn videō. · Ubi est Cornēlia? Eam nōn videō.*
6. Old lines, cracked open: *Hoc est corpus meum · In hōc signō vincēs · Docēbat eōs · Dabit deus hīs quoque fīnem · Idem velle atque idem nōlle.*
7. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part (Grandma gets the last laugh). Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
