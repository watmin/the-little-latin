# Caput XXII: Ūnus, Duo, Trēs

*Liber II, Rēs Cotīdiānae · Chapter 22*

*In which you finally count out loud. Chapter 15's* partēs trēs *and Chapter 20's* September…December *were waiting for numbers; Chapter 21's* duo nostrum *was waiting for* duo*. Today* ūnus *borrows the genitive and dative surprise from Chapter 19's pointing words, you meet* duo *and* trēs*, you ask* quantī? *at the cheese stall, and you tell the hour. How many cheeses? For how much? And whose stomach gets them?*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Ūnus cāseus.* · *Duo nostrum.* · *Partēs trēs.* · *Quantī est?* · *Duōbus dēnāriīs.* · *Hōrā tertiā.* · *Ūna hirundō nōn facit vēr.* · *Pecūnia nōn olet.* · *Carpe diem.*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** numeral (a counting word: one, two, three…); partitive genitive ("of" after a number, part, or "how many" — a part of the whole); ablative of time (the when-case without a preposition: *at the third hour*)
    - **Forms:** *ūnus, ūna, ūnum* (*ūnīus, ūnī*); *duo, duae, duo*; *trēs, tria*; *quattuor…decem*; *centum*; *mille / mīlia*; *prīmus, secundus, tertius…*; *quantī?*; coins *as, sēstertius, dēnārius*; *hōra*; months; *Kalendae, Nōnae, Īdūs* (chunks)
    - **Laws:** the Law of *Ūnus* (names the *ūnus nauta* set); the Law of the Part; the Law of When
    - **Words:** *ūnus, duo, trēs, quattuor, quīnque, sex, septem, octō, novem, decem, centum, mille, quot, quantī, hōra, mēnsis, annus, as, sēstertius, dēnārius, cōnstō, vendō, prīmus, secundus, tertius*
    - **Reading:** *Quantī cāseus?* (how many cheeses, for how much, at what hour)
    - **Speaking:** count 1–10; ask *quot?* and *quantī?*; name a price and an hour

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep Chapter 19's Law of the Pointing Words (*-īus / -ī*), Chapter 21's *duo nostrum*, Chapter 20's *-ber* months, and Chapter 10's *Annō Dominī* within reach. Numbers meet money and the clock today.

## 1. *Ūnus* — and the pointing-word surprise

:: Ūna hirundō nōn facit vēr. || One swallow does not make a spring. (proverb)

Ūnus cāseus. Ūna mēnsa. Ūnum mālum. Ūnīus puerī. Ūnī puellae.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 19's Law of the Pointing Words: genitive singular ***-īus***, dative singular ***-ī***, all genders (*illīus, illī*; *ipsīus, ipsī*). A few ordinary adjectives borrow the same surprise. The first is ***ūnus, ūna, ūnum***, "one." | Subject forms look like *magnus*: *ūnus puer, ūna puella, ūnum mālum*. The surprise hides in "of" and "to/for." |
| Guess "of one boy," "to/for one girl," from *illīus / illī*. | ***Ūnīus*** *puerī*. ***Ūnī*** *puellae*. Same endings for all three genders: *ūnīus / ūnī*. |
| Chant *ūnus* in the singular: subject, of, to/for, object, by/with/from. | *Ūnus, ūnīus, ūnī, ūnum, ūnō* (m.). *Ūna, ūnīus, ūnī, ūnam, ūnā* (f.). *Ūnum, ūnīus, ūnī, ūnum, ūnō* (n.). Object neuter = subject neuter (Neuter Twins). |
| "One cheese is on the table." "I see one cheese." "The price of one cheese…" | *Ūnus cāseus in mēnsā est. Ūnum cāseum videō. Pretium ūnīus cāseī…* (*Pretium*, n.: price — gloss; fuller money in §5.) |
| "I give a cheese to one boy." | *Ūnī puerō cāseum dō.* Dative *ūnī*. |
| The motto, *Ūna hirundō nōn facit vēr.* (*Hirundō, hirundinis*, f.: swallow; *vēr, vēris*, n.: spring.) | "One swallow does not make a spring." *Ūna* agrees with *hirundō* (feminine). Don't trust a single warm day. |
| Grammar name? | ***Ūnus* is a numeral**: a counting word. Old-school: "cardinal numeral" (how many), vs "ordinal" (which one in line: first, second… — §4). |
| Two more from the *ūnus nauta* set, same surprise: *sōlus, sōla, sōlum* ("alone") and *tōtus, tōta, tōtum* ("whole"). Of / to? | *Sōlīus, sōlī.* *Tōtīus, tōtī.* *Sōlīus puerī liber* — "the book of the boy alone." *Tōtīus Italiae* — "of all Italy." |
| "Grandpa alone washes himself." (Chapter 21's *sē* + today's *sōlus*.) | *Avus sōlus sē lavat.* *Sōlus* stresses "alone"; it is not the reflexive. |
| Plural of *ūnus*? Rare as "ones," common as "only / alone" in set phrases: *ūnī* "only they," *ūnae*, *ūna*. | Recognition for now. Everyday "alone / only" usually prefers *sōlus* (above). |

!!! tip "Numeral"
    A **numeral** is a counting word: *one, two, three…* or *first, second, third…*. Latin: *ūnus, duo, trēs…* (how many) and *prīmus, secundus, tertius…* (which place in line).

!!! tip "The *ūnus nauta* set"
    Nine common adjectives share *ūnus*'s *-īus / -ī* surprise. School mnemonic: ***ūnus nauta*** ("one sailor") — **ū**nus, **n**ūllus, **u**llus, **s**ōlus, **n**euter, **a**lius, **u**ter, **t**ōtus, **a**lter. You already know *nūllus* from *Nūlla diēs sine līneā* (Chapter 1 / 3). *Sōlus* "alone," *tōtus* "whole," *alter* "the other (of two)" show up below. Full drills on the rest come later; today you learn the shared shape.

## 2. *Duo* and *trēs*

:: Trēs faciunt collegium. || Three make a board. (legal proverb: it takes three to form a college)

Duo puerī. Duae puellae. Duo māla. Trēs mīlitēs. Tria nōmina.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 21: *Duo nostrum hīc sunt.* What was *duo*? | "Two." Today it opens up. *Duo* is a real plural with its own endings — not *ūnus* plus a suffix. |
| Subject forms of *duo*: masculine, feminine, neuter. | ***Duo, duae, duo.*** *Duo puerī, duae puellae, duo māla.* Neuter subject = neuter object (again). |
| "Of two," "to/for two," "two" as objects, "with two." | Gen. ***duōrum / duārum / duōrum***. Dat./abl. ***duōbus / duābus / duōbus***. Acc. ***duōs / duās / duo***. Long *ō* in *duōrum, duōbus, duōs*. |
| "I see two boys." "I give cheese to two girls." "With two soldiers." | *Duōs puerōs videō. Duābus puellīs cāseum dō. Cum duōbus mīlitibus.* |
| *Trēs, tria*: "three." Subject/object masculine-feminine ***trēs***; neuter ***tria***. | *Trēs mīlitēs, trēs puellae, tria nōmina.* Of three: ***trium***. To/for and with/from: ***tribus*** (all genders). |
| Chapter 15's Caesar: *Gallia est omnis dīvīsa in partēs trēs.* Crack *trēs*. | "Into three parts": *trēs* agrees with *partēs* (feminine object plural). |
| "Three of us." Which "of us," and why? | *Trēs nostrum.* Partitive after a number → ***nostrum*** (Chapter 21), not *nostrī*. Same for *duo nostrum*. |
| The motto, *Trēs faciunt collegium.* (*Collegium*, n.: board, college, guild.) | "Three make a board." In Roman law, three people could form a *collegium*. Two were not enough. |
| Side by side: *ūnus cāseus · duo cāseī · trēs cāseī*. | One cheese, two cheeses, three cheeses. *Cāseī* is nominative plural (Chapter 2). |

!!! success "*Duo* and *trēs* (plural only)"
    | | two (m.) | two (f.) | two (n.) | three (m./f.) | three (n.) |
    |---|---|---|---|---|---|
    | **subject** | *duo* | *duae* | *duo* | *trēs* | *tria* |
    | **of** | *duōrum* | *duārum* | *duōrum* | *trium* | *trium* |
    | **to/for** | *duōbus* | *duābus* | *duōbus* | *tribus* | *tribus* |
    | **object** | *duōs* | *duās* | *duo* | *trēs* | *tria* |
    | **by/with/from** | *duōbus* | *duābus* | *duōbus* | *tribus* | *tribus* |

## 3. Four to ten, a hundred, a thousand — and the Law of the Part

:: Quot hominēs, tot sententiae. || As many people, so many opinions. (Terence, *Phormio* 454; Chapter 14 had *Quot capita, tot sententiae*)

Quattuor, quīnque, sex, septem, octō, novem, decem. Centum. Mille. Quot cāseī?

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Four through ten do **not** change ending. Chant them. | *Quattuor, quīnque, sex, septem, octō, novem, decem.* (KWAT-tu-or, KWEEN-kweh, sex, SEP-tem, OK-tō, NO-wem, DE-kem.) |
| "Four boys are here." "I see five cheeses." | *Quattuor puerī hīc sunt. Quīnque cāseōs videō.* The number stays put; the noun does the declining. |
| Chapter 9 named *Quīntus* ("fifth"). Which counting word sits under that name? | ***Quīnque***, five. *Quīntus* is the **ordinal** (fifth in line) — §4. |
| Chapter 20: why is December the twelfth month if *decem* means ten? | Old Roman year began in March, so *September…December* were months 7–10. *Decem* paid off. |
| *Centum*: one hundred. Indeclinable, like *decem*. | *Centum mīlitēs*, "a hundred soldiers." |
| *Mille*: a thousand. As an adjective with a singular noun idea, it stays *mille*: *mille mīlitēs*, "a thousand soldiers." | For "thousands," Latin uses the noun ***mīlia*** (neuter plural) + a genitive: *duo mīlia mīlitum*, "two thousand soldiers" (literally "two thousands of soldiers"). |
| *Quot?* means "how many?" Chapter 14's motto used it. Answer with a number. | *Quot cāseī sunt? Trēs.* · *Quot puerī? Duo nostrum.* |
| After a number, a part, or *quot / multī / paucī / nihil / satis*, Latin often puts the whole in the **genitive**: *duo nostrum*, *pars cāseī*, *nihil novī*, *satis pecūniae*. | "Two **of us**," "a part **of the cheese**," "nothing **of new**," "enough **of money**." That's the **partitive genitive** — the "of" of the part. |
| "Many of the Romans." "A part of the cheese." "Enough money." | *Multī Rōmānōrum. Pars cāseī. Satis pecūniae.* Chapter 1's *satis* ("enough") now takes a genitive partner: enough **of** money. |
| The motto, *Quot hominēs, tot sententiae.* (*Tot*: so many.) | "As many people, so many opinions." *Quot… tot…* = "as many… so many…." Chapter 14's twin used *capita*. |

!!! warning "Law of the Part"
    After a **number**, a **part-word** (*pars*, *nihil*, *satis*, *multī*, *paucī*), or ***quot***, the whole often stands in the **genitive**: *duo **nostrum***, *pars **cāseī***, *satis **pecūniae***. English "of" after "some / enough / how many" is the same job. Chapter 21's *nostrum* after *duo* was already this Law in miniature.

## 4. First, second, third — and the months

:: Prīmus venit, prīmus edit. || First comes, first eats. (teaching line — useful at a cheese stall)

Prīmus, secundus, tertius. Mēnsis September. Annus novus.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| **Ordinals** say which place in line: first, second, third… They decline like *magnus, magna, magnum*. | ***Prīmus, prīma, prīmum*** (first). ***Secundus, secunda, secundum*** (second). ***Tertius, tertia, tertium*** (third). |
| "The first boy." "The second hour." "The third name." | *Puer prīmus. Hōra secunda. Nōmen tertium.* (Hour in §6.) |
| Keep going lightly: fourth–tenth. | *Quārtus, quīntus, sextus, septimus, octāvus, nōnus, decimus.* *Quīntus* is both a name and "fifth." |
| Months are often adjectives (Chapter 20's *ācer*-type for *-ber*). Name them, March through December, with the number under each late one. | *Mārtius, Aprīlis, Maius, Iūnius, Iūlius, Augustus, September (7), Octōber (8), November (9), December (10).* Old year started in March. |
| January and February? | *Iānuārius* (from *Iānus*, the two-faced god of doors) and *Februārius* (from *februa*, a purification feast). They were added at the front later. |
| *Mēnsis, mēnsis* (m.): month (Chapter 20). *Annus, annī* (m.): year (Chapter 10's *Annō Dominī*). | *Mēnsis September. Annus novus.* *Annō Dominī*: "in the year of the Lord" — ablative of time (§6) already in your mouth. |
| Roman month-markers, as chunks for now (full dating → later): ***Kalendae*** (1st of the month), ***Nōnae*** (the 5th, or the 7th in March/May/July/October), ***Īdūs*** (the 13th, or the 15th in those same four). | All three are feminine plural. *Kalendīs Mārtīīs*, "on the Kalends of March" (ablative of time). Recognition: don't decline them yet beyond noticing the ablative plural *-īs*. |
| The motto, *Prīmus venit, prīmus edit.* | "First comes, first eats." Ordinals + present verbs you know — and a warning when Quīntus reaches the cheese first. |

## 5. *Quantī?* — money and price

:: Pecūnia nōn olet. || Money doesn't smell. (Vespasian, as Suetonius tells it, *Vespasian* 23)

Quantī est? Duōbus dēnāriīs. Ūnō asse. Centum sēstertiīs.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| You've had *pecūnia* (money) since Chapter 4 and *emō, emere* (buy) since Chapter 6. New question: ***Quantī?*** — "for how much?" | *Quantī est cāseus? Quantī hunc cāseum emis?* Genitive singular of *quantus* ("how great"), used for **indefinite price**. |
| Answer with a **definite** price: Latin usually puts the coin-amount in the **ablative**. | *Duōbus dēnāriīs* — "(for) two denarii." *Ūnō asse* — "for one as." *Centum sēstertiīs* — "for a hundred sesterces." |
| Three coins to know: | ***As, assis*** (m.): the basic bronze coin. ***Sēstertius, sēstertiī*** (m.): a common unit of account (abbreviated HS). ***Dēnārius, dēnāriī*** (m.): a silver coin (originally "ten-as" piece; later worth four sesterces). |
| "The cheese costs two denarii." Two common shapes. | *Cāseus duōbus dēnāriīs cōnstat.* (*Cōnstō, cōnstāre*: cost, stand at.) Or simply *Duōbus dēnāriīs est.* / *Duōbus dēnāriīs eum emō.* |
| *Vendō, vendere*: sell (Chapter 4's *vendit* in the Lectiō). "He sells the cheese for one denarius." | *Cāseum ūnō dēnāriō vendit.* |
| Indefinite value stays in the genitive: *magnī*, *parvī*, *nihilī* — "at a high price," "cheap," "at nothing." | *Magnī aestimō* (I value highly — recognition). Everyday shopping: ask *quantī?*, answer with the ablative of the coins. |
| Schoolbook ratio (values shifted across centuries): one *dēnārius* ≈ four *sēstertiī*; the *as* is the small bronze unit under them. | So *sēstertiīs quattuor* and *ūnō dēnāriō* name the same price — handy when a merchant "drops" his ask. |
| The motto, *Pecūnia nōn olet.* | "Money doesn't smell." Vespasian taxed urine (used in tanning); when his son complained, he held up a coin and asked if it smelled. |
| "How much is this cheese? Two asses. I'll buy it." | *Quantī est hic cāseus? Duōbus assibus. Eum emō.* |

!!! tip "Price in two cases"
    Ask *quantī?* (genitive) for "how much?" Name a **definite** price in the **ablative** (*duōbus dēnāriīs*). Indefinite "dear / cheap" can stay genitive (*magnī / parvī*). Same idea as English "buy it **for** two dollars" vs "worth **a lot**."

## 6. *Hōrā tertiā* — the Law of When

:: Carpe diem. || Seize the day. (Horace, *Odes* 1.11.8)

Quā hōrā est? Hōrā tertiā. Nocte. Aestāte. Annō Dominī.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| ***Hōra, hōrae*** (f.): hour. Romans divided daylight into twelve *hōrae* (so a summer hour was longer than a winter one). | *Quā hōrā est?* "At what hour is it?" Answer: ***Hōrā tertiā*** — "at the third hour." |
| Why ablative? | **Time when** (at what point in time) takes the ablative, usually **without** a preposition: *hōrā tertiā, nocte, aestāte, annō Dominī*. |
| "At night." "In summer." "In the first hour." | *Nocte. Aestāte. Hōrā prīmā.* (*Nox, noctis*, f.: night — Chapter 15; *aestās, aestātis*, f.: summer — new.) |
| Chapter 16's Law of the Clock said the **verb** carries the clock (*cantābam / cantābō*). Today's Law says how Latin names the **time-word**. | Verb = tense. Ablative noun = "at / in / on [that time]." |
| *Diēs* (day) you've seen in *Nūlla diēs sine līneā* and *Carpe diem*. Full family (fifth declension) waits for Chapter 23; treat *diēs / diem* as chunks for now. | *Carpe diem* = "pluck / seize the day." *Diē* (ablative) = "on the day…" recognition. |
| "We will come at the second hour." "He sleeps at night." | *Hōrā secundā veniēmus. Nocte dormit.* |
| Duration ("for many years") often uses the **accusative** (*multōs annōs*). Contrast only: today's focus is **when**, not **how long**. | *Multōs annōs vīvēbat* — recognition; fuller treatment later (Book IV). |
| Chapter 10's *Annō Dominī* cracked as grammar: which Law? | Ablative of time when: "**in** the year of the Lord." Bare ablative *annō* — no *in* needed. Today's Law was already in your mouth. |
| The motto, *Carpe diem.* (*Carpō, carpere*: pluck, harvest.) | Horace to Leuconoë: don't ask the future; harvest today. |

!!! warning "Law of When"
    **When?** → ablative, usually bare: *hōrā tertiā, nocte, aestāte, Kalendīs Mārtīīs, annō Dominī*. Chapter 16's Law of the Clock still governs the **verb**. This Law governs the **time noun**. (How long? Often accusative — later.)

## 7. Mix and catch

:: Numerō, ergō sum. || I count, therefore I am. (play on *Cōgitō, ergō sum*; teaching joke)

Ūnus, duo, trēs… Quantī? Quā hōrā?

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|

| *Hōrā prīmā · nocte · aestāte · annō Dominī · multōs annōs.* | First four: time when (abl.). Last: duration (acc.) — different job. |
| Chant 1–10, then "first, second, third." | *Ūnus, duo, trēs, quattuor, quīnque, sex, septem, octō, novem, decem.* · *Prīmus, secundus, tertius.* |
| *September, Octōber, November, December* — number under each? | 7, 8, 9, 10 (*septem, octō, novem, decem*). |
| *Ūnus nauta* set: name three besides *ūnus* that share *-īus / -ī*. | e.g. *nūllus, sōlus, tōtus* (also *ūllus, alius, alter, uter, neuter*). |

## 8. Lectiō: *Quantī cāseus?*

:: Quantī cāseus? Quot cāseī? || How much for the cheese? How many cheeses?

Māne. Familia ad tabernam it. Mercātor cāseōs vendit.

::: dialogue lectio
Mercātor | Salvēte! Quid vultis? || Merchant | Hello, y'all! What do you want?
Avia | Cāseum emere volumus. Quot cāseōs habēs? || Grandma | We want to buy cheese. How many cheeses do you have?
Mercātor | Decem habeō. Magnōs et parvōs. || Merchant | I have ten. Big ones and small ones.
Quīntus | Ego duōs parvōs volō! || Quīntus | I want two small ones!
Marcus | Et ego ūnum magnum. || Marcus | And I want one big one.
Cornēlia | Nōs trēs cāseōs emimus? || Cornēlia | Are we buying three cheeses?
Iōannēs | Quattuor! Avō quoque ūnum. || John | Four! One for Grandpa too.
Avia | Quantī est ille cāseus magnus? || Grandma | How much is that big cheese?
Mercātor | Duōbus dēnāriīs. Optimus est! || Merchant | Two denarii. It's the best!
Avus | Duōbus dēnāriīs? Nimis est! Ūnō dēnāriō? || Grandpa | Two denarii? Too much! For one denarius?
Mercātor | Sēstertiīs quattuor. Nōn ūnō asse! || Merchant | For four sesterces. Not for one as!
Avia | Bene. Duōs magnōs et duōs parvōs emimus. Quantī omnēs? || Grandma | All right. We're buying two big and two small. How much for all of them?
Mercātor | Quīnque dēnāriīs. || Merchant | Five denarii.
Marcus | Quā hōrā est? || Marcus | What hour is it?
Mercātor | Hōrā tertiā. Etiam māne est. || Merchant | The third hour. It's still morning.
Quīntus | Ego iam cāseum edere volō! Ūnum parvum nunc! || Quīntus | I already want to eat cheese! One small one now!
Avia | Nōlī, Quīnte! Domī edēmus. || Grandma | Don't, Quīntus! We'll eat at home.
Avus | Ego ūnum cāseum mēcum portō… || Grandpa | I'm carrying one cheese with me…
Avia | Ūnum tuum, Ave — an nostrum? || Grandma | Your one, Grandpa — or ours?
Avus | Meum! … id est, nostrum. Pars cāseī mihi erit. || Grandpa | Mine! … that is, ours. A part of the cheese will be for me.
Cāseus | Bau! || Cheese (the dog) | Woof!
Marcus | Etiam canis cāseum vult! Duo nostrum iam edimus… || Marcus | The dog wants cheese too! Two of us are already eating…
Iōannēs | Minimē! Domum īmus. Hōrā quārtā domī erimus. || John | No way! We're going home. At the fourth hour we'll be home.
Avus | Et cāseī? Ubi erunt? || Grandpa | And the cheeses? Where will they be?
Omnēs | In stomachō… || Everyone | In the stomach of…
Avus | …Avī! Quattuor cāseī, ūnus stomachus! || Grandpa | …Grandpa! Four cheeses, one stomach!
Avia | Pecūnia nōn olet, Ave. Sed cāseus in stomachō tuō… || Grandma | Money doesn't smell, Grandpa. But cheese in your stomach…
Avus | …olet! Ēheu! Carpe diem — et carpe cāseum! || Grandpa | …does smell! Oh no! Seize the day — and seize the cheese!
:::

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Quot cāseōs habēs? · Decem habeō.* | How many / ten: *quot* + number. |
| *Ego duōs parvōs volō · ūnum magnum · trēs cāseōs · Quattuor!* | Counting order: 2, 1, 3, then John's 4 (including Grandpa's). |
| *Quantī est ille cāseus magnus? · Duōbus dēnāriīs.* | Price question in genitive; definite answer in ablative. |
| *Nimis est! Ūnō dēnāriō? · Sēstertiīs quattuor.* | Haggling in the ablative of price. (*Nimis*: too much — Chapter 18.) |
| *Quā hōrā est? · Hōrā tertiā. · Hōrā quārtā domī erimus.* | Law of When: bare ablative for the hour. |
| *Pars cāseī mihi erit. · Duo nostrum iam edimus.* | Partitive genitives: part of the cheese; two of us. |
| *Ūnum tuum… an nostrum?* | One cheese — yours or ours? Possession meets counting. |
| *Sēstertiīs quattuor* after *Ūnō dēnāriō?* — same price or a new one? | Same: schoolbook *dēnārius* ≈ four *sēstertiī*. The merchant accepts Grandpa's counter, just renamed. |
| *Pecūnia nōn olet… Sed cāseus in stomachō tuō… olet!* What is *olet*? | "Smells" — same verb as the Vespasian motto (*oleō, olēre*: smell). Money doesn't; Grandpa's cheese does. |
| End: *Quattuor cāseī, ūnus stomachus!* + *Carpe diem — et carpe cāseum!* | Four cheeses, one stomach; Horace twisted into a cheese joke. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking.

1. Chant 1–10: *ūnus, duo, trēs, quattuor, quīnque, sex, septem, octō, novem, decem*.
2. Chant *ūnus* singular (m.): *ūnus, ūnīus, ūnī, ūnum, ūnō*. Circle *-īus / -ī*.
3. Chant *duo* and *trēs* from the success box.
4. Prices out loud: *Quantī est? · Duōbus dēnāriīs · Ūnō asse · Quīnque sēstertiīs*.
5. Hours: *Quā hōrā est? · Hōrā prīmā · Hōrā secundā · Hōrā tertiā · Nocte · Aestāte*.
6. Partitives: *duo nostrum · trēs nostrum · pars cāseī · satis pecūniae · quot puerōrum?*
7. Old lines: *Ūna hirundō nōn facit vēr · Trēs faciunt collegium · Quot hominēs, tot sententiae · Pecūnia nōn olet · Carpe diem · Annō Dominī*.
8. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part. Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
