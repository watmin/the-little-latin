# Caput XIII: Rēx et Mīles

*Liber II, Rēs Cotīdiānae · Chapter 13*

*In which the third family of nouns finally gets all its forms. Father, Mother, brother, sister, and your own name have been stuck in one form since Chapter 9; today they learn to be owned, given to, seen, and walked with. The trick is one little word, the "of" form, which shows you what the naming form hides. Then the kids play King and Soldiers in the garden, Grandpa wears the crown, and the king's cheese is guarded by the two least trustworthy soldiers in Rome.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Rēx sum!* · *Iōannis sunt!* · *Patrī cāseum dō.* · *Cum patre ambulō.* · *Gladiī mīlitum.*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** stem (the part the endings hang on); third declension (the third family); consonant stem
    - **Forms:** one: *-is* (of), *-ī* (to/for), *-em* (object), *-e* (with/from); many: *-ēs* (subject and object), *-um* (of), *-ibus* (to/for and with/from); all six cases of *rēx, mīles, pater, soror*, and of your own name
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Stem, the Law of Double Duty
    - **Words:** people and roles: king, soldier, leader, judge, merchant, guard, thief, wife, woman, person; the whole family; peace and voice
    - **Reading:** *Rēx et Mīlitēs* (a garden game, a crown, and a royal cheese)
    - **Speaking:** say your family and your name in every job

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. This time, say every new noun as a **pair**, naming form and then "of" form: *rēx, rēgis*. The pair is the key to everything else.

## 1. *Rēx, rēgis*

:: Fīlius hominis. || The Son of Man. (the Gospels)

Ecce rēx. Ecce corōna rēgis. Ecce mīles. Ecce gladius mīlitis.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Marcus puts on a paper crown: *Rēx sum!* What is he? | A king. *Rēx*, RAYKS: Chapter 1's x is "ks." |
| Quīntus: *Ubi est corōna rēgis?* (*Corōna*, co-ROH-na, a crown.) Whose crown? | The king's. *Rēgis*: "of the king." A genitive (Chapter 10), but not *-ae* or *-ī*. A new ending: **-is**, short i. |
| Take *-is* off *rēgis*. What's left? | ***Rēg-***. That's the noun's **stem**: the part the endings hang on. And *rēx*? Just *rēg* + *s*, with g and s squeezed into x. |
| *Ecce gladius mīlitis.* *Mīles* (MEE-les) is a soldier. Whose sword? The stem? | The soldier's. Stem ***mīlit-***. Could you have guessed *mīlit-* from *mīles*? No: the naming form hides it. |
| *Pater → patris.* What happened? | The e drops: stem *patr-*, just like *magister → magistrī* (Chapter 10). |
| Then *māter* and *frāter*? | *Mātris* (of Mother), *frātris* (of a brother). Stems *mātr-*, *frātr-*. |
| *Soror → sorōris.* What changed? | The o grew long: stem *sorōr-*, so-ROH-ris. The naming form shortens it at the end. |
| In Chapter 10 your name's "of" form had to wait, so you claimed your shoes with *Meī sunt!* Now say "They're John's!" | ***Iōannis sunt!*** Stem *Iōann-*, plus *-is*. yō-AN-nis. |
| The motto, *Fīlius hominis*. Chapter 5's motto was *Homō sum*. Find the stem. | *Homō, hominis*: stem ***homin-***. "The son of the human being," "the Son of Man": what Jesus calls himself in the Gospels. |

!!! info "Grammar words, in plain English"
    **Stem**: the part of a noun that stays put while the endings change. *Puell-a, puell-ae*: stem *puell-*. In the third family the naming form often hides it, so you find it in the genitive.

    **Third declension**: the third family of nouns. It mixes all three genders and has its own endings. Its biggest group, this chapter's, has a stem ending in a consonant (*rēg-, mīlit-, patr-*): a **consonant stem**. Neuters wait for Chapter 14, and a cousin group (the "i-stems") for Chapter 15.

!!! success "The Law of the Stem"
    A third-family noun hides its stem in the naming form. The **genitive** shows it: take off *-is*, and every other ending goes on what's left.

    | naming form | genitive | stem |
    |---|---|---|
    | *rēx* | *rēgis* | *rēg-* |
    | *mīles* | *mīlitis* | *mīlit-* |
    | *pater* | *patris* | *patr-* |
    | *soror* | *sorōris* | *sorōr-* |
    | *homō* | *hominis* | *homin-* |

    That's why the dictionary gives both: *rēx, rēgis*. Learn the pair.

## 2. *Patrī cāseum dō*

:: Homō hominī lupus. || A human is a wolf to a human. (after Plautus; made famous by Thomas Hobbes)

Tibi dō, pater? Nunc patrī dō! Patrī cāseum dō. Mātrī pānem dō.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 11 left you saying it to his face: *Tibi dō, pater.* Now: *Patrī cāseum dō.* Find Father. | ***Patrī***: stem *patr-* + **-ī**, "to Father." Long ī. "I give the cheese to Father." |
| "I give the bread to Mother." | *Mātrī pānem dō.* |
| "Marcus gives the sword to the soldier." | *Marcus gladium mīlitī dat.* |
| You've met *-ī* before: *Marcī*, *hortī*. Is *mīlitī* "of the soldier"? | No. *Marcī* is "of Marcus" (second family genitive). *Mīlitī* is "to the soldier" (third family dative). Same letter, different families: the dictionary pair tells you the family, and the family tells you the job. |
| "Grandma gives John a cookie." | *Avia Iōannī crustulum dat.* yō-AN-nee. |
| A curiosity. Plautus has a man say *Mihi est Menaechmō nōmen*: "my name is Menaechmus." What did he do to the name? | Put it in the dative to match *mihi*: "to me is the name, to Menaechmus." So a Roman could also say *Nōmen mihi est Iōannī*. Both are right; Cicero mostly kept the naming form. You keep *Iōannēs*. |
| The motto, *Homō hominī lupus.* Who's who? | *Homō*, a human (subject); *hominī*, to a human (dative); *lupus*, a wolf. Plautus wrote *lupus est homō hominī*, "man is a wolf to man," when they're strangers (*Asinaria* 495). The philosopher Thomas Hobbes made it famous. |

## 3. *Rēgem videō*

:: Rēgem nōn faciunt opēs. || Riches do not make a king. (Seneca, *Thyestes* 344)

Marcus rēgem videt. Quīntus mīlitem videt. Ego patrem et mātrem videō.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 11: *Dā mihi pānem*, and the motto *Dōnā nōbīs pācem*. What do the objects end in? | ***-em***: *pān-em, pāc-em.* That's the third family's object ending. |
| "I see the king." | *Rēgem videō.* RAY-gem. |
| "We love Father and Mother." | *Patrem et mātrem amāmus.* |
| "Quīntus hears his sister." | *Quīntus sorōrem audit.* |
| *Pācem*: what's its naming form? (Think *rēg-* → *rēx*.) | *Pāx*, peace: c + s also squeeze into x. *Pāx, pācis.* |
| "I'm going to the king." (Chapter 8) | *Ad rēgem eō.* *Ad* + accusative works in every family. |
| The motto, *Rēgem nōn faciunt opēs.* Who does what to whom? | *Opēs*, riches, are the subject (a plural, section 5); *faciunt*, they make; *rēgem*, the object. "Riches don't make a king." Seneca's chorus goes on: a real king is the one who has put fear aside. |

## 4. *Cum patre*

:: Prō rēge et patriā. || For king and country. (an old soldiers' motto)

Marcus cum patre ambulat. Quīntus cum mātre ambulat. Cornēlia sine frātre ambulat.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 7: *cum amīcō*. Now "with Father." | *Cum patre.* Stem + short **-e**: PA-tre. |
| "Without Mother." | *Sine mātre.* |
| "From the king." | *Ā rēge.* (*Ā* before a consonant, Chapter 7.) |
| "Marcus walks with his sister." | *Marcus cum sorōre ambulat.* |
| *Rēge* and *Marce* both end in a short e. Same job? | No. *Marce* is calling Marcus (Chapter 9). *Rēge* follows a preposition: ablative. To call a king you just say *Rēx!*: third-family calling forms are the naming forms. |
| "With John." | *Cum Iōanne.* (Only the little pronouns glue *cum* on behind: *mēcum*.) |
| The motto, *Prō rēge et patriā.* | *Prō*, "for, on behalf of," takes the ablative: *rēge*, *patriā* (Chapter 12's *patria*, homeland). It still turns up on old coats of arms and soldiers' badges. |

## 5. *Rēgēs et mīlitēs*

:: Inter arma silent lēgēs. || In time of war, the laws fall silent. (after Cicero, *Prō Milōne* 11)

Rēx et rēgēs. Mīles et mīlitēs. Frāter et frātrēs.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Mīlitēs in hortō sunt.* How many soldiers? The ending? | More than one: stem *mīlit-* + **-ēs**. MEE-li-tays. |
| "The kings are in the forum." | *Rēgēs in forō sunt.* |
| *Marcus mīlitēs videt.* What is *mīlitēs* doing now? | It's the object: "Marcus sees the soldiers." The same **-ēs**! For many, subject and object look alike. |
| *Mīlitēs rēgēs vident.* Who sees whom? | The endings can't tell you: both *-ēs*. Read it the usual way, subject first: "The soldiers see the kings." Romans leaned on order and sense. |
| *Rēx mīlitēs videt.* Any doubt this time? | None. *Videt* is "he sees," one person. *Rēx* is one, so he's the subject; *mīlitēs* is the object. The verb's ending is a clue. |
| "Marcus and Quīntus are brothers." | *Marcus et Quīntus frātrēs sunt.* |
| Greet a room full of soldiers. | *Salvēte, mīlitēs!* Calling many is the same as the naming form (Chapter 9). |
| The motto, *Inter arma silent lēgēs.* | *Lēgēs*, laws: plural of *lēx, lēgis*, a cousin of *rēx*. *Silent*, they are silent (like *tacent*); *inter arma*, among weapons. Cicero, defending Milo in 52 BC: *silent enim lēgēs inter arma*. |

## 6. *Rēx rēgum*

:: Rēx rēgum. || King of kings. (the Book of Revelation 19:16)

Rēx rēgum. Gladiī mīlitum. Mīlitibus cāseum damus. Cum mīlitibus ambulāmus.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The motto, *Rēx rēgum.* One king over many. What's "of many"? | **-um**, short u: *rēg-um*, "of kings." Much plainer than *puellārum, puerōrum* (Chapter 10). Revelation's full title is *Rēx rēgum et Dominus dominantium*, "King of kings and Lord of lords." |
| "The soldiers' swords." | *Gladiī mīlitum.* |
| "The sisters' dolls." | *Pūpae sorōrum.* |
| *Hortum* and *rēgum* both end in *-um*. Same job? | No. *Hortum* is one garden, the object (second family). *Rēgum* is "of kings" (third family). Know the family, know the job. |
| "Grandpa gives cheese to the soldiers." | *Avus mīlitibus cāseum dat.* Many receivers: **-ibus**. mee-LI-ti-bus. |
| "We walk with the soldiers." | *Cum mīlitibus ambulāmus.* The same *-ibus*. |
| Chapter 11's *-īs* was both "to many" and "with many." The third family? | The same deal: *-ibus* does both, to/for and with/from. |
| "Mother gives cookies to the brothers." | *Māter frātribus crustula dat.* |

!!! success "Tertia dēclīnātiō: the third family (consonant stems, m. and f.)"
    **Singular**

    | case | ending | *rēx* | *mīles* | *pater* | *soror* |
    |---|---|---|---|---|---|
    | nom. (naming) | (varies) | *rēx* | *mīles* | *pater* | *soror* |
    | gen. (of, whose) | *-is* | *rēgis* | *mīlitis* | *patris* | *sorōris* |
    | dat. (to, for) | *-ī* | *rēgī* | *mīlitī* | *patrī* | *sorōrī* |
    | acc. (object; where to) | *-em* | *rēgem* | *mīlitem* | *patrem* | *sorōrem* |
    | abl. (where, from, with) | *-e* | *rēge* | *mīlite* | *patre* | *sorōre* |
    | voc. (calling) | = nom. | *rēx* | *mīles* | *pater* | *soror* |

    **Plural**

    | case | ending | *rēx* | *mīles* | *pater* | *soror* |
    |---|---|---|---|---|---|
    | nom. | *-ēs* | *rēgēs* | *mīlitēs* | *patrēs* | *sorōrēs* |
    | gen. | *-um* | *rēgum* | *mīlitum* | *patrum* | *sorōrum* |
    | dat. | *-ibus* | *rēgibus* | *mīlitibus* | *patribus* | *sorōribus* |
    | acc. | *-ēs* | *rēgēs* | *mīlitēs* | *patrēs* | *sorōrēs* |
    | abl. | *-ibus* | *rēgibus* | *mīlitibus* | *patribus* | *sorōribus* |
    | voc. | *-ēs* | *rēgēs* | *mīlitēs* | *patrēs* | *sorōrēs* |

!!! success "The Law of Double Duty"
    In the third family's plural, two endings each work two jobs:

    - ***-ēs***: subject **or** object (or calling): *Mīlitēs veniunt · Mīlitēs videō · Salvēte, mīlitēs!*
    - ***-ibus***: to/for **or** with/from: *Mīlitibus dō · Cum mīlitibus eō.*

    Sort them by their company: the verb's ending, a preposition, a giving verb. When nothing decides, the subject usually comes first.

!!! warning "Old letters, new jobs"
    - *-is* (short i) is "of one": *rēgis*. *-īs* (long ī) is the first and second families' "to/with many": *puerīs*.
    - *-ī* is "to one" here (*rēgī*), but "of one" in the second family (*Marcī*).
    - *-e* is "with/from" here (*cum rēge*), but calling in the second family (*Marce!*).
    - *-um* is "of many" here (*rēgum*), but one object in the second family (*hortum*).

## 7. *Quis est?*

:: Dux fēmina factī. || A woman was leader of the deed. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 1.364)

Quis est? Rēx, dux, mīles, iūdex, mercātor, custōs… et fūr!

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Mercātor, mercātōris*: a trader, a merchant (mer-KAH-tor). Stem? | *Mercātōr-*: the o grows long, as in *sorōr-*. |
| Chapter 6 named the command form after the *imperātor*, "the man giving orders." Hear the *-tor* in *mercātor, imperātor*? | ***-tor*** is the doer, like English -er: the one who trades, the one who commands. English kept it in actor, doctor, narrator. |
| *Fūr, fūris*: a thief (FOOR). An English cousin? | "Furtive": sneaky, like a thief. Stem *fūr-*, nothing hidden. |
| *Custōs, custōdis*: a guard. Stem? English? | *Custōd-*: d + s became plain s. Custody, custodian. |
| *Iūdex, iūdicis*: a judge. *Dux, ducis*: a leader, a general. Stems? | *Iūdic-* (the e turns i, as in *mīles, mīlit-*) and *duc-*. c + s = x again. Judicial; duke. |
| *Uxor, uxōris*: wife. *Mulier, mulieris*: woman. *Soror* and *uxor* are feminine, *mercātor* masculine. All three end in *-or*. So does the ending tell the gender? | No! In the third family the naming form doesn't give the gender away. For people, the meaning does: wife, sister, mother, woman are feminine; king, father, merchant masculine. For everything else, learn the gender with the pair. |
| *Pāx, pācis* (peace) and *vōx, vōcis* (voice). Masculine like *rēx*? | No: both feminine. Chapter 10's *Vōx populī*: "the voice of the people." Same x, different gender. Learn it with the word. |
| "The merchant gives the judge money." | *Mercātor iūdicī pecūniam dat.* (Uh-oh.) |
| The motto, *Dux fēmina factī.* | *Dux*, leader; *fēmina*, a woman; *factī*, of the deed (Chapter 11's *factum*). Vergil on Queen Dido, who led her people's escape from Tyre and went on to found Carthage. *Dux* is usually a man's word, but meaning wins. |

## 8. *Iōannēs, Iōannis*

:: Frāter, avē atque valē. || Brother, hail and farewell. (Catullus 101)

Iōannēs sum. Liber Iōannis. Dā Iōannī librum! Marcus Iōannem videt. Quīntus cum Iōanne ambulat.

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Your name, all six cases, singular. | *Iōannēs, Iōannis, Iōannī, Iōannem, Iōanne, Iōannēs.* Stem *Iōann-*. The calling form stays *Iōannēs* (Chapter 9). |
| *Iōannēs* ends in *-ēs*, like *mīlitēs*. Are there many of you? | No: that's just your naming form, kept from Greek. The verb tells: *Iōannēs venit*, he comes, one man. |
| "Marcus gives John the ball." | *Marcus Iōannī pilam dat.* |
| Chapter 9 said *pater meus*. Now, "I give the cheese to my father." (Chapter 12: *agricolae bonō*) | *Patrī meō cāseum dō.* *Meus* matches *patrī*: dative, one, masculine: *meō*. Same case, not same letters. (Every Book I adjective works this way with the third family; Chapter 20 practices it.) |
| "I love my mother." | *Mātrem meam amō.* |
| "Whose is the doll? It's my sister's." | *Cuius est pūpa? Sorōris meae est.* |
| "Grandpa is Father's father." | *Avus pater patris est.* |
| The motto, *Frāter, avē atque valē.* | *Frāter*, calling him (same as the naming form); *avē*, hail (Chapter 1); *atque*, and; *valē*, farewell. Catullus traveled across the sea to his brother's grave near Troy and ended his poem with these words. |

## 9. Lectiō: *Rēx et Mīlitēs*

:: Occāsiō fūrem facit. || Opportunity makes the thief. (proverb)

Līberī in hortō sunt. Avus rēx est.

Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. New words: *custōdīte*, "guard!" (from *custōdīre*, a *custōs*'s job); *cognōscit*, "recognizes"; *ergō*, "so" (Chapter 12's *Cōgitō, ergō sum*).

::: dialogue lectio
Quīntus | Lūdus novus: Rēx et Mīlitēs! || Quīntus | A new game: King and Soldiers!
Marcus | Ego rēx sum! Ubi est corōna rēgis? || Marcus | I'm the king! Where's the king's crown?
Cornēlia | Nōn tū, Marce. Avus rēx est. Ecce corōna! || Cornēlia | Not you, Marcus. Grandpa is the king. Here's the crown!
Avus | Ita! Rēx sum. Et ecce cāseus rēgis. || Grandpa | Yes! I'm the king. And here's the king's cheese.
Quīntus | Et nōs? || Quīntus | And us?
Avus | Vōs mīlitēs estis, Marce et Quīnte. Custōdēs cāseī estis! || Grandpa | You two are soldiers, Marcus and Quīntus. You're the guards of the cheese!
Marcus | Mīlitēs sumus! Gladiōs habēmus! || Marcus | We're soldiers! We have swords!
Cornēlia | Et ego? || Cornēlia | And me?
Avus | Tū dux es, Cornēlia: dux mīlitum! || Grandpa | You're the general, Cornēlia: the leader of the soldiers!
Iōannēs | Et ego, rēx? || John | And me, Your Majesty?
Avus | Tū iūdex es, Iōannēs: iūdex rēgis. || Grandpa | You're the judge, John: the king's judge.
Cornēlia | Mīlitēs! Cāseum rēgis custōdīte! || Cornēlia | Soldiers! Guard the king's cheese!
Avus | Nunc rēx dormit. || Grandpa | Now the king is sleeping.
Quīntus | Marce… cāseus magnus est… et rēx dormit… || Quīntus | Marcus… the cheese is big… and the king is asleep…
Marcus | Nōlī, Quīnte! Mīlitēs sumus, nōn fūrēs! || Marcus | Don't, Quīntus! We're soldiers, not thieves!
Cornēlia | Ecce! Ubi est cāseus? In mēnsā nōn est! || Cornēlia | Look! Where's the cheese? It's not on the table!
Avus | Quid? Ubi est cāseus meus? Fūr in hortō est! || Grandpa | What? Where's my cheese? There's a thief in the garden!
Marcus | Quīntus fūr est! Semper cāseum edit! || Marcus | Quīntus is the thief! He's always eating cheese!
Quīntus | Minimē! Tū fūr es! Fūr fūrem cognōscit! || Quīntus | No way! You're the thief! It takes one to know one!
Cornēlia | Iūdex! Quis est fūr? || Cornēlia | Judge! Who's the thief?
Iōannēs | Audīte, mīlitēs! Spectāte tunicam rēgis… || John | Listen, soldiers! Look at the king's tunic…
Cornēlia | In tunicā rēgis cāseus est! || Cornēlia | There's cheese on the king's tunic!
Puerī | Rēx fūr est! || The boys | The king is the thief!
Avus | Nōn fūr sum: rēx sum! Cāseus rēgis est, ergō meus est! || Grandpa | I'm not a thief: I'm the king! The cheese is the king's, so it's mine!
Iōannēs | Rēx, iūdex tuus sum. Dā cāseum mīlitibus! || John | Your Majesty, I'm your judge. Give the cheese to the soldiers!
Avus | Bene, bene. Mīlitibus meīs cāseum dō… et ducī… et iūdicī. || Grandpa | All right, all right. I give the cheese to my soldiers… and to the general… and to the judge.
Pater | Salvēte! Quid agitis, līberī? || Father | Hello! What are y'all up to, kids?
Marcus | Pater! Lūdus est: rēx, mīlitēs, dux, iūdex… et fūr! || Marcus | Dad! It's a game: a king, soldiers, a general, a judge… and a thief!
Quīntus | Et fūr est rēx! || Quīntus | And the thief is the king!
Iōannēs | Ecce, līberī: cāseum patrī vestrō dō, et mātrī vestrae. || John | Look, kids: I'm giving some cheese to your father, and to your mother.
Māter | Grātiās tibi agō, Iōannēs! Estne pāx in hortō? || Mother | Thank you, John! Is there peace in the garden?
Cornēlia | Pāx est, māter. Sed cāseus nōn est! || Cornēlia | There's peace, Mom. But there's no cheese!
Pater | Ubi est cāseus magnus? || Father | Where's the big cheese?
Iōannēs | Ubi semper: in stomachīs mīlitum… et in stomachō rēgis! || John | Where it always is: in the soldiers' stomachs… and in the king's stomach!
:::

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Ecce cāseus rēgis.* Whose cheese? | The king's: *rēgis*, "of the king." (And the king is Grandpa.) |
| *Custōdēs cāseī estis!* What form is *custōdēs*? | Plural, naming form: "you (two) are guards." *Custōs, custōdis → custōdēs.* |
| *Dux mīlitum*: one soldier or many? | Many: *-um*, "of the soldiers." |
| *Fūr fūrem cognōscit.* Which thief does the recognizing? | *Fūr*, the subject. *Fūrem* is the object. "A thief recognizes a thief": it takes one to know one. An old proverb. |
| *Mīlitibus meīs cāseum dō… et ducī… et iūdicī.* Count the receivers and their endings. | Soldiers (*-ibus*, many), the general (*ducī*), the judge (*iūdicī*): all datives. *Meīs* matches *mīlitibus*. |
| *Cāseum patrī vestrō dō.* Why *vestrō*, and why not *tuō*? | *Vestrō* matches *patrī* (dative, one, masculine). And it's "y'all's father": John is talking to all the kids (Chapter 9). |
| *In stomachīs mīlitum… et in stomachō rēgis.* | "In the soldiers' stomachs… and in the king's stomach": a genitive plural and a genitive singular. The cheese ends where it always does. |

## 10. Sound check

:: Ecce homō. || Behold the man. (John 19:5)

Audī et dīc.

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *rēx · rēgis · rēgibus*? | RAYKS · RAY-gis · RAY-gi-bus. |
| *mīles · mīlitis · mīlitibus*? | MEE-les · MEE-li-tis · mee-LI-ti-bus. The stress (emphasis) moves as the word grows. Chapter 1's rule: lean on the second-to-last syllable if it's long; if it's short, lean on the one before it. In *mīlitibus* the *ti* is short, so *li* gets it. |
| *soror · sorōris*? | SO-ror · so-ROH-ris. The long ō pulls the emphasis onto itself. |
| *rēgis · puerīs*? | RAY-gis (short i, "of the king") · PU-e-rees (long ī, "to the boys"). |
| *Iōannis · Iōannī · Iōannem*? | yō-AN-nis · yō-AN-nee · yō-AN-nem. |
| *vōx · pāx · dux*? | WOHKS · PAHKS · DUKS (short u). |
| *Ecce homō*? | EK-ke HO-moh. "Here is the man": Pilate showing Jesus to the crowd (John 19:5). Church Latin says EH-cheh. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking.

1. Say every new noun as a pair, slowly: *rēx, rēgis · mīles, mīlitis · pater, patris · soror, sorōris · homō, hominis · dux, ducis · iūdex, iūdicis · custōs, custōdis.*
2. Decline *rēx* and *mīles* through all six cases, singular and plural. Then *pater* and *soror*.
3. Hand things around your family and narrate: *Patrī cāseum dō · Mātrī pānem dō · Frātrī pilam dō · Sorōrī pūpam dō.*
4. Run your name through every job: *Iōannēs sum · liber Iōannis · Dā Iōannī! · Marcus Iōannem videt · cum Iōanne.*
5. One to many: *rēx → rēgēs · mīles → mīlitēs · frāter → frātrēs · cum rēge → cum rēgibus.*
6. Say the mottos and point at each third-family form: *Homō hominī lupus · Rēgem nōn faciunt opēs · Inter arma silent lēgēs · Rēx rēgum · Fūr fūrem cognōscit.*
7. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part. Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
