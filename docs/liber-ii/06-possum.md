# Caput XVIII: Possum!

*Liber II, Rēs Cotīdiānae · Chapter 18*

*In which you learn to say "I can," "I want," and "I don't want." The first one is an old friend in disguise: Chapter 5's* sum *with three letters stuck on the front, so Chapter 16's* eram *and* erō *give you "I could" and "I'll be able" for free. Then* volō *("I want") turns out to be a little wild,* nōlō *turns out to be the verb hiding behind Chapter 6's* nōlī*, and the second half of a rhyme from Chapter 12 finally makes sense. In Rome, the family hunts for the cheese, and Iōannēs finds out what he can and can't carry.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Possum!* · *Nōn possum.* · *Quid vīs?* · *Cāseum edere volō.* · *Nōlō!* · *Sī vīs vīvere in pāce…*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** complementary infinitive (the "to do it" form that completes *possum, volō, nōlō*)
    - **Forms:** *possum, potes, potest, possumus, potestis, possunt*; *poteram…*, *poterō…*; *posse, esse*; *volō, vīs, vult, volumus, vultis, volunt*, *velle*; *nōlō, nōn vīs, nōn vult, nōlumus, nōn vultis, nōlunt*, *nōlle*; *mālō* (to recognize)
    - **Laws:** the Law of *Pot-*, the Law of the Completing Infinitive
    - **Words:** *possum, volō, nōlō, mālō, exspectō, volō* ("fly"), *sī, simul, nimis, iam nōn, bellum*
    - **Reading:** *Cāseum emere volō!* (the forum and the cheese shop in Rome)
    - **Speaking:** say what you can and can't do, and what you want and don't want

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep two chants handy: Chapter 5's *sum, es, est, sumus, estis, sunt*, and Chapter 16's *eram…* and *erō…*. Today's first new verb is built right on top of them.

---

## 1. *Possum!*

:: Possunt, quia posse videntur. || They can, because they think they can. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 5.231)

Sum. Possum. Possum legere!

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 5's chant: "I am, you are, he is, we are, y'all are, they are." | *Sum, es, est, sumus, estis, sunt.* |
| Read the blurb: *Sum. Possum. Possum legere!* What could *possum legere* mean? | "I can read!" ***Possum***: "I can, I am able." *Legere* is the "to read" form (Chapter 6). |
| Look hard at *possum*. Which old word is hiding at the end? | ***sum***, "I am." *Possum* is "I am able": *pot-* ("able") + *sum*. Say *potsum* fast and the *t* melts into the *s*: ***possum***. (English *potent* and *potential* come from the same root.) |
| Guess "you can." Start from *es*, "you are." | ***Potes***: *pot-* + *es*. In front of a vowel, the *t* stays. |
| "He can"? "We can"? | *Potest* (*pot-* + *est*). *Possumus* (*pot-* + *sumus*, and the *t* melts again). |
| Now all six. | *Possum, potes, potest, possumus, potestis, possunt.* Under each one sits *sum, es, est, sumus, estis, sunt*. |
| When is it *pos-* and when *pot-*? | ***Pos-*** before an *s* (*possum, possumus, possunt*). ***Pot-*** before an *e* (*potes, potest, potestis*). |
| A friend asks *Potesne?*, "Can you?" Say yes the Roman way. | ***Possum!***, "I can!" Latin often says yes by repeating the verb, not with a "yes" word. And no: ***Nōn possum***. |
| "We can!" "They can't." "Can y'all?" | *Possumus! · Nōn possunt. · Potestisne?* |
| The motto, *Possunt, quia posse videntur.* (*Quia*: because; *videntur*: "they seem," a form for later.) Find "they can." | ***Possunt.*** Vergil's rowers in a boat race "can, because they seem to themselves to be able": they think they can. *Posse* is "to be able" (§3). |

## 2. *Possum legere*

:: Nēmō potest duōbus dominīs servīre. || No one can serve two masters. (Matthew 6:24)

Possum legere. Possum currere. Nōn possum dormīre!

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Possum legere.* What kind of form is *legere*? | The **infinitive**, Chapter 6's "to do it" form: "to read." No person, no number. |
| English says "I can read," with no "to." What does Latin say? | *Possum* + the infinitive: *possum legere*, literally "I am able to read." |
| "I can run." "Marcus can swim." (*Natō*, Chapter 15) | *Possum currere. Marcus natāre potest.* The infinitive often stands just before *possum*, but either order is fine. |
| "We can't sleep!" Which word tells you **who** can, and which one tells you **what**? | *Dormīre nōn possumus!* (or *Nōn possumus dormīre!*) *Possumus* carries the person ("we"). *Dormīre* carries the action, and it never changes: *possum dormīre, potes dormīre, possunt dormīre*. |
| *Possum* alone feels unfinished: "I can… what?" The infinitive fills the gap. Its grammar name? | The **complementary infinitive**: the infinitive that *completes* a verb (Latin *complēre*, "fill up"). Spelled with an *e*, like *complete*, not "compliment." |
| You've already used one. Chapter 6's *Nōlī timēre!* | ***Timēre*** completes *nōlī*: "be unwilling **to fear**." (You'll meet *nōlī*'s own verb in §5.) |
| And Chapter 6's sound-check motto, Horace's *Sapere audē*? | ***Sapere*** ("to be wise") completes *audē* ("dare!"): "Dare **to be wise**." Another complementary infinitive. |
| "I can read the book." Where does *librum* go? | *Librum legere possum.* The object belongs to the infinitive: you read the book; *possum* just says you can. |
| "Can y'all hear me?" (*Mē*, Chapter 11) | *Potestisne mē audīre?* |
| The motto, *Nēmō potest duōbus dominīs servīre.* (*Nēmō*: no one; *duōbus*: two, Chapter 22; *servīre*: to be a servant, with the dative, Chapter 11's "to/for" case.) Find the pair. | ***Potest servīre***, "can serve." "No one can serve two masters." *Dominīs* is the dative plural of *dominus*. |

## 3. *Poteram, poterō*

:: Nōn omnia possumus omnēs. || We can't all do everything. (Vergil, *Eclogues* 8.63)

Heri nōn poteram. Hodiē possum. Crās poterō!

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 16: "I was," all six. | *Eram, erās, erat, erāmus, erātis, erant.* |
| *Possum* is *pot-* + *sum*. Guess "I was able, I could." | ***Poteram***: *pot-* + *eram*. Every form starts with a vowel, so it's *pot-* all the way. |
| All six. | *Poteram, poterās, poterat, poterāmus, poterātis, poterant.* |
| Now "I will be able." Chapter 16 gave you *erō, eris, erit…* | *Poterō, poteris, poterit, poterimus, poteritis, poterunt.* |
| Is *poterō* a bō future or an *ē* future (Chapter 17)? | Neither. It's *sum*'s own future, *erō*, with *pot-* in front. Chapter 17's Law of the Two Futures named *sum* a rebel, and *possum* rebels right along with it. |
| "Yesterday I couldn't sleep." | *Heri dormīre nōn poteram.* "I wasn't able to sleep": the whole night was a scene that kept going, so the imperfect. (A one-time "I managed it" is the perfect, later.) |
| "Tomorrow we'll be able to see Rome." | *Crās Rōmam vidēre poterimus.* |
| Grandpa: "As a boy, I could always run." Start *Puer…* | *Puer semper currere poteram.* A habit in the past: the imperfect, like Chapter 16's *Avus puer saepe natābat*. |
| *Sum* has an infinitive you haven't met: "to be." It's ***esse*** (ES-se). What do you guess for "to be able"? | ***Posse***: *pot-* + *esse*, squeezed. (Early Latin sometimes still says *potesse*.) Not "*potēre*," not "*possere*." |
| The motto, *Nōn omnia possumus omnēs.* (*Omnia*: all things, everything; *omnēs*: all of us. Chapter 20's family.) No infinitive this time. What does *possumus* mean alone? | "We are able to do": *possum* can take a neuter word like *omnia* as its object. "We can't all do everything." Vergil, handing the next song to another singer, shrugs to the Muses: everyone has their own gifts. |

!!! success "The Law of *Pot-*"
    ***Possum* is *pot-* + *sum*.** Write *pos-* before an *s*, *pot-* before a vowel.

    - Present: *pos-sum, pot-es, pot-est, pos-sumus, pot-estis, pos-sunt*
    - Imperfect: *pot-eram, pot-erās…* (Chapter 16's *eram*)
    - Future: *pot-erō, pot-eris…* (Chapter 16's *erō*)
    - Infinitive: ***posse*** ("to be able"), like *esse* ("to be")

    Learn *sum* well and *possum* comes free.

## 4. *Volō*

:: Sī vīs pācem, parā bellum. || If you want peace, prepare for war. (proverb, after Vegetius)

Quid vīs? Cāseum volō! Cāseum edere volō!

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Read the blurb: *Quid vīs? Cāseum volō! Cāseum edere volō!* | "What do you want? I want cheese! I want to eat cheese!" ***Volō***: "I want." ***Vīs***: "you want." |
| *Cāseum volō* and *cāseum edere volō*. What's the difference? | *Volō* can take a plain object (*cāseum*, "I want cheese") or a complementary infinitive (*edere*, "I want **to eat**"). In the second one, *cāseum* belongs to *edere*. |
| Here's the whole chant: *volō, vīs, vult, volumus, vultis, volunt.* Which three look like a normal verb? | ***Volō, volumus, volunt***: stem *vol-* with endings you know (*-ō, -mus, -nt*). |
| And the three wild ones? | ***Vīs*** (you want), ***vult*** (he, she, it wants), ***vultis*** (y'all want). Just learn them: WEES, WULT, WUL-tis. |
| Its infinitive, "to want"? Not "*volere*." | ***Velle***, WEL-le. Like *esse* and *posse*, it skips the usual *-re*. The oldest infinitive ending was *-se*: *es-se* kept it, and *vel-se* melted into *velle*. (Between two vowels that *s* turned into *r*, the way Chapter 14's *corpos-* became *corpor-*: that's where *-re* comes from.) |
| "Do you want cheese?" "What do y'all want?" | *Vīsne cāseum? · Quid vultis?* |
| "Marcus wants to watch gladiators." (Chapter 17's *gladiātor*) | *Marcus gladiātōrēs spectāre vult.* |
| Careful! The series motto *Verba volant*: do the words want something? | No. ***Volant*** is "they fly," from a different verb: ***volō, volāre***, "fly" (the ā family, Chapter 5). "They want" is ***volunt***. Two verbs both say *volō* for "I"; the other forms tell them apart (*volat* flies, *vult* wants). |
| Chapter 12 quoted a whole rhyme but could only explain its first half: *Audī, vidē, tacē, sī vīs vīvere in pāce.* (*Sī*: if.) Now read the second half yourself. | "Hear, see, be quiet, **if you want to live in peace**." *Vīs* "you want," *vīvere* "to live" (Chapter 17), *in pāce* "in peace" (Chapter 13). |
| The motto, *Sī vīs pācem, parā bellum.* (*Parō, parāre*: prepare; *bellum*: war.) | "If you want peace, prepare for war." The Roman writer Vegetius said it longer (*quī dēsīderat pācem, praeparet bellum*); the short form is the proverb. *Pācem* is the object of *vīs*: no infinitive. |
| A man with leprosy kneels before Jesus (Matthew 8:2): *Domine, sī vīs, potes mē mundāre.* (*Mundō, mundāre*: make clean.) | "Lord, if you want to, you can make me clean." *Vīs* and *potes* side by side, and *mundāre* completes *potes*. |
| His answer (8:3) starts with one word: *Volō.* What does it mean here? | "I want to," "I will": a whole yes in one verb, the way *Possum!* was in §1. (Then *mundāre!*, "be clean!": a command form for much later that happens to look like the infinitive.) |
| *Volō* in the other tenses is tame. Guess "I was wanting" and "I will want" from Chapter 17. | ***Volēbam*** (like *legēbam*) and ***volam, volēs, volet…*** (like *legam*). Only the present is wild. |

## 5. *Nōlō* (and *mālō*)

:: Nōlī turbāre circulōs meōs! || Don't disturb my circles! (Archimedes's last words, as later tradition tells them)

Volō. Nōlō. Nōlī!

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| ***Nōlō*** is *ne* ("not") + *volō*, squeezed into one word. What does it mean? | "I don't want, I'm unwilling." NOH-loh. |
| Here's the chant: *nōlō, nōn vīs, nōn vult, nōlumus, nōn vultis, nōlunt.* Find the pattern. | Where *volō* has ***vol-***, *nōlō* has ***nōl-*** (*nōlō, nōlumus, nōlunt*). Where *volō* is wild (*vīs, vult, vultis*), *nōlō* just puts ***nōn*** in front. |
| Its infinitive? (From *velle*.) | ***Nōlle***, "to be unwilling." NOHL-le. |
| "I don't want to sleep!" | *Dormīre nōlō!* |
| "Grandpa doesn't want to wait." (*Exspectō, exspectāre*: wait, wait for.) | *Avus exspectāre nōn vult.* No "*nōvult*": the wild forms take a plain *nōn*. |
| Chapter 6 said *nōlī* literally means "be unwilling!" and promised its verb in Book II. Here it is. What is *nōlī*? | The **command** of *nōlō*: *nōlī* to one person, *nōlīte* to y'all. So Chapter 6's Law of *Nōlī* was a complementary infinitive all along: *nōlī timēre*, "be unwilling to fear." |
| Is there a command "Want!" from *volō*? | No. Romans never needed one. From this family, the everyday commands are ***nōlī, nōlīte***. |
| *Vīsne dormīre?* Answer "No!" the Roman way, then "Yes!" | ***Nōlō!*** and ***Volō!*** One verb each, like *Possum!* |
| ***Mālō*** is *magis volō*, "I want more": "I prefer, I'd rather." (*Magis*: more.) "I'd rather have cookies." (*Crustula*, Chapter 10) | *Crustula mālō.* Just learn to spot it for now: *mālō, māvīs, māvult* ("I prefer, you prefer, he prefers"); the rest waits for Chapter 32. |
| Wait: Chapter 10 had *mālō* as a form of *mālum*, "apple." Which is it? | Both! *Mālō* is also the dative or ablative of *mālum* ("to/with an apple," Chapter 11). The company decides: *Crustula mālō* has no other verb, so it's "I'd rather"; *in mālō* is "in the apple." |
| The motto, *Nōlī turbāre circulōs meōs!* (*Turbō, turbāre*: disturb; *circulus*: circle.) | "Don't disturb my circles!" The story says the old mathematician Archimedes was drawing circles in the sand when a Roman soldier burst in. *Nōlī* + *turbāre*, Chapter 6's pattern. |

!!! success "The Law of the Completing Infinitive"
    ***Possum, volō, nōlō*** (and ***mālō***) **feel unfinished. Complete them with an infinitive.**

    - The "can / want" verb carries the **person and the time**: *possum, potes, poterat, volumus, nōlunt*.
    - The infinitive carries the **action** and never changes: *legere, edere, īre, esse*.
    - The object belongs to the infinitive: *Cāseum edere volō.*
    - *Nōlī / nōlīte* + infinitive is "don't!" (Chapter 6).

!!! success "Can and want, side by side"
    | | *sum* (am) | *possum* (can) | *volō* (want) | *nōlō* (don't want) |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | I | *sum* | *possum* | *volō* | *nōlō* |
    | you | *es* | *potes* | *vīs* | *nōn vīs* |
    | he, she, it | *est* | *potest* | *vult* | *nōn vult* |
    | we | *sumus* | *possumus* | *volumus* | *nōlumus* |
    | y'all | *estis* | *potestis* | *vultis* | *nōn vultis* |
    | they | *sunt* | *possunt* | *volunt* | *nōlunt* |
    | to … | *esse* | *posse* | *velle* | *nōlle* |

## 6. *Quid vīs? Quid potes?*

:: Quae volumus, et crēdimus libenter. || What we want, we also gladly believe. (Julius Caesar, *Civil War* 2.27)

Quid vīs facere? Quid facere potes? Quid facere nōn vīs?

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "What do you want to do tomorrow?" | *Quid crās facere vīs?* |
| "I want to go to the forum, but I can't." | *In forum īre volō, sed nōn possum.* |
| "Can Grandpa run?" "He can't run, but he can sleep." | *Potestne Avus currere? Currere nōn potest, sed dormīre potest.* |
| From Matthew 20:21–22: Jesus asks a mother *Quid vīs?*, then asks her two sons *Potestis bibere calicem…?* (*Calix*: cup.) They answer with one word. Guess it. | ***Possumus***, "We can." The same one-word yes as *Possum!* |
| *Possum legō.* What's wrong? | The second verb must be the infinitive: ***Possum legere.*** |
| *Marcus cāseum vīs.* What's wrong? | *Vīs* is "you want." Marcus is "he": ***Marcus cāseum vult.*** |
| *Potunt.* ("They can.") What's wrong? | *Pot-* + *sunt*, and the *t* melts before *s*: ***possunt***. |
| *Crās venīre possum.* ("Tomorrow I'll be able to come.") What's wrong? | Chapter 16's Law of the Clock: *crās* wants the future. ***Crās venīre poterō.*** |
| *Nōn volō* instead of *nōlō*: wrong? | Not exactly wrong, and you'll see it now and then for contrast ("I *don't* want to"). But the everyday word is ***nōlō***. |
| The motto, *Quae volumus, et crēdimus libenter.* (*Quae*: the things that, what; *crēdō, crēdere*: believe; *et*: also, here; *libenter*: gladly, Chapter 17.) | "What we want, we also gladly believe." Caesar on wishful thinking, two thousand years early. |

## 7. Lectiō: *Cāseum emere volō!*

:: Dūcunt volentem fāta, nōlentem trahunt. || The Fates lead the willing and drag the unwilling. (Seneca, *Letters* 107.11)

Rōmae sunt. Ecce forum! Quid vult familia? Quid potest?

Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. New words: *simul*, "together," and *nimis*, "too, too much"; *iam nōn* is "no longer, not anymore."

::: dialogue lectio
Māter | Ecce forum! Quid vultis vidēre, līberī? || Mother | Here's the forum! What do y'all want to see, kids?
Cornēlia | Ego Viam Sacram vidēre volō. Et librōs emere! || Cornēlia | I want to see the Sacred Way. And buy books!
Marcus | Ego gladiātōrēs spectāre volō! || Marcus | I want to watch gladiators!
Quīntus | Et ego! Gladiātōrēs spectāre volumus! || Quīntus | Me too! We want to watch gladiators!
Māter | Nōn potestis, puerī. Gladiātōrēs nōn spectābimus. || Mother | You can't, boys. We won't be watching gladiators.
Marcus | Sed volumus! || Marcus | But we want to!
Pater | Nōlīte, puerī. Hodiē cāseum quaerimus. || Father | Don't, boys. Today we're looking for cheese.
Avus | Ita! Cāseum! Ubi est taberna? Ambulāre iam nōn possum… || Grandpa | Yes! Cheese! Where's the shop? I can't walk anymore…
Iōannēs | Ecce taberna! Et tabernārius multōs cāseōs habet! || John | Look, a shop! And the shopkeeper has lots of cheeses!
Tabernārius | Salvēte! Quid vultis? || Shopkeeper | Hello, all of you! What do y'all want?
Iōannēs | Cāseum emere volō. Cāseum magnum! || John | I want to buy a cheese. A big cheese!
Tabernārius | Cāseum parvum an magnum vīs? || Shopkeeper | Do you want a small cheese or a big one?
Iōannēs | Magnum! Avō meō cāseum emō. || John | Big! I'm buying the cheese for my grandpa.
Tabernārius | Ecce cāseus magnus. Sed potesne portāre? || Shopkeeper | Here's a big cheese. But can you carry it?
Iōannēs | Possum! … Ēheu! Nimis magnus est. Nōn possum! || John | I can! … Oh, no! It's too big. I can't!
Marcus | Quīnte, venī! Simul portāre possumus! || Marcus | Quīntus, come! We can carry it together!
Cornēlia | Et ego portāre possum! || Cornēlia | And I can carry it too!
Līberī | Possumus! || The kids | We can!
Tabernārius | Bene! Et crustula vultis? || Shopkeeper | Good! And do y'all want cookies?
Quīntus | Ita! Ego crustula mālō! || Quīntus | Yes! I'd rather have cookies!
Cornēlia | Semper crustula vīs, Quīnte. || Cornēlia | You always want cookies, Quīntus.
Avus | Ego cāseum volō. Nunc! Cāseum edere volō! || Grandpa | I want the cheese. Now! I want to eat the cheese!
Māter | Nōn potes, Ave. Cāseum domī edēmus, vesperī. || Mother | You can't, Grandpa. We'll eat the cheese at home, this evening.
Avus | Sed exspectāre nōlō! || Grandpa | But I don't want to wait!
Pater | Exspectāre potes, Ave. Et ego in viā cantāre possum! || Father | You can wait, Grandpa. And I can sing on the road!
Līberī | Nōlī, Pater! Nōlī! || The kids | Don't, Dad! Don't!
Māter | In viā librum legēs, Cornēlī. || Mother | On the road you'll read a book, Cornēlius.
Pater | Legam… sed cantāre volō. || Father | I'll read… but I want to sing.
Iōannēs | Ubi est Avus? Ubi est capsa? || John | Where's Grandpa? Where's the box?
Marcus | Ecce! Avus in viā sedet… et capsam habet! || Marcus | Look! Grandpa's sitting in the street… and he has the box!
Iōannēs | Ave! Quid facis? || John | Grandpa! What are you doing?
Avus | Exspectāre nōn poteram… Nōn omnia possum. Sed cāseum edere possum! || Grandpa | I couldn't wait… I can't do everything. But I can eat cheese!
Cornēlia | Perendiē? Minimē! Hodiē cāseus est in stomachō… || Cornēlia | The day after tomorrow? No way! Today the cheese is in the stomach of…
Omnēs | …Avī! || Everyone | …Grandpa!
:::

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Quid vultis vidēre, līberī?* Which word wants, and which word completes it? | *Vultis*, "y'all want" (one of the wild three). *Vidēre*, "to see," completes it. |
| *Gladiātōrēs spectāre volumus!* · *Nōn potestis, puerī.* Person of each verb? | *Volumus*: we (the boys). *Potestis*: y'all (Mother to the boys): *pot-* before *e*. |
| *Ambulāre iam nōn possum…* What does *iam nōn* add? | "No longer, not anymore." *Iam* is "already, by now" (Chapter 16), so *iam nōn* is "by now, not": "I can't walk anymore." |
| *Cāseum parvum an magnum vīs?* | "Do you want a small cheese or a big one?" *An*, "or" in a question (Chapter 17); *vīs*, "you want," with a plain object. |
| *Possum! … Nimis magnus est. Nōn possum!* Where's the infinitive? | Left out: the shopkeeper just asked *potesne portāre?*, so *portāre* is understood. Like English "I can!" *Nimis*: too. |
| *Simul portāre possumus!* | "We can carry it together." *Simul*: together, at the same time. Marcus and Quīntus make one "we": *possumus*. Then Cornēlia joins (*Et ego… possum*), and all three shout *Possumus!* |
| *Ego crustula mālō!* Apple, or preference? | Preference: no other verb in the line, so *mālō* is "I'd rather." Quīntus picks cookies over cheese. |
| *Nōlī, Pater! Nōlī!* What's left out after *nōlī*? | *Cantāre*: "Don't (sing)!" Dad's singing again (Chapter 16's *Ēheu! Pater cantābit!*). |
| *Exspectāre nōn poteram…* Tense? And where's the cheese? | Imperfect of *possum*, "I wasn't able to wait" (all that time). Chapter 17 predicted *perendiē… in stomachō Avī*, the day after tomorrow. It got there today. |

## 8. Sound check

:: Sī vīs, potes. || If you want to, you can.

Audī et dīc.

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking.

1. Chant *sum* and *possum* side by side: *sum, possum · es, potes · est, potest…* Then *possum* in the imperfect and the future.
2. Chant *volō* and *nōlō* side by side: *volō, nōlō · vīs, nōn vīs · vult, nōn vult…*
3. One-word answers, out loud: *Potesne? Possum! · Vīsne? Volō! · Nōlō! · Nōn possum.*
4. Say three things you can do and one you can't: *Legere possum. Currere possum. Cantāre possum… Volāre nōn possum!*
5. Say what you want and don't want today: *Cāseum edere volō. Labōrāre nōlō.*
6. Old lines, finished at last: *Audī, vidē, tacē, sī vīs vīvere in pāce · Sī vīs pācem, parā bellum · Nēmō potest duōbus dominīs servīre · Nōlī timēre.*
7. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part (Grandpa can't wait). Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
