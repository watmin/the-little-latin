# Caput XV: Ovēs et Mare

*Liber II, Rēs Cotīdiānae · Chapter 15*

*In which the third family shows its last big branch: nouns with a little i hiding in their stem. They change only one or two endings, so the real skill is spotting them, and you'll learn two quick tests for that. Then we meet sheep, dogs, cats, birds, and fish, take the whole family to the sea, find out what Cheese the puppy really is, and watch the family cheese fly away.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Cavē canem!* · *Mare nostrum* · *Urbī et orbī* · *Cīvis Rōmānus sum.* · *In marī piscēs natant.*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** i-stem (a third-family noun whose stem once ended in *i*), parisyllabic ("equal-syllabled")
    - **Forms:** "of many" in *-ium* (*ovium, urbium*); neuters in *-e, -al, -ar*: *-ī, -ia, -ium* (*in marī, maria, animālium*); all cases of *ovis, urbs, mare, animal*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Little i, the Law of the Sea
    - **Words:** animals (sheep, dog, cat, bird, fish, fox, mouse); nature (sea, shore, hill, night, fire, cloud); city, citizen, enemy, ship; tooth, bone, mind
    - **Reading:** *Ad mare* (a day at the sea, and a very rare bird)
    - **Speaking:** name the animals; say what's in the sea, on the hill, and in the sky

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep saying every new noun as a **pair** with its gender: *ovis, ovis, feminine*. In this chapter, also **count the syllables** in the pair. You'll see why in section 1.

---

## 1. *Ovis, ovis*

:: Pāsce ovēs meās. || Feed my sheep. (the Gospel of John 21:17)

Ecce ovis. Ovis alba est. Ecce ovēs. Ovēs albae sunt.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Ovis, ovis*: a sheep (O-wis). Look at the pair. What's odd about it, after *rēx, rēgis* and *nōmen, nōminis*? | The two forms are **identical**: naming *ovis*, "of" *ovis*. Nothing is hiding. Take *-is* off and the stem is plain ***ov-***. |
| Count the syllables in each pair: *rēx · rē-gis*; *mī-les · mī-li-tis*; *o-vis · o-vis*. | *Rēx* and *mīles* grow a syllable in the "of" form. *Ovis* doesn't: two and two. Grammarians call that **parisyllabic**, "equal-syllabled" (*pār*, equal, as in parity). |
| *Ovis* is feminine. "A white sheep." | *Ovis alba.* Same game as Chapter 3: the adjective matches gender, number, and case. |
| Chapter 13's endings *-ī, -em, -e*: "to the sheep," "I see the sheep," "with the sheep." | *Ovī · Ovem videō · Cum ove.* Exactly like *rēgī, rēgem, rēge*. |
| The motto, *Pāsce ovēs meās.* (*Pāscō, pāscere*: feed, graze.) One sheep or many? Which job does *ovēs* have, and why *meās*? | Many, and the object: *-ēs* does double duty, like *rēgēs* (Chapter 13). English "sheep" doesn't change; *ovis* does. *Meās* is feminine plural object, like *puellās*. "Feed my sheep": Jesus to Peter, in the Gospel of John. Your Gospel, Iōannēs. The one who feeds them is a *pāstor*. |
| Chapter 1's bread was *pānis*, and Chapter 11 gave its pair: *pānis, pānis*. Count. | Two and two, *-is* and *-is*: the same pattern as *ovis*. You've had one of this chapter's nouns since day one. |
| Now "of many sheep." From *rēgum*, you'd guess…? | *Ovum*? No! Latin says ***ovium***, O-wi-um. An **i** sneaks in before *-um*. (Good thing, too: *ōvum* is Chapter 2's egg.) |
| Where does that *i* come from? | Long ago the stem was *ovi-*. The *i* hides in most forms but peeks out in *ovium*. So *ovis* is an **i-stem**: a third-family noun whose stem once ended in *-i*. *Rēx* is a consonant stem (Chapter 13). |
| "The sheep's head" (one sheep). "The sheep's heads" (many sheep). | *Caput ovis · Capita ovium.* |

## 2. *Cīvis Rōmānus sum*

:: Cīvis Rōmānus sum. || I am a Roman citizen. (Cicero, *Against Verres* 2.5.162)

Cīvis, cīvis. Hostis, hostis. Nāvis, nāvis. Fēlēs, fēlis.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Cīvis, cīvis*: a citizen (KEE-wis), masculine or feminine. Equal syllables? Then "of many citizens." | Yes, two and two. ***Cīvium***, KEE-wi-um. Civil, civic. |
| The motto, *Cīvis Rōmānus sum.* What's going on? | "I am a Roman citizen." Cicero tells how a man named Gavius kept shouting it while a crooked governor, Verres, had him flogged. It should have saved him: no Roman citizen could lawfully be flogged. *Rōmānus* matches *cīvis*: masculine, one, naming. |
| *Hostis, hostis* (m.): an enemy, especially the country's enemy. "Of the enemies"? | *Hostium.* Hostile. |
| *Nāvis, nāvis* (f.): a ship (NAH-wis). "The citizens' ships." | *Nāvēs cīvium.* Navy, naval. |
| *Fēlēs, fēlis* (f.): a cat (FAY-lays). The naming form ends in *-ēs*, not *-is*. Count. | *Fē-lēs, fē-lis*: two and two. Parisyllabic, so an i-stem: ***fēlium***. Feline. |
| Chapter 13's *mīles, mīlitis* ends in *-ēs* too. i-stem? | No: *mī-les* has two syllables, *mī-li-tis* three. Consonant stem: *mīlitum*. The count decides, not the ending alone. |
| Chapter 13's *pater, patris* is two and two! i-stem? | No. The naming form must end in *-is* or *-ēs*, and *pater* ends in *-er*. So *patrum, mātrum, frātrum*, as you learned. |
| And your own name, *Iōannēs, Iōannis*? | On paper it qualifies: *-ēs* and *-is*, three syllables each. But there's only one of you, so you'll never need "of many Johns." |
| Sort: *hostis, rēx, nāvis, mīles, fēlēs, soror*. Which say "of many" with *-ium*? | *Hostium, nāvium, fēlium.* The others: *rēgum, mīlitum, sorōrum*. |

## 3. *Urbs et mōns*

:: Urbī et orbī. || To the City and to the world. (the Pope's Easter and Christmas blessing)

Urbs, urbis. Mōns, montis. Pars, partis. Dēns, dentis. Mēns, mentis.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Urbs, urbis* (f.): a city; to a Roman, *the* City, Rome. Equal syllables? | No: *urbs* has one, *ur-bis* two. And yet "of many cities" is ***urbium***. Something else is going on. Urban. |
| Look at the stem, *urb-*. What's at the end of it? Compare *rēg-*. | Two consonants, *r* and *b*. *Rēg-* ends in just one. |
| *Mōns, montis* (m.): a mountain, a hill. Stem? "Of many hills"? | *Mont-*, ending in *n* and *t*: two consonants. ***Montium.*** Mountain, Montana. (Long ō in *mōns*, short o in *montis*: a vowel before *ns* is always long.) |
| *Pars, partis* (f.): a part; stem *part-*, so "of the parts" is *partium*. Caesar opens his war diary: *Gallia est omnis dīvīsa in partēs trēs.* Find *partēs*. | The object after *in*, "into": "All Gaul is divided into three parts." The plural is plain *-ēs*: only "of many" shows the *i*. (*Trēs*, three, waits for Chapter 22; *omnis* is "all," like Chapter 8's *Omnēs viae*.) |
| *Dēns, dentis* (m.): a tooth. Chapter 14's body again: "My teeth hurt." | *Dentēs mihi dolent.* Dentist. |
| Chapter 14's chunk, *Mēns sāna in corpore sānō.* Now meet the pair: *mēns, mentis* (f.), the mind. Stem? "Of many minds"? | *Ment-*, *mentium*. MAYNS, but MEN-tis: the e is long only before *ns*. Mental. The chunk is now fully yours. |
| The motto, *Urbī et orbī.* (*Orbis, orbis*: a circle, the world.) Which case? | Dative, *-ī*: "to the City (Rome) and to the world." The Pope's blessing at Easter and Christmas. *Orbis* is parisyllabic, so an i-stem too. |
| Chapter 14's other *os*: *os, ossis*, bone. Stem? "Of many bones"? | *Oss-*, a double *s*. ***Ossium***: an i-stem by the same test, even though it's neuter. |
| *Rēx* and *urbs* are both one syllable. Why *rēgum* but *urbium*? | Count the consonants before *-is*: *rēg-is*, one; *urb-is*, two. |

!!! success "The Law of the Little i"
    A third-family noun is an **i-stem**, and says "of many" with ***-ium***, if:

    1. its naming form ends in *-is* or *-ēs* and has **as many syllables** as its "of" form: *ovis, ovis · cīvis, cīvis · fēlēs, fēlis*; or
    2. its naming form ends in *-s* or *-x* and its stem ends in **two consonants**: *urbs, urb-is · mōns, mont-is · os, oss-is*.

    Everything else is the same as *rēx*. For masculine and feminine nouns, the only new ending is *-ium*. (A few rebels are next.)

## 4. *Cavē canem!*

:: Cavē canem. || Beware of the dog. (a doorway mosaic in Pompeii; Petronius, *Satyricon* 29)

Cāseus catulus est. Catulus est canis parvus.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Canis, canis* (m. or f.): a dog (KA-nis). By the Law of the Little i, "of many dogs" should be…? | *Canium*, by the law. But Latin says ***canum***. *Canis* is a rebel: learn it with its pair. Canine. |
| The motto, *Cavē canem!* *Cavē* is "watch out (for)!" from *caveō, cavēre*. Which form is *canem*? | The object, *-em*: "Watch out for the dog!" A house in Pompeii has it in its doorway mosaic, under a black dog on a chain. |
| "Y'all, beware of the dogs!" | *Cavēte canēs!* Plural command *-te* (Chapter 6), plural object *-ēs*. |
| Cheese is a *catulus*. In this chapter's words, what is that? | *Canis parvus*: a little dog. *Parvus* matches *canis*: masculine, one, naming. |
| Dogs bark: *lātrō, lātrāre* (LAH-troh), first conjugation. "The dog barks." "The dogs bark." | *Canis lātrat. Canēs lātrant.* Like *amat, amant*. |
| Careful: *latrō, latrōnis*, with a **short** a, is a robber. | *Lātrō*, I bark · *latrō*, robber. One vowel's length makes a different word, as with *ōs* and *os*. |
| *Mūs, mūris* (m.): a mouse. Stem? i-stem? | *Mūr-*: one consonant (the *s* turned into *r*, as in *corpus, corporis*). Not an i-stem by the law, yet Romans said both *mūrium* and *mūrum*. Recognize either. |
| Horace mocks a big build-up with a small payoff: mountains in labor give birth to a silly mouse (*montēs… mūs*, *Ars Poetica* 139). One or many? | Many mountains, *montēs*; one mouse, *mūs*. "The mountains labor, and out comes a mouse." |
| In poets and older prose you'll meet objects like *ovīs, urbīs, nāvīs* (long ī). What are they? | The old i-stem object plural, *-īs*, meaning the same as *-ēs*. Don't confuse it with *puellīs, hortīs* (to/with many, Book I): here it's an object. Recognize it, don't drill it: *ovēs* is always right. |

## 5. *Rāra avis*

:: Rāra avis in terrīs. || A rare bird on the earth. (Juvenal, *Satires* 6.165)

Canis, fēlēs, avis, piscis, vulpēs, mūs. Animālia!

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "The cat sees the mouse." Then turn it around: "The mouse sees the cat." | *Fēlēs mūrem videt. Mūs fēlem videt.* The endings say who sees whom, in any order. |
| *Avis, avis* (f.): a bird (A-wis). i-stem? "Of many birds"? | Yes: two and two, *-is*. ***Avium.*** Aviation. |
| Don't mix them up: *avus, avia, avis*. | Grandpa (second family), Grandma (first), bird (third, i-stem). *Avia avem videt*: Grandma sees a bird. |
| The motto, *Rāra avis in terrīs.* (*Terra*, earth.) | "A rare bird on the earth." Juvenal says a perfect wife is as rare as a black swan. Now "rara avis" means any rare find. *Rāra* matches *avis*: feminine, one. |
| *Piscis, piscis* (m.): a fish (PIS-kis). "Many fish." "Of many fish." | *Piscēs* (the zodiac's Pisces), *piscium*. Classical *sc* is always "sk"; church Latin says PEE-shis. |
| *Vulpēs, vulpis* (f.): a fox. i-stem? Then a Roman saying: *Vulpēs pilum mūtat, nōn mōrēs.* (*Pilus*, hair; *mūtō, mūtāre*, change.) | Yes: *vul-pēs, vul-pis*, two and two, so *vulpium*. "The fox changes its fur, not its ways." Chapter 14's *mōrēs*. A herdsman said it to the money-loving emperor Vespasian (after Suetonius, *Vespasian* 16). |
| Genders: *canis, ovis, fēlēs, avis, piscis, vulpēs, mūs*. Can the ending tell you? | No. *Ovis, fēlēs, avis, vulpēs* are feminine; *piscis, mūs* masculine; *canis* either. Learn the gender with the pair. |
| "Grandma gives the cat a fish." | *Avia fēlī piscem dat.* *Fēlī*, to the cat (dative); *piscem*, the object. |
| "The dogs' food and the cats' food." | *Cibus canum et cibus fēlium.* The rebel *canum* next to the regular *fēlium*. |

## 6. *Mare nostrum*

:: Mare nostrum. || Our sea. (the Romans' name for the Mediterranean)

Mare, maris. Animal, animālis. Cubīle, cubīlis. In marī piscēs natant.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Mare, maris*: the sea, **neuter** (MA-re; both vowels short). "I see the sea"? | *Mare videō.* Object = naming form: the Neuter Twins (Chapter 14). Marine. |
| The motto, *Mare nostrum.* Why *nostrum*? | *Mare* is neuter, so the possessive is too, like *nōmen tuum*. "Our sea": the Mediterranean, once Rome owned every shore of it. |
| Chapter 14 said the third family's "in" form ends in *-e*: *in corpore*. So "in the sea"? | Surprise: ***in marī***, with *-ī*. "Fish swim in the sea": *In marī piscēs natant.* (*Natō, natāre*: swim.) |
| Many seas. Chapter 14's neuters say *corpora*. So…? | ***Maria***, MA-ri-a: an *i* before the *-a*. The little i again. |
| *Animal, animālis*: an animal, neuter (A-ni-mal, a-ni-MAH-lis). "Many animals"? | ***Animālia***, a-ni-MAH-li-a. Like *maria*. |
| "Of the animals." "With the animal." | *Animālium. Cum animālī.* The i in every place it can be. |
| *Cubīle, cubīlis*: a bed, a den, where an animal sleeps (ku-BEE-le). "Cheese is in his bed." | *Cāseus in cubīlī est.* *-Ī* again. |
| What do *mare, animal, cubīle* share? | All three are neuters ending in *-e, -al,* or *-ar*. Their *i* shows in three places: with/from one *-ī*, many *-ia*, of many *-ium*. |
| *Os, ossis* (bone) is a neuter i-stem too. Same three places? | No: *os* ends in *-s*, not *-e, -al, -ar*. Only "of many" is *ossium*. Otherwise it's like *corpus*: *in osse*, plural *ossa*. "The dog has bones": *Canis ossa habet.* |

!!! success "The Law of the Sea"
    Third-family **neuters ending in *-e, -al, -ar*** (*mare, animal, cubīle*) are i-stems all the way: with/from one ***-ī*** (*in marī*, not *in mare*; a poet bends this once in a while), many ***-ia*** (*maria, animālia*), of many ***-ium*** (*animālium*). The Neuter Twins still hold: naming = object = calling.

!!! success "Tertia dēclīnātiō: i-stems"
    **Singular**

    | case | m./f. | *ovis* (f.) | *urbs* (f.) | neuter | *mare* | *animal* |
    |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
    | nom. (naming) | *-is, -ēs*, or short (*urbs*) | *ovis* | *urbs* | *-e, -al, -ar* | *mare* | *animal* |
    | gen. (of, whose) | *-is* | *ovis* | *urbis* | *-is* | *maris* | *animālis* |
    | dat. (to, for) | *-ī* | *ovī* | *urbī* | *-ī* | *marī* | *animālī* |
    | acc. (object; where to) | *-em* | *ovem* | *urbem* | = nom. | *mare* | *animal* |
    | abl. (where, from, with) | *-e* | *ove* | *urbe* | ***-ī*** | ***marī*** | ***animālī*** |
    | voc. (calling) | = nom. | *ovis* | *urbs* | = nom. | *mare* | *animal* |

    **Plural**

    | case | m./f. | *ovis* | *urbs* | neuter | *mare* | *animal* |
    |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
    | nom. | *-ēs* | *ovēs* | *urbēs* | ***-ia*** | ***maria*** | ***animālia*** |
    | gen. | ***-ium*** | ***ovium*** | ***urbium*** | ***-ium*** | (*marium*, rare) | ***animālium*** |
    | dat. | *-ibus* | *ovibus* | *urbibus* | *-ibus* | *maribus* | *animālibus* |
    | acc. | *-ēs* (*-īs*) | *ovēs* | *urbēs* | ***-ia*** | ***maria*** | ***animālia*** |
    | abl. | *-ibus* | *ovibus* | *urbibus* | *-ibus* | *maribus* | *animālibus* |
    | voc. | *-ēs* | *ovēs* | *urbēs* | ***-ia*** | ***maria*** | ***animālia*** |

    The bold forms are the only differences from *rēx* and *nōmen*.

## 7. *Ignis et nox*

:: Ubi fūmus, ibi ignis. || Where there's smoke, there's fire. (proverb)

Nox est. Ignis in lītore est. Ecce nūbēs, ecce astra.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Ignis, ignis* (m.): fire. Which test makes it an i-stem? "Of many fires"? | The first: two and two, *-is*. *Ignium.* Ignite. |
| The motto, *Ubi fūmus, ibi ignis.* (*Fūmus*, smoke.) | Chapter 7's *ubi… ibi*: "Where (there's) smoke, there (there's) fire." |
| Seneca: *Ignis aurum probat.* (*Probō, probāre*: test.) | "Fire tests gold." (*Aurum*, OW-rum, from Chapter 1.) He goes on: hard times test brave men. |
| *Nox, noctis* (f.): night. Which test? "Of many nights"? | The second: *-x*, and the stem *noct-* ends in two consonants. *Noctium.* Nocturnal. |
| Catullus begs for kisses now, because after life *nox est perpetua ūna dormienda*. Find the night and its words. | *Nox* is the subject; *perpetua* (never-ending) and *ūna* (one) match it, feminine. "There is one never-ending night to be slept." (*Dormienda* is a form for much later: "to be slept.") |
| *Nūbēs, nūbis* (f.): a cloud. i-stem? "The bird is in the clouds." | Yes: two and two. *Avis in nūbibus est.* |
| *Lītus, lītoris*: the shore. Chapter 14's Law of the Disguise: family and gender? "On the shore"? | Third family, **neuter**, *-us* in disguise. A consonant stem, not an i-stem, so *in lītore*: *-e*, not *-ī*. |
| Sort: *nox, rēx, ignis, pēs, nāvis, corpus, animal, dēns*. Which say "of many" with *-ium*? | *Noctium, ignium, nāvium, animālium, dentium.* The others: *rēgum, pedum, corporum*. |
| "On the ship." | *In nāve.* A few i-stems (*nāvis, ignis, avis, cīvis*) sometimes use *-ī* here too: *in nāvī*. Recognize it; *-e* is fine. |

## 8. Lectiō: *Ad mare*

:: Aquila nōn capit muscās. || An eagle doesn't catch flies. (proverb)

Familia ad mare it. Cāseus quoque it. Et cāseus…

Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. Only one new word: *Bau!*, "Woof!" Everything else is from this chapter or before.

::: dialogue lectio
Pater | Salvēte, līberī! Hodiē ad mare īmus! || Father | Morning, kids! Today we're going to the sea!
Līberī | Ad mare! Ad mare! || The kids | To the sea! To the sea!
Māter | Ecce cibus: pānis, māla, et cāseus magnus. Cibus in capsā est. || Mother | Here's the food: bread, apples, and a big cheese. The food is in the box.
Avus | Et Cāseus? Catulus quoque it? || Grandpa | And Cheese? Is the puppy coming too?
Cornēlia | Ita! Cāseus pars familiae est! || Cornēlia | Yes! Cheese is part of the family!
Quīntus | Spectāte! In monte sunt ovēs! Multae ovēs! || Quīntus | Look! There are sheep on the hill! Lots of sheep!
Cāseus | Bau! Bau! || Cheese | Woof! Woof!
Marcus | Cāseus lātrat! Ovēs timent! || Marcus | Cheese is barking! The sheep are scared!
Avia | Minimē. Ovēs nōn timent: Cāseus canis parvus est. || Grandma | Not a bit. The sheep aren't scared: Cheese is a little dog.
Avus | Cavē canem, ovis! || Grandpa | Beware of the dog, sheep!
Iōannēs | Ecce mare! Mare magnum et caeruleum! || John | There's the sea! The big blue sea!

Marcus | Ego in marī natō! || Marcus | I'm swimming in the sea!
Quīntus | Et ego! Ecce piscēs! Multī piscēs in marī sunt! || Quīntus | Me too! Look, fish! There are lots of fish in the sea!
Marcus | In marī ego rēx piscium sum! || Marcus | In the sea, I'm the king of the fish!
Cornēlia | Cāseus quoque natat! Nāsus niger in marī! || Cornēlia | Cheese is swimming too! A black nose in the sea!
Avia | Ego in lītore sedeō. || Grandma | I'm sitting on the shore.
Avus | Et ego in lītore dormiō. || Grandpa | And I'm sleeping on the shore.
Iōannēs | Spectāte! Avis magna et alba in lītore est. || John | Look! There's a big white bird on the shore.
Marcus | Avis ad capsam it… || Marcus | The bird is going to the box…
Quīntus | Ad cāseum nostrum! Cavē avem, Avia! || Quīntus | To our cheese! Watch out for the bird, Grandma!
Cāseus | Bau! Bau! Bau! || Cheese | Woof! Woof! Woof!
Cornēlia | Avis cāseum capit… et nunc in nūbibus est! || Cornēlia | The bird grabs the cheese… and now it's up in the clouds!
Avus | Quid est? Ubi est cāseus? || Grandpa | What is it? Where's the cheese?
Avia | Rāra avis: avis fūr! || Grandma | A rare bird: a bird that's a thief!
Avus | Ō tempora, ō mōrēs! Avēs fūrēs sunt! || Grandpa | Oh, the times! Oh, the customs! Birds are thieves!
Pater | Nox est. Ignem in lītore facimus. || Father | It's night. We're making a fire on the shore.
Marcus | Ecce astra! Multa astra! || Marcus | Look at the stars! So many stars!
Māter | Ecce pānis et māla. Edite, līberī! || Mother | Here's bread and apples. Eat up, kids!
Quīntus | Pānis et māla… sed ubi est cāseus noster? || Quīntus | Bread and apples… but where's our cheese?
Cornēlia | Spectāte Cāseum! Os in ōre habet. || Cornēlia | Look at Cheese! He has a bone in his mouth.
Avus | Canis os habet, avis cāseum… || Grandpa | The dog has a bone, the bird has the cheese…
Iōannēs | Et cāseus noster? Ubi semper: in stomachō… avis! || John | And our cheese? Where it always is: in the stomach… of a bird!

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Cāseus pars familiae est.* Which case is *familiae*? And "of many parts"? | *Familiae* is the genitive, "of the family." *Pars* makes *partium*. |
| *In monte sunt ovēs.* Which case is *monte*? One hill or many? | Ablative after *in*, place where: one hill. *Ovēs* is the plural subject. |
| *Ego rēx piscium sum!* Why *piscium*, not *piscum*? | *Piscis, piscis*: two and two, so an i-stem: "king **of the fish**" is *-ium*. Chapter 13's game, now at sea. |
| *Cāseus canis parvus est.* Why *canis*, not *canem*? | After *est*, the naming form (Chapter 2): Cheese = a little dog. |
| *Ego in marī natō!* Why *marī*, not *mare*? | The Law of the Sea: a neuter in *-e* says "in" with *-ī*. |
| *Ecce nāvēs mercātōrum.* Which noun is an i-stem? | *Nāvēs* (*nāvis, nāvis*: "of many" *nāvium*). *Mercātor, mercātōris* is a consonant stem: *mercātōrum*. |
| *Cavē avem, Avia!* Sort out *avem, Avia, avis*. | *Avem*, the bird (object); *Avia*, Grandma (calling); *avis*, the bird (naming), as in the next lines. |
| *Os in ōre habet.* Which is which? | *Os* (short) is the bone, the object; *in ōre* (long) is "in his mouth." Chapter 14's warning pays off. |
| *In stomachō… avis!* *Avis* can be the naming form or the "of" form. Which is it here? | "Of": "in the stomach of a bird." When the pair is equal-syllabled, naming and "of" look the same, so let the sentence decide. |

## 9. Sound check

:: Ad astra per aspera. || To the stars through hardships. (proverb; Chapter 8 had it the other way round)

Audī et dīc.

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *ovis · ovēs · ovium*? | O-wis · O-ways · O-wi-um. Classical *v* is "w." The stress (emphasis) stays on the first syllable: the *i* before *-um* is short. |
| *cīvis · piscis*? | KEE-wis · PIS-kis. Church Latin says CHEE-vis and PEE-shis; classical *c* is always "k," and *sc* is always "sk." |
| *mōns · montis · dēns · dentis · mēns · mentis*? | MOHNS · MON-tis · DAYNS · DEN-tis · MAYNS · MEN-tis. Long before *ns*, short before *nt*. |
| *mare · marī · maria*? | MA-re · MA-ree · MA-ri-a. The final *e* is a short "eh," never silent: not "mair." |
| *animal · animālis · animālia*? | A-ni-mal · a-ni-MAH-lis · a-ni-MAH-li-a. The stress jumps to the long ā when the word grows. |
| *Cavē canem*? | KA-way KA-nem. |
| *lātrō · latrō*? | LAH-troh (I bark) · LA-troh (robber). Hold the long ā. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking.

1. Say every new noun as a pair with its gender, and count the syllables: *ovis, ovis, f. (two and two) · urbs, urbis, f. (one and two, but urb-) · mare, maris, n.*
2. Decline *ovis* and *urbs* through all six cases, singular and plural. Then *mare* and *animal*. Lean on the bold forms.
3. "Of many," fast: *ovium · cīvium · urbium · noctium · animālium*, then the rebel: *canum*.
4. Name the animals and what they do: *Canis lātrat. Fēlēs mūrem videt. Piscēs in marī natant. Avis in nūbibus est. Ovēs in monte sunt.*
5. Point at each i: *in marī · maria · animālia · Urbī et orbī · Cīvis Rōmānus sum · Cavē canem · Mare nostrum.*
6. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part (Cheese too). Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
