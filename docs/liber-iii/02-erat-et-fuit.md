# Caput XXVI: Erat et Fuit

*Liber III, Fābulae · Chapter 26*

*In which the film and the snapshot finally meet. Chapter 16 gave you the imperfect, a film still running; Chapter 25 gave you the perfect, a snapshot of a finished fact. Both say "was." Today you learn which camera to pick, why Vergil says* erat *in one line and* fuit *in another, and how a Latin story sets its stage with one tense and moves forward with the other. Then Grandpa tells a story, and somebody eats the cheese while he talks. Speaking goal: tell a story.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Nox erat.* · *Avus dormiēbat; subitō canis lātrāvit.* · *Ōlim puer eram.* · *Quid tum?* · *Nārrā nōbīs fābulam!* · *Fuit cāseus!*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** aspect (how a verb looks at an action: from inside while it runs, or from outside as one finished whole); narration (telling a story); the "trying" imperfect (old-school: conative imperfect)
    - **Forms:** no new endings. Old imperfects (*-bam*, *eram*) and Chapter 25's perfects (*-ī, -istī, -it…*, *fuī*) side by side; new keys for old verbs: *dormīvī, sēdī, timuī, cucurrī, lātrāvī, spectāvī, mānsī, vīxī*
    - **Laws:** the Law of Erat and Fuit; the Law of the Stage and the Step
    - **Words:** story words *ōlim, subitō, deinde, semel, cotīdiē, diū*; *fābula*, *nārrō*, *clāmō*, *Quid tum?*
    - **Reading:** *Fābula Avī* (Grandpa tells a story; the cheese has other plans)
    - **Speaking:** tell a short story in the past: set the scene, then make things happen

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep two chants handy: Chapter 16's *amābam, amābās, amābat…* and Chapter 25's *amāvī, amāvistī, amāvit…*. Nothing new to memorize in the endings today. The new skill is **choosing**.

## 1. Film and snapshot

:: Nōn sum quālis eram. || I am not what I was. (Horace, *Odes* 4.1.3; Chapter 16)

Heri Avus dormiēbat. Heri Avus dormīvit. Quid interest?

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 16 called the imperfect a film. Chapter 25 called the perfect a snapshot. Which tense is *dormiēbat*? Which is *dormīvit*? | *Dormiēbat*: imperfect (sign *-bā-*), "he was sleeping." *Dormīvit*: perfect (*dormiō, dormīre, dormīvī, dormītum*), "he slept." |
| Are both in the past? | Yes. Same time: yesterday. So the difference isn't **when**. |
| *Heri Avus dormiēbat.* Where does the sentence put you? | Inside the moment, watching Grandpa asleep. The film is running. We don't know yet when (or whether) he woke up. |
| *Heri Avus dormīvit.* And this one? | Outside, looking back at the whole nap as one finished fact: "He slept." Click. Done. |
| Plain English: **aspect** is how a verb looks at an action — from inside while it's going on, or from outside as one finished whole. Old-school name? | Aspect (Latin *aspectus*, "a look, a view"). Linguists say **imperfective** (film) vs **perfective** (snapshot); older grammars call the imperfect "continued action" and the perfect "completed action." Tense = **when**; aspect = **how you look at it**. |
| "Marcus was eating the apple." "Marcus ate the apple." Latin? | ***Marcus mālum edēbat.*** (film) · ***Marcus mālum ēdit.*** (snapshot; long ē in the perfect stem *ēd-*, Chapter 25) |
| Which sentence might leave half an apple on the table? | *Edēbat*: we're in the middle; maybe Quīntus grabbed it first. *Ēdit* says the eating is finished. |
| English "Grandpa slept" can mean either. Does Latin let you stay vague? | **No.** Each of your two Latin pasts is a camera: film (imperfect) or snapshot (perfect). Telling a story, you pick one for every verb. That choice is what this chapter trains. |

## 2. *Erat et fuit*

:: Fuimus Trōes, fuit Īlium. || We Trojans are no more; Troy is no more. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 2.325; literally "we have been Trojans, Ilium has been")

Nox erat. Urbs antīqua fuit. Erat an fuit?

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "I was": Chapter 16 gave you one Latin word, Chapter 25 another. Which two? | ***Eram*** (imperfect of *sum*) and ***fuī*** (perfect of *sum*). Third person: ***erat*** and ***fuit***. |
| *Avus fessus erat.* What is *erat* doing? | Describing how things were at that moment: "Grandpa was tired" (then, in the scene). A state on the screen. |
| Chapter 25's Lectiō: Grandpa said *Fuī fēlīx!* Why not *eram*? | He sums up the whole cheese-eating as a closed fact: "I was happy (and that was that)." Looking back from outside. |
| Vergil opens a night scene with two words: *Nox erat.* (*Aeneid* 4.522) Why *erat*? | It paints the background: "It was night." Nothing has happened yet; the stage is set. |
| Vergil introduces Carthage: *Urbs antīqua fuit.* (*Aeneid* 1.12) Why *fuit*? | "There was (once) an ancient city." He looks back at the whole city as a finished fact. His readers knew Rome had destroyed it (146 BC). |
| The motto: *Fuimus Trōes, fuit Īlium.* A Trojan priest says it as the city burns. Translate it literally, then say what it means. | Literally "We have been Trojans; Ilium (Troy) has been." Meaning: **it's over.** We're Trojans no more; Troy is gone. A perfect of *sum* can mean "no longer is." |
| Cicero, after the Catilinarian conspirators were executed (63 BC), announced to the crowd in one word: *Vīxērunt.* (*Vīvō, vīvere, vīxī, vīctum*, Chapter 17's *vīvō*.) | "They have lived" = **they're dead.** The perfect closes the book on their lives. (Reported by Plutarch, *Cicero* 22.) |
| John 1:1, Chapter 16: *In prīncipiō erat Verbum.* Why not *fuit*? | The Word wasn't a finished event; it **was there**, ongoing, at the beginning. *Fuit* would turn it into a closed episode, looked back on from outside. |
| *Ubi erās?* vs *Ubi fuistī?* | *Ubi erās?* "Where were you (at that moment)?" — placing you in the scene. *Ubi fuistī?* "Where have you been? Where were you (all that time)?" — the whole stretch, summed up. |

!!! success "The Law of Erat and Fuit"
    ***Erat*** paints how things **were at the time**: the scene, a state on the screen (*Nox erat. Avus fessus erat.*).
    ***Fuit*** states the whole thing as a **closed fact**, looked at from outside (*Urbs antīqua fuit. Fēlīx fuī.*). Sometimes that means "and it's over": *Fuit Īlium. Vīxērunt.*

## 3. The stage and the step

:: Ībam forte Viā Sacrā. || I happened to be walking along the Sacred Way. (Horace, *Satires* 1.9.1; Chapter 16)

Avus dormiēbat. Subitō canis lātrāvit.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 16: Horace begins a satire *Ībam forte Viā Sacrā.* What has happened so far? | Nothing yet. The imperfect ***ībam*** just puts Horace on the street, walking. Then a bore runs up and grabs his hand: that's the **event**. |
| *Avus dormiēbat. Subitō canis lātrāvit.* (*Subitō*: suddenly. *Lātrō, lātrāre, lātrāvī, lātrātum*: bark, Chapter 15.) Translate. | "Grandpa was sleeping. Suddenly the dog barked." |
| Which verb is the scene, which is the thing that happened? | *Dormiēbat* (imperfect) is the **scene** running in the background. *Lātrāvit* (perfect) is the **event**: one bark, and the story moves. |
| Plain English: **narration** means telling a story (Latin *nārrō*, "I tell"). In narration, what jobs do the two pasts get? | The imperfect sets the **stage**: what was going on, how things looked. The perfect takes the **steps**: what happened next. |
| A quick test for any past verb in a story: ask "And then what?" If the verb answers it, which tense? | The **perfect**. It's a step forward. If the verb describes what was going on around the steps, it's the **imperfect**. |
| Caesar: *Vēnī, vīdī, vīcī.* Stage or steps? | Three steps, three perfects, bang bang bang. No scenery at all. That's why it sounds so fast. |
| *Nox erat. Avia stertēbat. Cāseus in culīnā dormiēbat.* Does anything happen? | No. Three imperfects = a picture. A lovely, snoring stage, waiting for a step. |
| Add a step: "Suddenly John came into the kitchen." | ***Subitō Iōannēs in culīnam vēnit.*** (*In* + accusative for "into," Chapter 8; long ē in *vēnit*.) |
| "We were sitting on the shore. Suddenly a bird came and took the cheese." (*Sedeō*, Chapter 5) | ***In lītore sedēbāmus. Subitō avis vēnit et cāseum cēpit.*** Stage, then two steps. |
| English "The kids were running in the garden when Dad came home." Latin can keep it as two plain sentences. How? | ***Līberī in hortō currēbant. Pater domum vēnit.*** (A Latin "when" clause comes later; today, two sentences do the job.) |

!!! success "The Law of the Stage and the Step"
    In a story, the **imperfect** builds the **stage**: what was going on, how things were, what kept happening (*Nox erat. Avus dormiēbat.*).
    The **perfect** takes the **steps**: what happened, one event after another (*Subitō canis lātrāvit. Avus in culīnam vēnit.*).
    Ask "**And then what?**" A verb that answers it is a step: perfect.

## 4. Habit, once, and trying

:: Libenter eum audiēbat. || He used to listen to him gladly. (Mark 6:20, Vulgate; Chapter 17)

Saepe natābat. Semel natāvit.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 16: *Avus puer saepe in marī natābat.* Which English? | "As a boy, Grandpa **used to** swim in the sea often." The imperfect for a **habit**: the film keeps replaying. |
| "Yesterday Grandpa swam once." (*Semel*: once, one time.) | ***Heri Avus semel natāvit.*** One counted time, finished: snapshot. |
| "Every day Mother used to read a book." (*Cotīdiē*: every day.) | ***Cotīdiē Māter librum legēbat.*** Repeated → imperfect. |
| "One day Mother read a book to the dog." (*Ōlim*: once, one day.) | ***Ōlim Māter canī librum lēgit.*** One event → perfect. (*Canī*: dative, Chapter 13.) |
| English sneaks in "would" for habits: "Every summer Grandpa would swim." Future? | No: that "would" means "used to." Latin: ***natābat***. Imperfect. |
| Words that lean **imperfect**? Words that lean **perfect**? | Imperfect: *saepe, semper, cotīdiē* (repeating). Perfect: *semel, subitō, statim, tandem* (one moment). Lean, not law: *Saepe hoc vīdī* "I've often seen this" counts up a total as a fact. |
| *Avus cāseum capiēbat, sed Avia eum cēpit.* Grandpa "was taking" the cheese… but Grandma took it. Did Grandpa get it? | No! The imperfect can mean **was trying to**: "Grandpa was trying to grab the cheese, but Grandma grabbed it." The film was running; it never reached the end. |
| Plain English: the "trying" imperfect. Old-school name? | The **conative imperfect** (from *cōnor*, "I try"). Same forms, just the film of an attempt. Context tells you. |
| *Avus diū dormīvit* vs *Avus diū dormiēbat.* (*Diū*: for a long time.) | *Dormīvit*: "Grandpa slept a long time" — the whole long nap, summed up and over. *Dormiēbat*: "Grandpa was sleeping for a long time" — we're in the long nap; something may happen during it. |

!!! tip "Leaning words"
    | Leans imperfect (film) | Leans perfect (snapshot) |
    |---|---|
    | *saepe* often · *semper* always · *cotīdiē* every day | *semel* once · *subitō* suddenly · *statim* at once · *tandem* finally |
    | "was …ing," "used to," "kept …ing," "would" (habit), "was trying to" | "…ed," "did," "have …ed" |

    Leaning words help; they don't decide. The question still decides: **stage or step?**

## 5. Story words

:: Ōlim truncus eram fīculnus, inūtile lignum. || Once I was a fig-tree trunk, a useless log. (Horace, *Satires* 1.8.1; a garden statue tells its story)

Ōlim… tum… subitō… deinde… tandem.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Horace's statue starts its story *Ōlim truncus eram.* (*Truncus*: trunk.) Which tense sets it going? | ***Eram***: imperfect, "I was (back then) a trunk." The stage. ***Ōlim***: "once, long ago." |
| Chapter 16's motto *Forsan et haec ōlim meminisse iuvābit*: "someday." So *ōlim* means? | "At some other time": **once (long ago)** with a past verb, **someday** with a future. With a story in the past: "Once upon a time." |
| Apuleius begins the tale of Cupid and Psyche: *Erant in quādam cīvitāte rēx et rēgīna.* (*In quādam cīvitāte*: "in a certain city" — chunk, Chapter 29.) | "There were, in a certain city, a king and a queen." A fairy tale opens with ***erant***: imperfect stage. (*Metamorphōsēs* 4.28) |
| Steps need little signposts. You know *tum* (then, Chapter 16) and *tandem* (finally, Chapter 12). New: *subitō* (suddenly), *deinde* (next, after that). Put them in story order. | *Tum… subitō… deinde… tandem.* (Order can vary; *tandem* comes last.) |
| *Nārrō, nārrāre, nārrāvī, nārrātum*: tell, narrate. *Fābula, fābulae* (f.): story. "Tell us a story!" | ***Nārrā nōbīs fābulam!*** To several people: ***Nārrāte nōbīs fābulam!*** "Y'all, tell us a story!" |
| *Fābulae* is the name of Liber III. Why? | This book is about **stories**: narrating the past (today), then relatives and passives, all the tools of a storyteller. |
| A listener's favorite question: *Quid tum?* | "What then? And then what?" (*Quid* + *tum*.) It begs for a **step**, so the answer is a perfect. |
| Build a four-line story: stage (*Ōlim cāseus in mēnsā erat*), step with *subitō*, step with *deinde*, end with *tandem*. | E.g. ***Ōlim cāseus in mēnsā erat. Subitō Quīntus vēnit. Deinde cāseum cēpit. Tandem Avus clāmāvit!*** (*Clāmō, clāmāre, clāmāvī, clāmātum*: shout.) |

## 6. Liber II, retold

:: Urbs antīqua fuit. || There was an ancient city. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 1.12)

Heri… erat, erant, vēnit, cēpit.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 16 told the gull story all in imperfects: *Avis cāseum nostrum portābat… in nūbēs.* Retell the theft as a step. | ***Avis cāseum nostrum cēpit et in nūbēs tulit.*** "The bird took our cheese and carried it into the clouds." Two snapshots. |
| Chapter 16: *Ita, Cāseus lātrābat. Sed avis nōn timēbat.* Keep or change? | Keep! Those are the scene: the dog **kept barking**, the bird **wasn't scared**. For one finished fact: *Avis nōn timuit* (*timeō, timēre, timuī*). |
| Chapter 16: Marcus says *Rēx piscium eram!* Why should that stay *eram*? | It's what he **was** in the scene, his role all afternoon. Not a step. |
| Chapter 14's puppy: *Pater catulum domum portāvit.* Then the kids argued names all evening. Which tense for "kept suggesting"? | Imperfect: ***Līberī nōmina dīcēbant: "Rēx! Mīles! Stella!"*** |
| Then the puppy grabbed Quīntus's cheese, and the family named him. Two steps. | ***Subitō catulus cāseum Quīntī cēpit. Tum eī nōmen "Cāseus" dedimus.*** "Then we gave him the name 'Cheese.'" |
| Chapter 18, Rome: Grandpa couldn't walk anymore (scene); John bought the cheese (step). | ***Avus ambulāre iam nōn poterat. Iōannēs cāseum magnum ēmit.*** |
| Chapter 18: Grandma and Cāseus stayed home while the rest went to Rome. Step or stage? Pick the tense. (*Maneō, manēre, mānsī, mānsum*.) | As a whole-trip fact: ***Avia et Cāseus domī mānsērunt.*** (*Domī manēbant* also works if you're painting the scene at home while the others were away.) |
| Chapter 20's race: the kids were running around the farmhouse; then the dog ran and beat everyone. (*Currō, currere, cucurrī, cursum*.) | ***Līberī circum vīllam currēbant. Cāseus cucurrit et omnēs vīcit.*** |
| Chapter 22's market: the merchant kept wanting two denarii; finally we bought it for one. | ***Mercātor duōs dēnāriōs volēbat. Tandem cāseum ūnō dēnāriō ēmimus.*** (Price in the ablative, Chapter 22.) |

## 7. Pick the camera

:: Tempus fugit. || Time flies. (after Vergil, *Georgics* 3.284, *fugit inreparābile tempus*)

Imperfectum an perfectum?

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "It was a dark night." Stage or step? (*Obscūrus*: dark.) | Stage: ***Nox obscūra erat.*** |
| "Grandma was snoring." | ***Avia stertēbat.*** Stage (Chapter 17's *stertō*). |
| "Suddenly the dog came into the kitchen." | ***Subitō canis in culīnam vēnit.*** Step. |
| "The day was long" — as a summary at bedtime. | ***Diēs longus fuit.*** Looking back at the whole day: closed fact. (In the middle of the day, painting it: *Diēs longus erat.*) |
| Fix the form: *Avus dormībat.* | ***Dormiēbat.*** Fourth-conjugation imperfect keeps *-iē-* (Chapter 17: *audiēbam*). |
| Fix the form: *Avus cāseum ēdēbat.* | ***Edēbat***, short e. The long *ēd-* is the **perfect** stem only; the imperfect is built on the present stem *ed-*. |
| Fix: *Canis lātrāvēbat.* | Pick one camera: ***lātrābat*** (was barking) or ***lātrāvit*** (barked). Never glue *-bā-* onto a perfect stem. |
| Fix: *Erāvit* / *Fuēbat.* | ***Erat*** or ***fuit***. *Sum* has two separate stems, *er-* and *fu-*; they never mix. |
| A story opens: *Nox fuit. Avus dormīvit. Canis dormīvit.* Grammar OK? | The forms are fine, but the camera's wrong: three closed facts read like a list ("It was night. Grandpa slept. The dog slept. Done."). To set a scene: ***Nox erat. Avus dormiēbat. Canis dormiēbat.*** |
| "Yesterday Grandpa swam once": *Heri Avus semel natābat.* Fix? | ***Natāvit.*** One counted time = snapshot. *Semel* + imperfect fights itself. |

## 8. Lectiō: *Fābula Avī*

:: Nārrā nōbīs fābulam, Ave! || Tell us a story, Grandpa!

Vesperī est. Līberī fābulam volunt. Avus nārrat… et cāseus in mēnsā est. Spectā tempora!

Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. As you read, sort every past verb: **stage** (imperfect) or **step** (perfect)?

::: dialogue lectio
Quīntus | Ave, nārrā nōbīs fābulam! || Quīntus | Grandpa, tell us a story!
Cornēlia | Ita! Et cāseus in fābulā erit? || Cornēlia | Yes! And will there be cheese in the story?
Avus | Semper! Audīte. Ōlim puer eram. Parvus eram, sed multum edēbam. || Grandpa | Always! Listen, y'all. Once upon a time I was a boy. I was small, but I used to eat a lot.
Marcus | Quīntus quoque! || Marcus | Quīntus too!
Quīntus | Ēheu! || Quīntus | Oh no!
Avus | Domus nostra parva erat. Nox erat. Omnēs dormiēbant. || Grandpa | Our house was small. It was night. Everyone was asleep.
Avus | Māter mea stertēbat, et canis noster in culīnā dormiēbat. || Grandpa | My mother was snoring, and our dog was sleeping in the kitchen.
Avus | In mēnsā erat cāseus magnus. Ego in lectō cāseum cōgitābam… || Grandpa | On the table there was a big cheese. I was lying in bed, thinking about the cheese…
Līberī | Quid tum? || The kids | And then what?
Avus | Subitō in culīnam iī. Cāseum cēpī et ēdī — tōtum! || Grandpa | Suddenly I went into the kitchen. I took the cheese and ate it — the whole thing!
Cornēlia | Et canis? Nōn lātrāvit? || Cornēlia | And the dog? Didn't he bark?
Avus | Lātrāvit! Māter mea vēnit et clāmāvit: "Quis cāseum ēdit?" || Grandpa | He barked! My mother came and shouted: "Who ate the cheese?"
Marcus | Et tū? Quid dīxistī? || Marcus | And you? What did you say?
Avus | "Canis ēdit!" dīxī. || Grandpa | "The dog ate it!" I said.
Līberī | Ave! || The kids | Grandpa!
Avus | Sed māter mea sapiēns erat. Cāseum in ōre meō vīdit! || Grandpa | But my mother was wise. She saw cheese in my mouth!
Quīntus | Et deinde? || Quīntus | And after that?
Avus | Deinde diū in hortō labōrāvī… sine cāseō. || Grandpa | After that I worked in the garden for a long time… without cheese.
Pater | Bona fābula fuit! || Father | That was a good story!
Marcus | Et nunc? Ubi est cāseus noster? In mēnsā erat… || Marcus | And now? Where's our cheese? It was on the table…
Cornēlia | Spectāte! Mēnsa vacua est! || Cornēlia | Look, y'all! The table's empty!
Avus | Quis cāseum ēdit? || Grandpa | Who ate the cheese?
Cāseus | Bau! || Cheese | Woof!
Avia | Nōn canis. Tū fābulam nārrābās… et ego cāseum edēbam. || Grandma | Not the dog. You were telling your story… and I was eating the cheese.
Omnēs | In stomachō… || Everyone | In the stomach of…
Avia | …Aviae! Cāseus erat. Nunc… fuit. || Grandma | …Grandma! There was a cheese. Now… it's history.
Avus | Fuit Īlium… et fuit cāseus! || Grandpa | Troy is no more… and the cheese is no more!
:::

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Ōlim puer eram. Parvus eram, sed multum edēbam.* Stage or step? | All stage. *Eram, eram, edēbam*: who Grandpa was and what he **used to** do (habit). *Multum*: a lot (Chapter 12's *Nōn multa, sed multum*). |
| *Nox erat. Omnēs dormiēbant… stertēbat… dormiēbat.* | Vergil's *Nox erat*, then a whole snoring house. Pure scenery. Nothing has happened yet. |
| *Ego in lectō cāseum cōgitābam.* (*Lectus*: bed. *Cōgitō*: think, Chapter 12's *Cōgitō, ergō sum*.) | Imperfect: "I **kept thinking** about the cheese." Latin says "think the cheese" with an object. Still the film. |
| *Quid tum?* What does the question demand? | A step. And Grandpa answers with perfects: ***iī, cēpī, ēdī***. The story moves. |
| *Lātrāvit! … vēnit et clāmāvit … vīdit.* | Four steps in a row, all perfect: bark, come, shout, see. Like Caesar's *vēnī, vīdī, vīcī*. |
| *"Quis cāseum ēdit?"* Present or perfect? | Perfect: long ē in *ēdit*, "Who **ate** the cheese?" (Present *edit*, short e, would be "Who is eating…?") |
| *Sed māter mea sapiēns erat.* Why *erat* in the middle of the steps? | It's a description, not an event: how his mother **was**. The stage can be repainted between steps. |
| *Bona fābula fuit!* Why *fuit*, not *erat*? | Dad sums up the whole story, now finished: "That **was** a good story." Closed fact. |
| *Tū fābulam nārrābās… et ego cāseum edēbam.* Two imperfects together. What does that picture? | Two films running **at the same time**: while he was telling, she was eating. Then the payoff: *Cāseus erat. Nunc… fuit.* There was a cheese; now it's history. *Fuit Īlium… et fuit cāseus!* |

## 9. Sound check

:: Ōre rotundō. || With a well-rounded mouth. (Horace, *Ars Poetica* 323)

Audī et dīc.

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *dormiēbat · dormīvit*? | dor-mi-AY-bat · dor-MEE-wit. Stress (emphasis) on the long penult each time. |
| *edēbat · ēdit · edit*? | e-DAY-bat · AY-dit · E-dit. The long ē is the whole difference between "ate" and "eats." |
| *erat · fuit · fuērunt · fuimus*? | E-rat · FU-it · fu-AY-runt · FU-i-mus. |
| *lātrābat · lātrāvit · cucurrit · mānsērunt · vīxērunt*? | lah-TRAH-bat · lah-TRAH-wit · ku-KUR-rit · mahn-SAY-runt · wee-KSAY-runt (*x* = ks). |
| *ōlim · subitō · deinde · semel · cotīdiē · diū*? | OH-lim · SU-bi-toh · DAYN-de (*ei* glides into one syllable, as poets scan it) · SE-mel · ko-TEE-di-ay · DI-oo. |
| *fābula · nārrā · Fuimus Trōes, fuit Īlium*? | FAH-bu-la · NAHR-rah (double r, both sounded) · FU-i-mus TROH-es (short e: *Trōes* is a Greek-style plural), FU-it EE-li-um. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Nārrandō discimus. || We learn by telling. (built on Chapter 25's *Dīcendō discimus*)

1. Pairs, fast: *dormiēbat / dormīvit · edēbat / ēdit · lātrābat / lātrāvit · currēbant / cucurrērunt · erat / fuit*.
2. Say the Law of Erat and Fuit in your own words, then *Nox erat* and *Fuimus Trōes, fuit Īlium.*
3. Stage, then step: *Avus dormiēbat. Subitō canis lātrāvit.* Make three more pairs of your own.
4. Habit vs once: *Cotīdiē natābam. Heri semel natāvī.*
5. Trying: *Avus cāseum capiēbat, sed Avia eum cēpit.*
6. Tell the gull story with both cameras: *In lītore sedēbāmus… Subitō avis vēnit…*
7. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part. Then hide the Latin and rebuild it. Ask *Quid tum?* at every pause.
