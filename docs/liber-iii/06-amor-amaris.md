# Caput XXX: Amor, Amāris

*Liber III, Fābulae · Chapter 30*

*In which the cheese stops doing things and starts having things done to it. Since Chapter 4 every subject has been busy: Marcus eats, Grandpa loves, the dog steals. Today the subject sits still and receives: "the cheese is loved," "the cheese is being eaten," "the cheese will be guarded." Latin does it with one new set of endings, mostly marked by an* r: amor, amāris, amātur… *And the title is a pun you can finally read: Chapter 4's* Amor *is "Love," and* amor *is also "I am loved." You will learn to say who did it (*ab Avō, *"by Grandpa") and what it was done with (*manū, *"by hand"). Two old lines crack open: Chapter 3's* Nōn semper ea sunt quae videntur *and Chapter 18's* Possunt, quia posse videntur. *Speaking goal: say what is done to whom, by whom, and with what, in the present, past, and future.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Cāseus ab Avō amātur.* · *Ā quō?* · *Cāseus manū portātur.* · *Vidēris fessus.* · *Mihi vidētur…* · *Crās cāseus ā mē emētur.* · *Avus amārī vult.* · *Loquerisne Latīnē?*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** voice (does the subject do the action, or receive it?); active (the subject does it); passive (the subject receives it); agent (the doer in a passive sentence); ablative of agent (*ā/ab* + the doer); ablative of means (the plain ablative of the tool)
    - **Forms:** *amor, amāris, amātur, amāmur, amāminī, amantur* · *habeor, legor, audior, capior* · *amābar* · *amābor, amāberis* · *legar, legēris* · *amārī, habērī, audīrī, legī, capī*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Passive Endings · the Law of the Doer and the Tool · the Law of the Passive Clock
    - **Words:** *laudō, culpō, appellō, mūtō* (all *-āre*); *videor, vidērī*; *mihi vidētur*
    - **Reading:** *Ā quō editur?* (a new cheese, a midnight noise, and a dog with a familiar name)
    - **Speaking:** turn "Grandpa eats the cheese" into "the cheese is eaten by Grandpa," in three tenses

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep Chapter 16's time signs and Chapter 17's two futures in your head: today they don't change at all. Only the endings do.

## 1. *Amor*: a noun, and a verb

:: Omnia vincit Amor. || Love conquers all. (Vergil, *Eclogues* 10.69; Chapter 4)

Avus cāseum amat. Cāseus ab Avō amātur.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 4's motto: *Omnia vincit Amor.* Which word is the subject, and what is it? | ***Amor***, "Love," a noun (*amor, amōris*, m., the third family). *Omnia* is the object. |
| Chapter 5: *amō* = "I love." *Avus cāseum amat.* Who does the loving? What receives it? | ***Avus*** does it: the subject, nominative. ***Cāseum*** receives it: the object, accusative. |
| Turn it around in English: "The cheese is loved by Grandpa." Which word is the subject now? Is it doing anything? | "The cheese" is now the subject, but it does nothing: it **receives** the loving. Grandpa has been pushed into a "by" phrase. |
| Plain English: **voice** tells whether the subject does the action or receives it. Subject does it: **active** voice (every verb you have learned so far). Subject receives it: **passive** voice (Latin *passīvus*, "putting up with," "having it done to you"; English "is loved," "is being eaten"). Which voice is "Grandpa loves the cheese"? And "The cheese is loved by Grandpa"? | "Grandpa loves the cheese": **active**. "The cheese is loved by Grandpa": **passive**. Same event, seen from the other end. |
| English needs two or three words: "is loved." Latin needs one. *Cāseus amātur.* Compare Chapter 5's *amat*. What was added? | ***-ur*** on the end: *amat* → ***amātur***, "loves" → "**is loved**." (The *a* is short in *amat*, before a final *-t*, and long in *amātur*.) |
| "Grandpa and Grandma are loved." Compare *amant*. | ***Avus et Avia amantur.*** *-nt* → *-ntur*. The passive's favorite letter is ***r***. |
| Now "I love" is *amō*. Guess "I am loved." (*-ō* gives way to *-or*.) | ***Amor!*** The same spelling as Chapter 4's "Love." The title *Amor, amāris* is "I am loved, you are loved." Which one you mean, the noun or the verb, the sentence tells you: *Amor vincit* (Love conquers) · *Ab Avō amor* (I am loved by Grandpa). |

## 2. *Amor, amāris, amātur*

:: Amor, amāris, amātur. || I am loved, you are loved, he (she, it) is loved. (teaching chant; the chapter's title)

Amor. Amāris. Amāmur. Amāminī!

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "You love" is *amās*. "You are loved": the *-s* grows to *-ris*. | ***Amāris.*** a-MAH-ris: "you are loved." The title's second word. |
| "We love" is *amāmus*. "We are loved": *-mus* turns to *-mur*. | ***Amāmur.*** "We are loved." |
| "Y'all love" is *amātis*. "Y'all are loved" is the one odd ending, with no *r* at all: *-minī*. | ***Amāminī.*** a-MAH-mi-nee: "you all (y'all) are loved." Just learn it. |
| Chant it, then chant the active beside it. | ***Amor, amāris, amātur, amāmur, amāminī, amantur.*** Active: *amō, amās, amat, amāmus, amātis, amant.* Side by side: *-ō/-or · -s/-ris · -t/-tur · -mus/-mur · -tis/-minī · -nt/-ntur.* |
| Chapter 28's *servō* ("save, keep"). The lifeboat arrives: "We are saved!" Then to the others: "Y'all are saved!" | ***Servāmur!*** · ***Servāminī!*** |
| Tell Grandpa "You are loved!" Then ask, worried, "Am I loved?" (Chapter 2's *-ne*.) | ***Amāris, Ave!*** · ***Amorne?*** (Chapter 9: the vocative of *avus* is *ave*, short e; *avē* with a long ē is the greeting.) |

!!! success "The Law of the Passive Endings"
    Keep the stem and the vowel; swap the active ending for the passive one:

    | | active | passive |
    |---|---|---|
    | I | *-ō* (or *-m*) | ***-r*** |
    | you | *-s* | ***-ris*** |
    | he, she, it | *-t* | ***-tur*** |
    | we | *-mus* | ***-mur*** |
    | y'all | *-tis* | ***-minī*** |
    | they | *-nt* | ***-ntur*** |

    Say it like a drum: ***r, ris, tur · mur, minī, ntur.*** Five of the six have an *r*. A vowel right before a final *-r* is short (*amor*), just as before a final *-m* or *-t* (Chapter 16).

## 3. *Ā quō?* By whom?

:: Laudātur ab hīs, culpātur ab illīs. || He is praised by these, blamed by those. (Horace, *Satires* 1.2.11)

Cāseus ab Avō amātur. Ā quō? Ab Avō!

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "The cheese is loved **by Grandpa**." Latin's "by" for a person is Chapter 7's *ā/ab* ("away from") + the ablative. *Cāseus ab Avō amātur.* Why *ab*, not *ā*? | *Avō* starts with a vowel, and a vowel needs the full ***ab*** (Chapter 7). Before a consonant it usually shrinks: ***ā Marcō***. |
| Plain English: the doer in a passive sentence is the **agent** (Latin *agō*, "I do"; the agent is "the one doing it"). *Ā/ab* + the ablative for the doer is called the **ablative of agent**. In *Cāseus ā Marcō portātur*, find the agent. | ***Ā Marcō***, "by Marcus." Marcus carries; the cheese is carried. |
| Flip an active sentence: *Marcus Quīntum spectat.* (*Spectō*: watch, Chapter 26.) Three steps: the object becomes the subject, the subject goes into *ā/ab*, the verb goes passive. | ***Quīntus ā Marcō spectātur.*** "Quīntus is watched by Marcus." *Quīntum* → *Quīntus*; *Marcus* → *ā Marcō*; *spectat* → *spectātur*. |
| *Līberī Avum amant.* Flip it. Careful with the verb. | ***Avus ā līberīs amātur.*** The verb agrees with its **new** subject, *Avus*, so it is singular, even though the lovers are many. |
| Chapter 29's *quō*, with *ā* in front: "By whom is the cheese being carried?" Answer: "By Cornēlia." | ***Ā quō cāseus portātur?*** · ***Ā Cornēliā.*** (English "is carried" and "is being carried" are both the Latin present passive.) |
| The motto: *Laudātur ab hīs, culpātur ab illīs.* (*Laudō*: praise. *Culpō*: blame, from Chapter 9's *culpa*. *Hīs, illīs*: Chapter 19.) Find the two agents. | "He is **praised by these**, **blamed by those**." ***Ab hīs*** and ***ab illīs*** are the agents (*ab* before *h*). Horace's man throws money around so no one will call him stingy: some praise him, some blame him. |
| Can an animal be the agent? *Avus ā cane amātur.* | Yes. A living doer, person or animal, takes ***ā/ab***: "Grandpa is loved by the dog." (*Cane*: ablative of *canis*, Chapter 15.) |

## 4. *Manū*: by what?

:: Sōlā fidē. || By faith alone. (a slogan of the Protestant Reformation; *fidēs*: Chapter 23)

Cāseus manū portātur. Nōn "ā manū": manū!

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Marcus cāseum manū portat.* (*Manū*: Chapter 23's *manus*.) What does *manū* tell you, and is there a preposition? | "Marcus carries the cheese **with his hand**." *Manū* is the tool, and it is a **plain ablative**: no *ā*, no *cum*. |
| Plain English: the plain ablative of the tool or the means is the **ablative of means** (old-school "ablative of instrument"): "by means of, with, by." In the passive: *Cāseus ā Marcō manū portātur.* Find the agent and the means. | ***Ā Marcō***: agent (a person, with *ā*). ***Manū***: means (a thing, no preposition). "The cheese is carried by Marcus by hand." |
| English says "with" for two different things. Chapter 7's *cum Marcō* and today's *manū*. Which one takes *cum*? | ***Cum*** is for company: "together with Marcus." The **tool** takes no preposition: *manū*, "with (using) a hand." *Cum manū* would make the hand your walking buddy. |
| *Avus ab Aviā aquā lavātur.* (*Lavō*: wash, Chapter 21. *Aqua*: water.) Sort the two ablatives. | ***Ab Aviā***: agent, a person. ***Aquā***: means, a thing. "Grandpa is being washed by Grandma with water." |
| Caesar, right after Chapter 15's *Gallia est omnis dīvīsa in partēs trēs*, names the third people: *quī ipsōrum linguā Celtae, nostrā Gallī appellantur.* (*Appellō*: call, name. *Lingua*: tongue, so also "language," Chapter 14. *Nostrā* = *nostrā linguā*.) | "who **in their own language** are called Celts, **in ours** Gauls." *Appellantur*: "they are called." *Linguā, nostrā*: plain ablatives, no preposition: the language is the means of the naming. (Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.1.) |
| The motto *Sōlā fidē*: which ablative, and why no preposition? | Ablative of **means**: "by faith alone." Faith is a thing, not a doer, so no *ā*. (*Sōlā* agrees with *fidē*, Chapter 22's *sōlus*.) |

!!! success "The Law of the Doer and the Tool"
    **The doer** (a person or an animal) → ***ā/ab* + ablative**: the ablative of agent. *Cāseus **ab Avō** editur.*
    **The tool** (a thing used) → **plain ablative**, no preposition: the ablative of means. *Cāseus **manū** capitur.*
    **Company** (someone along with you) → ***cum* + ablative** (Chapter 7). *Cāseum **cum Avō** edō.*
    Ask: *Ā quō?* (by whom?) or *Quō?* (with what?)

## 5. *Videor*: the second family

:: Nōn semper ea sunt quae videntur. || Things are not always what they seem. (Phaedrus 4.2.5; Chapter 3)

Habeor, habēris, habētur… Vidēris fessus!

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Same endings on Chapter 5's *habeō*. Chant "I am held, you are held…" Which vowel runs through? | ***Habeor, habēris, habētur, habēmur, habēminī, habentur.*** Long *ē* all the way, except short before *-ntur* (and in *habeor*, where *e* sits before another vowel). |
| Chapter 16's *Docēbat eōs*: *doceō*, teach. "The students are taught by the teacher." | ***Discipulī ā magistrō docentur.*** |
| *Timeō*, fear (Chapter 26). Flip *Fēlēs canem timet*, "The cat fears the dog." (*Fēlēs*: Chapter 15.) | ***Canis ā fēle timētur.*** "The dog is feared by the cat." |
| *Videō*, see. What is *videor*? And Chapter 3's motto said *quae videntur* meant "what they seem." Why? | *Videor* = "I am seen," so *videntur* = "they are seen," and in Latin **"to be seen" is "to seem."** Things are not always what they **are seen (to be)**. |
| Chapter 18's motto, *Possunt, quia posse videntur*, said *videntur* was "a form for later." It's later. | The passive of *videō*: "They can, because they **seem** to be able" (to themselves): they think they can. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 5.231.) |
| Two everyday chunks: "You seem tired, Grandpa." And "It seems to me…" for giving your opinion. | ***Vidēris fessus, Ave.*** (*vidēris*, long ē: 2nd family) · ***Mihi vidētur…*** "It seems to me," "I think": *Canis, mihi vidētur, fūr est!* "The dog, it seems to me, is a thief!" |

## 6. *Legitur*: the third family and *-iō*

:: Amāre et sapere vix deō concēditur. || To love and be wise at once is hardly granted even to a god. (Publilius Syrus, *Sentences* A22)

Liber legitur. Cāseus editur. Ab omnibus audior!

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Marcus librum legit.* Flip it. (Just add the *-ur*.) | ***Liber ā Marcō legitur.*** "The book is (being) read by Marcus." *Legit* → *legitur*. And *legunt* → ***leguntur***, *legimus* → ***legimur***, *legitis* → ***legiminī***. |
| "You are read." *Legis* + *-ris*? Latin won't say *legiris*: a short *i* right before *r* turns to *e* (the same *e* you hear in *leg-e-re*). | ***Legeris.*** LE-ge-ris: "you are read." |
| Chant the third family's passive. | ***Legor, legeris, legitur, legimur, legiminī, leguntur.*** |
| The cheese line at last. *Avus cāseum edit.* Flip it. Then ask "By whom is the cheese being eaten?" | ***Cāseus ab Avō editur.*** · ***Ā quō cāseus editur?*** |
| *Audiō* and *capiō* keep their *i* where the active has it (*audiunt, capiunt*). Chant both. | ***Audior, audīris, audītur, audīmur, audīminī, audiuntur.*** · ***Capior, caperis, capitur, capimur, capiminī, capiuntur.*** (*Caperis*: the *i* → *e* before *r* again.) |
| Dad starts singing in the garden and announces: "I am heard by everyone!" (*Omnēs*: Chapter 20.) | ***Ab omnibus audior!*** The children: *Ēheu! Ab omnibus audīris, Pater.* "Alas! You **are** heard by everyone, Dad." |
| The motto: *Amāre et sapere vix deō concēditur.* (*Sapere*: be wise, Chapter 6's *Sapere audē*. *Vix*: hardly. *Concēdō*: grant.) What is the subject of *concēditur*? | The two infinitives, *amāre et sapere* ("to love and to be wise"), like Chapter 20's *Dulce et decōrum est prō patriā morī*. "**Is granted** hardly even to a god (*deō*, dative)." |

!!! success "The present passive, every family"
    | | *amō* | *habeō* | *legō* | *audiō* | *capiō* |
    |---|---|---|---|---|---|
    | I am … | *amor* | *habeor* | *legor* | *audior* | *capior* |
    | you are … | *amāris* | *habēris* | ***legeris*** | *audīris* | ***caperis*** |
    | he, she, it is … | *amātur* | *habētur* | *legitur* | *audītur* | *capitur* |
    | we are … | *amāmur* | *habēmur* | *legimur* | *audīmur* | *capimur* |
    | y'all are … | *amāminī* | *habēminī* | *legiminī* | *audīminī* | *capiminī* |
    | they are … | *amantur* | *habentur* | *leguntur* | *audiuntur* | *capiuntur* |

    Only the bold ones surprise: a short *i* before *r* turns to *e*.

## 7. *Amābar*: was being loved

:: Baptīzābantur ab illō. || They were being baptized by him. (Mark 1:5, Vulgate; the crowds coming to John the Baptist, *Iōannēs*)

Heri cāseus ab Avō edēbātur.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 16: *amābam* = "I was loving," with the time sign *-bā-*. Keep the sign, change the ending: *-m* → *-r*. "I was being loved"? | ***Amābar.*** (Short *a* before the final *-r*.) |
| Chant it. The endings are today's; the *-bā-* is Chapter 16's. | ***Amābar, amābāris, amābātur, amābāmur, amābāminī, amābantur.*** |
| Chapter 17's imperfects (*legēbam, audiēbam, capiēbam, habēbam*) do the same. "The book was being read by Marcus." | ***Liber ā Marcō legēbātur.*** And *audiēbātur, capiēbātur, habēbātur*. |
| Chapter 26's camera: the imperfect is the film. "Yesterday the cheese was being eaten by Grandpa…" (and then something happened). | ***Heri cāseus ab Avō edēbātur…*** The film is rolling: the eating was in progress. |
| Mark 1:5 tells of the crowds who came to John the Baptist, *Iōannēs*: *baptīzābantur ab illō.* (*Baptīzō*: baptize.) Tense, voice, agent? | Imperfect (*-bā-*), passive (*-ntur*), agent ***ab illō*** (Chapter 19's *ille*): "they **were being baptized by him**," over and over, a crowd at a time. |

## 8. *Amābor*: will be loved

:: Petite, et dabitur vōbīs. || Ask, and it will be given to you all. (Matthew 7:7, Vulgate)

Crās cāseus ā mē emētur!

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 16's *amābō tē*: "I will love you." Turn the *-bō* into *-bor*. | ***Amābor***: "I will be loved." |
| "You will love" is *amābis*. "You will be loved"? Remember what a short *i* does before *r*. | ***Amāberis*** (a-MAH-be-ris), not *amābiris*. Then: *amābitur, amābimur, amābiminī, amābuntur.* |
| Chapter 17's other future (*legam, legēs, leget*…): same swap. "I will be read," "you will be read," "it will be read." | ***Legar, legēris, legētur***, then *legēmur, legēminī, legentur*. Likewise ***audiar, audiēris*** and ***capiar, capiēris***. |
| A trap made of one macron: *legeris* and *legēris*. Which is "you are read," which "you will be read"? | ***Legeris*** (short e, LE-ge-ris): **present**. ***Legēris*** (long ē, le-GAY-ris): **future**. Only the long vowel tells them apart, in the book and in your mouth. |
| The whole verse: *Petite, et dabitur vōbīs; quaerite, et inveniētis; pulsāte, et aperiētur vōbīs.* (Chapter 2's motto *Quaerite et inveniētis* in the middle. *Petō*: ask. *Pulsō*: knock. *Aperiō*: open.) Find the two future passives. | ***Dabitur*** ("it will be given," from *dō*: short *a*) and ***aperiētur*** ("it will be opened," like *audiētur*). "Ask, and it will be given to y'all; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to y'all." (Matthew 7:7.) |
| Promise Grandpa: "Tomorrow a cheese will be bought by me!" (*Emō*: Chapter 6.) | ***Crās cāseus ā mē emētur!*** *Emō* is third family, so the *-ē-* future: *emētur*. |

!!! success "The Law of the Passive Clock"
    The **time signs don't change**; only the endings do.
    **Present:** *amātur, legitur* · **Imperfect:** *-bā-* → *amābātur, legēbātur* · **Future:** *-bō, -bi-* → *amābor, amābitur* (*-bō, -bi-* families) or *-ar, -ē-* → *legar, legētur* (the rest).
    Two twists: *-bis* → ***-beris*** (*amāberis*), and the third family's ***legeris*** (now) vs ***legēris*** (later).

## 9. *Amārī*: to be loved

:: Amāre et amārī dulce mihi erat. || To love and to be loved was sweet to me. (Augustine, *Confessions* 3.1.1)

Avus amārī vult. Cāseus edī nōn vult!

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Amāre* = "to love." "To be loved" swaps the final *-e* for a long *-ī*. Also *habēre*, *audīre*. | ***Amārī*** (a-MAH-ree) · ***habērī*** · ***audīrī***. |
| The third family and *-iō* are shorter: *legere* → *legī*, *capere* → *capī*. Just *-ī* on the stem. | ***Legī***: "to be read." ***Capī***: "to be taken." |
| Another one-macron trap: *legī* and Chapter 25's *lēgī*. | ***Legī*** (short e): "**to be read**." ***Lēgī*** (long ē): "**I read**, I have read" (the perfect). |
| Chapter 18's complementary infinitive. "Grandpa wants to be loved." "The cheese does not want to be eaten." | ***Avus amārī vult.*** · ***Cāseus edī nōn vult!*** (*Edere* → *edī*.) |
| The motto: *Amāre et amārī dulce mihi erat.* Which infinitive is active, which passive? | ***Amāre***: active, "to love." ***Amārī***: passive, "to be loved." "To love and to be loved was sweet to me." (*Dulce*: Chapter 20's neuter, with infinitives as subject.) |
| A proverb from the 1500s: *Mundus vult dēcipī.* (*Mundus*: world, Chapter 14's *caput mundī*. *Dēcipiō*: deceive, built like *capiō*.) | "The world **wants to be deceived**." *Dēcipī* follows *capī*. (Sebastian Franck recorded it in the 1530s; it is often pinned on Petronius, who never wrote it.) |

## 10. Reading tips and look-alikes

:: Tempora mūtantur, nōs et mūtāmur in illīs. || Times change, and we change with them. (a proverb of the 1500s, often wrongly credited to Ovid)

Amor an amor? Amāre an amāre?

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The motto word for word: *mūtantur* (Chapter 15's *mūtō*, change) is "they are changed." Why does good English say "times **change**"? | English often drops the "are …ed": "times are changed, and we are changed in them" sounds stiff, so we say "**change**." A Latin passive can be English "change," "move," "wash": *Avus lavātur*, "Grandpa is washed," or simply "Grandpa washes" (Chapter 21's *Avus sē lavat*). |
| *Amor vincit omnia.* · *Ab omnibus amor.* Which *amor* is the noun? | The first: "**Love** conquers all." The second: "**I am loved** by everyone." The verb has an agent; the noun has a job in its sentence. |
| A reader's warning: poets sometimes write the "you" passive with *-re* instead of *-ris*: *amāre* = *amāris*, "you are loved." But *amāre* is also "to love"! How do you choose? | Usually it is "to love." If "to love" makes no sense and "you" fits, try "you are loved." (Likewise *amābere* = *amāberis*.) |
| A handful of verbs wear passive endings but mean something active (grammar books call them *deponent*, "laying aside"; Chapter 43). One useful chunk now: *Loquerisne Latīnē?* | "**Do you speak** Latin?" Answer: ***Latīnē loquor!*** "I speak Latin!" (*Loquor, loqueris, loquitur*: passive endings, active meaning.) If "is being …ed" makes no sense, it may be one of these. |
| Name voice, tense, person, and number: *amābant* · *amābantur* · *legētur* · *legitur*. | *Amābant*: active, imperfect, "they were loving." *Amābantur*: passive, imperfect, "they were being loved." *Legētur*: passive, future, "it will be read." *Legitur*: passive, present, "it is read." |
| Chapter 25's motto *Factum est* ("It is done") hinted at a passive. What about "the cheese **was eaten**," a finished snapshot? | Not with today's endings. The finished passive uses Chapter 25's fourth key plus *sum*: that is Chapter 31, *Amātus est*. Today: present, imperfect, future. |

## 11. Fix it

:: Errās? Corrigeris! || Made a mistake? You get corrected! (teaching line; *corrigō*, correct)

Ā quō? Quō? Cum quō?

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "The cheese is loved by Grandpa": *Cāseum ab Avō amātur.* | ***Cāseus ab Avō amātur.*** The passive subject is nominative. |
| "The cheese is carried by Marcus": *Cāseus Marcō portātur.* | ***Cāseus ā Marcō portātur.*** A person doing it needs *ā/ab*. |
| "The cheese is carried by hand": *Cāseus ā manū portātur.* | ***Cāseus manū portātur.*** A tool takes the plain ablative. |
| "Grandpa is loved by the children": *Avus ā līberīs amantur.* | ***Avus ā līberīs amātur.*** The verb agrees with *Avus*. |
| "Y'all are loved": *Amātis.* | ***Amāminī.*** *-tis* → *-minī*. |
| "You will be loved": *Amābiris.* | ***Amāberis.*** Short *i* → *e* before *r*. |
| "Grandpa wants to be loved": *Avus amāre vult.* | ***Avus amārī vult.*** *Amāre* is "to love"; "to be loved" is *amārī*. |

## 12. Lectiō: *Ā quō editur?*

:: Cāseus ab omnibus amātur. || Cheese is loved by everyone. (teaching line)

Nox est. Aliquid audītur. Ā quō cāseus editur?

Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. As you read, find every passive verb, and for each one ask *Ā quō?* (by whom?) and *Quō?* (with what?).

::: dialogue lectio
Avia | Ecce cāseus novus! Ab omnibus amātur. || Grandma | Look, a new cheese! It's loved by everyone.
Avus | Et ego ab omnibus amor! || Grandpa | And I'm loved by everyone!
Avia | Tū? Cāseus ā tē amātur, Ave… sed tū ā cāseō nōn amāris! || Grandma | You? The cheese is loved by you, Grandpa… but you are not loved by the cheese!
Māter | Hodiē cāseus in culīnā servātur. Nōn ab Avō editur! || Mom | Today the cheese is kept in the kitchen. It is not being eaten by Grandpa!
Pater | Et ā cane servābitur. Cāseus nōbīs fīdus est! || Dad | And it will be guarded by the dog. Cheese is loyal to us!
Cāseus | Bau! || Cheese | Woof!
Iōannēs | Nox est… Aliquid audītur! || John | It's night… Something is heard!
Cornēlia | Quid audītur? || Cornēlia | What is heard?
Marcus | Capsa in culīnā tangitur! || Marcus | The box in the kitchen is being touched!
Quīntus | Ā quō tangitur? Ā fūre? || Quīntus | By whom is it being touched? By a thief?
Avia | Ēheu! Avus in lectō nōn est! || Grandma | Oh no! Grandpa isn't in bed!

Avia | Servās? Cāseus dentibus tuīs servātur? || Grandma | Guarding it? The cheese is being guarded by your teeth?
Cāseus | Bau! Bau! || Cheese | Woof! Woof!

Quīntus | Nōn manū, sed dentibus! || Quīntus | Not by hand, but by teeth!
Pater | Cāseus ab omnibus amābātur… || Dad | The cheese was loved by everyone…
Māter | …et nunc ab ūnō editur. || Mom | …and now it's being eaten by one.
Omnēs | In stomachō… || Everyone | In the stomach of…
Avus | …Cāseī. Ēheu! Nōn ab omnibus amor! || Grandpa | …Cheese. Alas! I'm not loved by everyone!
Iōannēs | Ā nōbīs amāris, Ave! Crās cāseus novus ā mē emētur. || John | You're loved by us, Grandpa! Tomorrow a new cheese will be bought by me.

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Ab omnibus amātur.* · *Et ego ab omnibus amor!* Find the verbs and the agents. | *Amātur*: "it is loved." *Amor*: "I am loved" (the verb, not the noun!). Agent both times: ***ab omnibus***, "by everyone." |
| *Et ā cane servābitur.* Tense? Voice? | Future (*-bi-*), passive: "it **will be guarded** by the dog." The agent is an animal, so *ā cane*. |
| *Cāseus manū Avī capitur!* Is *manū* the agent? | No. *Manū* is the **means** (plain ablative, no *ā*); *Avī* is the owner, genitive: "by Grandpa's **hand**." |
| *Cāseus dentibus tuīs servātur?* What is Grandma saying? | "The cheese is being guarded **by your teeth**?" *Dentibus* (Chapter 15's *dēns*): plain ablative of means. She doesn't believe him. |
| *Cāseus ā Cāseō editur!* Explain the joke. | The first *Cāseus* is the cheese, the subject. *Ā Cāseō* is the dog named Cheese (Chapter 14), the agent. Cheese is eaten by Cheese. |
| *Crās cāseus novus ā mē emētur.* And Grandpa's *Amor! Amor!* | "Tomorrow a new cheese **will be bought** by me" (*emētur*, future passive). *Amor!*: "I am loved!", the title, said by Grandpa. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Ā tē dīcitur! || It is said by you! (teaching line)

1. Chant the passive of *amō* in all three tenses: *amor…*, *amābar…*, *amābor, amāberis…* Then *legor, legeris…* and *legar, legēris…*
2. Say the three Laws in your own words: Passive Endings (*r, ris, tur · mur, minī, ntur*), Doer and Tool (*ā/ab* vs plain ablative vs *cum*), Passive Clock (time signs stay, endings change).
3. Flip five sentences aloud, active to passive: *Avus cāseum edit · Marcus librum legit · Līberī Avum amant · Canis fēlem timet · Avia Avum aquā lavat.*
4. Ask *Ā quō?* and *Quō?* about each one, and answer.
5. Say "to love / to be loved," "to read / to be read," and the two traps *legeris/legēris*, *legī/lēgī*.
6. The real lines: *Laudātur ab hīs, culpātur ab illīs.* · *Nōn semper ea sunt quae videntur.* · *Petite, et dabitur vōbīs.* · *Amāre et amārī dulce mihi erat.* · *Tempora mūtantur.*
7. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part (the dog too). Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
