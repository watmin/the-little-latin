# Caput XXXV: Ablātīvus

*Liber III, Fābulae · Chapter 35*

*In which the busiest case in Latin gets a chapter of its own. Chapter 7 met the ablative as the "where / from / with" case and promised that it "picks up more jobs in later books." It has: the hour and the price (Chapter 22), the doer and the tool (Chapter 30), the "than" (Chapter 33). Chapter 32 cracked its name,* ablātum, *"carried away," and Chapter 34 left two loose ends,* magnopere *and* quōmodo, *with a note: "Chapter 35 explains that ablative." Today you'll map every job you already know, add six more (with whom, how, why, by how much, in what respect, what kind), and learn one habit that sorts them all: ask the ablative its question. No new endings at all. Along the way: the three Latin honors on American diplomas, what the priest says at Mass and what you say back, what Julius Caesar looked like, and a doctor who makes a house call for Grandpa's stomach. Speaking goal: say who you're with, how you do it, why, by how much, and what someone looks like.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Cum Avō ambulō. · Magnā cum cūrā! · Nūllō modō! · Gaudiō clāmō! · Multō maior · Paulō post · Nātū maximus · Canis magnīs auribus*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** ablative of accompaniment ("with whom") · ablative of manner ("how") · ablative of cause ("why") · ablative of degree of difference ("by how much"; old-school "measure of difference") · ablative of respect ("in what respect"; old-school "specification") · ablative of description ("what kind"; old-school "quality")
    - **Forms:** none new! Chapter 23's five ablatives do all the work: *-ā, -ō, -e/-ī, -ū, -ē · -īs, -ibus*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Three Old Jobs · the Law of the Three Withs · the Law of the Six Questions
    - **Words:** *cūra, laus, gaudium, metus, īra, dolor, causa, modus, virtūs, auris, barba, claudus, nimius, medicus, tremō* · *multō, paulō, paulō post, nihilō minus, nūllō modō, nātū*
    - **Reading:** *Nimiō cāseō!* (Grandpa's stomach pays for Chapter 34, a doctor with a big beard makes a house call, and the cure ends up in the wrong stomach)
    - **Speaking:** say who's with you, how, why, by how much, and what anyone looks like

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep two old friends close: Chapter 23's **Law of Five Families** (every ablative ending you'll need today) and Chapter 30's **Law of the Doer and the Tool**.

## 1. The carrying-away case

:: Auferō, auferre, abstulī, ablātum. || I carry away, to carry away, I carried away, carried away. (Chapter 32's four keys)

Cāsus ablātīvus: in, ex, ab, cum, sine… et multa alia.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 32 gave you *auferō, auferre, abstulī, **ablātum***, "carry away." Chapter 7's case is the *cāsus ablātīvus*. Read the blurb. What does the name say, and which job earned it? | "The **carrying-away case**." Its oldest job is "**from**": *ex hortō, ab Avō*, something carried away from somewhere. (*Multa alia*: "many other things." Hold that thought.) |
| Chapter 7's Law: five little words take the ablative. Name them and their jobs. | ***In*** (where), ***ex/ē*** and ***ab/ā*** (from), ***cum*** (with), ***sine*** (without). Chapter 7 called it the "where / from / with" case and promised that it "picks up more jobs in later books." |
| Then the ablative started working with **no** little word at all. *Domō veniō. Rōmā veniō.* (Chapter 8) · *Hōrā tertiā veniō.* (Chapter 22) · *Cāseum ūnō dēnāriō ēmimus.* (Chapter 22) | "I come **from home**, **from Rome**" (place from) · "I'm coming **at the third hour**" (time when) · "We bought the cheese **for one denarius**" (price). Three jobs, no preposition. |
| Chapters 30 and 33: *Cāseus ab Avō editur.* · *Avus cāseum manū capit.* · *Marcus Quīntō altior est.* | "The cheese is eaten **by Grandpa**" (the doer: ablative of agent, with *ā/ab*) · "Grandpa takes the cheese **with his hand**" (the tool: ablative of means, plain) · "Marcus is taller **than Quīntus**" (ablative of comparison). |
| Count the jobs you already know. | **Nine**: where, from where, with, without, when, price, doer, tool, "than." The busiest case in Latin. Today adds six more, and a way to sort them all, with **no new endings**. |

## 2. Same endings, every family

:: Mēns sāna in corpore sānō. || A sound mind in a sound body. (Juvenal 10.356; Chapter 14)

Mēnsā, hortō, rēge, manū, diē.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 23's Law of Five Families. Read the blurb. What does each family's ablative singular end in? | ***-ā*** (*mēnsā*), ***-ō*** (*hortō*), ***-e*** (*rēge*; but ***-ī*** for the *mare* type, *marī*, Chapter 15), ***-ū*** (*manū*), ***-ē*** (*diē*). All vowels; all long except the third family's *-e*. |
| And the plural? | ***-īs*** in the first two families (*mēnsīs, hortīs*), ***-ibus*** in the other three (*rēgibus, manibus, diēbus*). The same as the dative plural: Chapter 11's Look-alikes and Chapter 13's Double Duty. |
| Adjectives come along in their own family's form. "With a big cheese." "With a brave king." "With a taller boy." | ***Cum cāseō magnō · cum rēge fortī · cum puerō altiōre.*** *Fortī* with *-ī* (Chapter 20: always); *altiōre* with *-e* (Chapter 33: not an i-stem). |
| Pronouns you've used: "with me," "with y'all," "in this (one)," "with whom?" | ***Mēcum*** (Chapter 7), ***vōbīscum*** (Chapter 21), ***hōc*** (Chapter 17's *In hōc signō*), ***quōcum?*** (Chapter 29). With these, *cum* is glued on the end. |
| The motto, Chapter 14's Juvenal: *Mēns sāna in corpore sānō.* Find the ablative and its job. | ***In corpore sānō***: *in* + ablative, **where**: "a sound mind **in a sound body**." Third family *-e*, second family *-ō*: same case, different letters (Chapter 20's Law of the Two Families). |

## 3. Three old jobs in one coat

:: Ubi? Unde? Quōcum? || Where? From where? With whom? (teaching line)

In hortō. Ex hortō. Cum amīcīs.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| A family secret. Latin's distant ancestor had **three** separate cases: one for "**from**," one for "**with / by means of**," one for "**in / at**." Latin poured all three into one: the ablative. Read the blurb. Sort the three. | ***Ex hortō***: from. ***Cum amīcīs***: with. ***In hortō***: in / at. Grammar books name the old three: the "ablative proper" (from), the "instrumental" (with / by), the "locative" (in / at). Chapter 8's locative *domī, Rōmae* is a survivor that never moved in. |
| Sort: *Rōmā veniō* · *hōrā tertiā* · *ab Avō editur*. | ***Rōmā***: from. ***Hōrā tertiā***: at (a point in time is an "at"). ***Ab Avō***: from: the doer is where the action comes **from**. |
| Sort: *manū capit* · *ūnō dēnāriō ēmimus*. | Both **with / by**: the tool you take it **with**; the coin you buy it **with**. A price is a tool for buying. |
| Chapter 33's *melle dulcior*, "sweeter than honey." Which old job? | **From**: "sweeter, starting from honey." Latin measures **from** the thing compared, so most grammars file the "than" ablative under "from." |
| Today's six new jobs: with whom, how, why, by how much, in what respect, what kind. Guess: which old job do most of them belong to? | Almost all to "**with / by**." That's why English "with" and "by" translate so many of them, and why today needs a Law of the Withs. (Only "why" straddles: "**from** fear" sounds like the old "from" job, and many grammars file it there.) |

!!! success "The Law of the Three Old Jobs"
    The ablative is three old cases wearing one coat:

    - **from**: *ex, ab, dē*; *domō, Rōmā*; the doer (*ā/ab*); the "than" (*melle dulcior*)
    - **with / by**: *cum*; the tool (*manū*); the price (*ūnō dēnāriō*); and most of today's new jobs
    - **in / at**: *in* + where; the hour, the day, the year (*hōrā tertiā, diē tertiā, annō Dominī*)

    When a bare ablative turns up, try "from," "with / by," "in / at," and keep the one that makes sense.

## 4. With whom: accompaniment

:: Dominus vōbīscum. Et cum spīritū tuō. || The Lord be with you all. And with your spirit. (the Mass)

Marcus cum Avō ambulat.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Read the blurb. Who's walking, and who is the company? | **Marcus** walks; **Grandpa** comes along. *Cum* + the person who goes with you is the **ablative of accompaniment** ("with whom": accompaniment is just "company"). Chapter 7's oldest friend, with its proper name at last. |
| *Marcus cum Avō ambulat* · *Marcus et Avus ambulant*. Why is the first verb singular? | Only **Marcus** is the subject; Grandpa is company, tucked into the ablative. With *et*, both are subjects, so the verb is plural. |
| "With me, with you, with us, with y'all, with himself." | ***Mēcum, tēcum, nōbīscum, vōbīscum, sēcum*** (Chapters 7 and 21). With these pronouns *cum* always goes on the **end**. |
| The motto. At Mass the priest says *Dominus vōbīscum*, and the people answer *Et cum spīritū tuō*. (*Spīritus, spīritūs*: spirit, breath; fourth family, Chapter 23.) | "**The Lord be with you all.**" "**And with your spirit.**" Two accompaniments: *vōbīscum* and *cum spīritū* (long *ū*: the fourth family's ablative). Church Latin says vo-BEES-kum; classical says wo-BEES-kum. |
| Company can be a thing you bring along. "John comes with the cheese." "Grandpa sleeps with the dog." | ***Iōannēs cum cāseō venit. · Avus cum cane dormit.*** (*Canis*, ablative *cane*.) |

## 5. How: manner

:: Abiit in montāna cum fēstīnātiōne. || She went off into the hill country in a hurry. (Luke 1:39, of Mary)

Sine cūrā? Minimē! Cum cūrā.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 7's *sine cūrā*, "without care" (English *sinecure*). Read the blurb. *Cūra, cūrae*: care, worry. What's *cum cūrā*, and which question does it answer? | "**With care**," so "**carefully**." It answers Chapter 34's *quōmodo?*, "**how?**" That's the **ablative of manner** ("how it's done"; same name in the old books). *Cum cūrā* = Chapter 34's *dīligenter*. |
| Add an adjective: "with **great** care." Romans love to sandwich *cum* in the middle. | ***Magnā cum cūrā*** (adjective, *cum*, noun), or with no *cum* at all: ***magnā cūrā***. The rule of thumb: with an adjective, *cum* may drop; with a bare noun, keep it (*cum cūrā*). |
| *Laus, laudis*, f.: praise (Chapter 30's *laudō*). American diplomas say *cum laude, magnā cum laude, summā cum laude*. (*Summus*: highest.) | "**With praise**," "**with great praise**," "**with highest praise**": honors, high honors, highest honors. Three ablatives of manner hanging on the wall. |
| Chapter 34 promised to explain *magnopere* and *quōmodo*. Pull them apart. (*Opus, operis*, n.: work, Chapter 14's Disguise. *Modus, modī*: way, manner.) | ***Magnō opere***, "with great work" → "**greatly**." ***Quō modō***, "in what way?" → "**how?**" Two ablatives of manner, glued into adverbs. Same kit: ***hōc modō***, "in this way," and ***nūllō modō***, "in no way": "**No way!**" |
| Luke on Mary, after the angel's news: *abiit in montāna cum fēstīnātiōne*. (*Abiit*: Chapter 32. *Montāna*: the hill country. *Fēstīnātiō, -ōnis*: haste, from Chapter 12's *Fēstīnā*.) | "**She went off into the hill country with haste**": in a hurry. *Cum fēstīnātiōne* tells **how** she went. |
| "Grandpa eats with great joy." (*Gaudium, gaudiī*: joy.) "Quīntus reads in silence." (Chapter 7's motto *silentium*; Romans say just *silentiō*.) | ***Avus magnō cum gaudiō edit*** (or *magnō gaudiō*). ***Quīntus silentiō legit.*** A few everyday words do manner alone; *silentiō* is the one to know. |

## 6. Three withs

:: Omnia mea mēcum portō. || I carry all my things with me. (Bias of Priene, via Cicero, *Paradoxes of the Stoics* 1.8; Chapter 20)

Cum Marcō. Cum cūrā. Manū.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Three "with"s. *Cāseum cum Marcō portō · Cāseum cum cūrā portō · Cāseum manū portō.* Which asks "with whom?", which "how?", which "with what?" | ***Cum Marcō***: with whom (company). ***Cum cūrā***: how (manner). ***Manū***: with what (the tool: Chapter 30's ablative of means), and **no *cum***. |
| Why no *cum* on the tool? | Chapter 30's Law of the Doer and the Tool: a tool is a plain ablative. *Cum* + a thing makes it a **companion**: *Marcus cum gladiō venit*, "Marcus comes with a sword (on him)." *Marcus gladiō pugnat*, "Marcus fights with (using) a sword." (*Pugnō*: fight, Chapter 34.) |
| *Baculum, baculī*: walking stick. "Grandpa walks with a stick, and with the dog." | ***Avus baculō ambulat, et cum cane.*** The stick is the tool (plain); the dog is company (*cum*). |
| Chapter 30's doer joins in: *Cāseus ā Marcō manū capitur.* | "The cheese is taken **by Marcus** (doer, *ā*) **with his hand** (tool, plain)." English "by" and "with" again; Latin keeps them apart. |
| The motto. Chapter 20's wise Bias, when his city fell and everyone else fled with armfuls of belongings, took nothing and said: *Omnia mea mēcum portō.* Which "with"? | ***Mēcum***: company. "I carry all my things **with me**": his wisdom goes everywhere he goes. |

!!! success "The Law of the Three Withs"
    English "with" has three Latin faces:

    | ask | Latin | example |
    |---|---|---|
    | **with whom?** (company) | *cum* + the person | *cum Avō · mēcum* |
    | **how?** (manner) | *cum* + a noun like care, joy, haste; with an adjective, *cum* may drop | *cum cūrā · magnā (cum) cūrā* |
    | **with what?** (tool, Chapter 30) | the plain ablative, **never** *cum* | *manū · baculō* |

    And "**by whom?**" (the doer) is *ā/ab*.

## 7. Why: cause

:: Rēgnī cupiditāte inductus. || Led on by desire for a kingdom. (Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.2, of Orgetorix)

Quīntus metū tremit. Avus gaudiō clāmat.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| New: *metus, metūs*, m.: fear (fourth family). *Tremō, tremere, tremuī*: tremble. Read the blurb: *Quīntus metū tremit.* Why does he tremble? | "**From fear**," "**with fear**." *Metū* tells **why**: the **ablative of cause** ("why"; same name in the old books). No preposition needed. |
| *Avus gaudiō clāmat.* Then: "Grandma shouts with anger." (*Īra, īrae*: anger.) "Marcus shouts with pain." (*Dolor, dolōris*, m.: pain, grief; Chapter 14's *doleō*.) | "Grandpa shouts **for joy**." ***Avia īrā clāmat. · Marcus dolōre clāmat.*** Feelings as causes: a plain ablative. |
| *Nimius, -a, -um*: too much (Chapter 33's *nimis*). *Aegrōtō, aegrōtāre*: be sick (Chapter 34's *aegrōtus*). "Grandpa is sick from too much cheese." | ***Avus nimiō cāseō aegrōtat.*** The cause is the cheese. (That's today's Lectiō title.) |
| The motto. Caesar on Orgetorix, the richest Helvetian, who plotted to rule all Gaul: *rēgnī cupiditāte inductus*. (*Rēgnum*: kingdom, Chapter 33. *Cupiditās, -ātis*: desire. *Inductus*: led on, a fourth-key adjective like Chapter 31's, from *indūcō*.) | "**Led on by desire for a kingdom.**" *Cupiditāte*: why he did it. Cause and tool blur here (the desire also *pushed* him); Romans didn't fuss, and you needn't either. |
| *Causa, causae*: cause, reason. Latin puts ***causā*** or ***grātiā*** (Chapter 11's *grātia*) **after** a genitive for "for the sake of." Crack *e.g.* = *exemplī grātiā* (*exemplum*: example) and the honorary degree *honōris causā* (*honor, honōris*). | "**For the sake of an example**": for example. "**For the sake of honor**": honorary. Two ablatives of cause, each after its genitive. |
| A handy "why?": ***Quā dē causā?***, "for what reason?" (Chapter 29's *quī, quae, quod* as a question word + *causā*; *dē* + ablative: "from, about," Chapter 34's "down from.") Ask it about Quīntus, and answer. | ***Quā dē causā Quīntus tremit? · Metū tremit!*** Without the question mark, *quā dē causā* means "**for this reason**": Caesar uses it that way in §9. |

## 8. By how much: degree of difference

:: Quantō magis vōs, modicae fideī? || How much more (will he clothe) you, you of little faith? (Matthew 6:30)

Multō maior. Paulō celerius.

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 33 left a chunk: *multō maior*, "much bigger." Read the blurb. *Multō* is the ablative of *multum*, "much." What does it measure? | **By how much** the two differ: "bigger **by much**." That's the **ablative of degree of difference** ("by how much"; old-school "measure of difference"). It sits on comparatives. |
| Chapter 34 left *paulum*, "a little." Its ablative is ***paulō***. "A little faster." "Much better." | ***Paulō celerius · multō melius.*** With a comparative, classical prose measures the gap with the ablative ***paulō, multō***, not *paulum, multum*. |
| Two ablatives in one line: *Marcus Quīntō pede altior est.* (*Pēs, pedis*: foot, Chapter 14.) | "**Marcus is taller than Quīntus by a foot.**" *Quīntō*: "than" (comparison). *Pede*: "by how much" (degree). |
| *Ante* and *post* also work as how-words: "before, earlier" and "after, later." *Paulō post. Multō ante.* | "**A little later.**" "**Long before**" ("earlier by much"). *Paulō post* is a storyteller's friend: put it next to Chapter 26's *deinde* and *tandem*. |
| *Nihilō minus*: Chapter 32's *nihilum* "nothing" + Chapter 34's *minus* "less." | "**Less by nothing**": "**nonetheless, all the same**." *Avus aegrōtat; nihilō minus cāseum vult.* "Grandpa is sick; all the same, he wants cheese." |
| The motto. Jesus on the lilies of the field: if God dresses the wild grass so beautifully, *quantō magis vōs, modicae fideī?* (*Quantus*: how great. *Modicus*: small. *Fidēs, fideī*: faith, Chapter 23.) | "**How much more** (will he clothe) **you, you of little faith?**" *Quantō*, "by how much," asks the degree. (*Modicae fideī* is a "what kind" genitive: §10.) |

## 9. In what respect

:: Hī omnēs linguā, īnstitūtīs, lēgibus inter sē differunt. || All these differ from one another in language, customs, and laws. (Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.1)

Marcus nātū maior est. Quīntus nātū minor.

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Read the blurb. *Nātū* means "by birth" (an old noun used only in this form). What's *Marcus nātū maior est*? | "Marcus is **older**": "bigger **by birth**." In what respect bigger? In birth. That's the **ablative of respect** ("in what respect"; old-school "specification"). *Quīntus nātū minor*: "younger." The oldest of a family is *nātū maximus*. |
| New: *claudus, -a, -um*, "lame, limping." Marcus hurt his nose in Chapter 34's race; suppose it had been his foot: *Marcus pede claudus est.* | "**Marcus is lame in the foot.**" *Pede*: in what respect he's lame. |
| Caesar again, a line after Chapter 15's *Gallia est omnis dīvīsa* (Gauls, Belgians, Aquitanians): *Hī omnēs linguā, īnstitūtīs, lēgibus inter sē differunt.* (*Īnstitūtum*: custom. *Inter sē*: from one another. *Differō*: differ, Chapter 32's *ferō* + *dis-*.) | "**All these differ from one another in language, customs, and laws.**" Three ablatives of respect in a row. |
| The same paragraph, right after Chapter 33's *Hōrum omnium fortissimī sunt Belgae* (because they live next to the warlike Germans): *Quā dē causā Helvētiī quoque reliquōs Gallōs virtūte praecēdunt.* (*Helvētiī*: the Helvetians, in today's Switzerland. *Reliquus*: the rest. *Virtūs, virtūtis*, f.: courage, excellence. *Praecēdō*: outdo.) | "**For this reason the Helvetians too outdo the rest of the Gauls in courage.**" *Virtūte*: respect. *Quā dē causā* points back: "for this reason" (§7): they too fight the Germans almost every day. |
| *Vir quīdam, nōmine Galēnus.* (Chapter 29's *quīdam*.) | "**A certain man, Galen by name**," "named Galen." *Nōmine*: in respect of name. (Chapter 1's *Nōmen mihi est* says it for yourself.) |
| "Grandpa: tired in body, brave in spirit." (*Corpus*, Chapter 14. *Animus*: mind, spirit, Chapter 32.) | ***Avus corpore fessus, animō fortis.*** Two ablatives of respect, one on each adjective. |

## 10. What kind: description

:: Excelsā statūrā, colōre candidō, nigrīs vegetīsque oculīs. || Tall in stature, fair in complexion, with dark, lively eyes. (Suetonius, *Julius* 45, describing Caesar)

Canis magnīs auribus. Vir magnā barbā.

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| New: *auris, auris*, f.: ear (an i-stem like Chapter 15's *ovis*). *Barba, barbae*: beard. Read the blurb. | "**A dog with big ears.** **A man with a big beard.**" An ablative **with its adjective**, hung on a noun to say what kind: the **ablative of description** ("what kind"; old-school "quality"). |
| Try it without the adjective: *vir barbā*? | Romans don't. Description **always** needs an adjective: *vir **magnā** barbā*. A bare *barbā* sounds like a tool ("by means of a beard"). |
| Chapter 14's puppy: Quīntus said *crūra parva, nāsus niger*. Describe the dog Cāseus with two ablatives. | ***Cāseus est canis parvus, nāsō nigrō, crūribus parvīs.*** "A little dog with a black nose and little legs." |
| *Color, colōris*, m.: color (Chapter 3's motto). Describe a cheese "of yellow color" and a girl "of great courage." | ***Cāseus colōre flāvō · puella magnā virtūte.*** Looks and character both work. |
| The motto. Suetonius on Julius Caesar: *Excelsā statūrā, colōre candidō, teretibus membrīs, ōre paulō pleniōre, nigrīs vegetīsque oculīs.* (*Excelsus*: tall. *Statūra*: height. *Candidus*: white, fair. *Teres, teretis*: smooth, rounded. *Membrum*: limb. *Ōs, ōris*: face, Chapter 14. *Plēnus*: full. *Vegetus*: lively.) | "**Tall in stature, fair in complexion, with well-shaped limbs, a rather full face, and dark, lively eyes.**" Five descriptions; and *ōre paulō pleniōre*, "a face **a little** fuller," hides §8's degree inside one. |
| The genitive has a cousin job: *vir magnae virtūtis* = *vir magnā virtūte*, "a man of great courage." Where did you just meet one? | §8's *modicae fideī*, "of little faith." Both cases can describe. A rough Roman habit: **looks and the body** lean to the ablative (*magnā barbā, excelsā statūrā*); **measurements, ages, and numbers** to the genitive (*puer decem annōrum*, "a boy of ten years"; *annus*: year). |

## 11. Ask the ablative

:: Interrogā ablātīvum! || Ask the ablative! (teaching line)

Quōcum? Quōmodo? Quā dē causā? Quantō?

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Ask each ablative its question. *Avus magnō cum gaudiō cāseum edit.* | **How?** "With great joy." Manner. |
| *Medicus magnā barbā venit.* | **What kind?** "A doctor with a big beard is coming." Description (it hangs on *medicus*). |
| *Quīntus metū tremit.* | **Why?** "From fear." Cause. |
| *Marcus multō celerius currit.* | **By how much?** "Much faster." Degree of difference. |
| *Cornēlia cum Aviā coquit.* | **With whom?** "With Grandma." Accompaniment. |
| *Avus nātū maior est quam Pater, et pede claudus.* | **In what respect?** "Older **by birth** than Dad," "lame **in the foot**." Respect, twice. |

!!! success "The Law of the Six Questions"
    No new endings: just ask the ablative its question.

    | ask | job | example |
    |---|---|---|
    | **with whom?** | accompaniment | *cum Avō · mēcum* |
    | **how?** | manner | *cum cūrā · magnā (cum) cūrā · nūllō modō* |
    | **why?** | cause | *metū · gaudiō · nimiō cāseō* |
    | **by how much?** | degree of difference | *multō maior · paulō post* |
    | **in what respect?** | respect | *nātū maior · pede claudus* |
    | **what kind?** | description | *canis magnīs auribus* |

    Plus the old ones: **where?** (*in hortō*) · **from where?** (*ex hortō, domō*) · **when?** (*hōrā tertiā*) · **with what?** (*manū*) · **by whom?** (*ā Marcō*) · **than whom?** (*Quīntō altior*) · **for how much?** (*ūnō dēnāriō*).

## 12. Fix it

:: Errāre hūmānum est. || To err is human. (proverb)

Magnā cum cūram? Canis magnae auribus?

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "With great care": *magnā cum cūram*. | ***Magnā cum cūrā.*** *Cum* takes the ablative: *cūrā*, long *ā*. |
| "A dog with big ears": *canis magnae auribus*. | ***Canis magnīs auribus.*** The adjective agrees with *auribus*: ablative plural, *-īs*. |
| "Much bigger": *multus maior*. | ***Multō maior.*** "By how much" goes in the ablative. |
| "Grandpa fights with a stick": *Avus cum baculō pugnat*. | ***Avus baculō pugnat.*** The tool is plain. (*Cum baculō* would be "with a stick along," as company.) |
| "Marcus walks with Grandpa": *Marcus Avō ambulat*. | ***Marcus cum Avō ambulat.*** Company needs *cum*; a bare *Avō* sounds like "by means of Grandpa." |
| "The Lord be with you all": *Dominus cum vōs*. | ***Dominus vōbīscum.*** *Cum* takes the ablative, and with pronouns it's glued on the end. |

## 13. Lectiō: *Nimiō cāseō!*

:: Medice, cūrā tē ipsum! || Physician, heal yourself! (Luke 4:23)

Avus nimiō cāseō aegrōtat. Venit medicus.

Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. As you read, ask every ablative its question: with whom? how? why? by how much? in what respect? what kind? New words: *iaceō*, lie; *senex*, old man; *remedium*, cure; *odor, odōris*, smell; *perīculōsus*, dangerous; *fenestra*, window (Chapter 8). One trap in Grandma's last line (and the motto): *cūrā tē ipsum* uses ***cūrā***, the command of *cūrō*, "take care of, heal," not today's ablative *cūrā*. Same letters; the company tells you.

::: dialogue lectio
Avia | Ēheu! Avus in lectō iacet. Stomachus eī dolet! || Grandma | Oh dear! Grandpa is lying in bed. His stomach hurts!
Marcus | Quā dē causā? || Marcus | For what reason?
Avia | Nimiō cāseō! Heri cāseum tōtum celerrimē ēdit. || Grandma | Too much cheese! Yesterday he ate the whole cheese very fast.
Avus | Ō! Ō! Dolōre dormīre nōn possum! || Grandpa | Oh! Oh! I can't sleep for the pain!
Māter | Iōannēs, ī ad forum cum Marcō. Medicum quaerite! || Mom | John, go to the forum with Marcus. Look for the doctor, you two!
Iōannēs | Īmus, magnā cum fēstīnātiōne! || John | We're going, in a great hurry!
Cornēlia | Medicum vīdī: vir magnā barbā et parvīs oculīs. || Cornēlia | I've seen the doctor: a man with a big beard and little eyes.
Quīntus | Et magnō nāsō! Metū tremō! || Quīntus | And a big nose! I'm shaking with fear!
Avia | Paulō post… Ecce! Medicus cum puerīs venit! || Grandma | A little later… Look! The doctor's coming with the boys!
Medicus | Salvēte! Medicus sum, nōmine Galēnus. Ubi est aegrōtus? || Doctor | Hello, y'all! I'm a doctor, Galen by name. Where's the patient?
Avus | Hīc! Corpore fessus sum… sed animō fortis! || Grandpa | Here! I'm tired in body… but brave in spirit!
Medicus | Et quis est iste, cum aegrōtō in lectō? || Doctor | And who's that, in bed with the patient?
Iōannēs | Canis noster, Cāseus: parvus, nāsō nigrō. || John | Our dog, Cāseus: little, with a black nose.
Medicus | Canis in lectō? Nūllō modō! Abī, Cāsee! || Doctor | A dog in the bed? No way! Off you go, Cāseus!
Medicus | Quid ēdistī, senex? || Doctor | What did you eat, old man?
Avus | Paulum cāseī… || Grandpa | A little cheese…
Avia | Paulum?! Cāseum tōtum, multō maiōrem quam caput tuum! || Grandma | A little?! The whole cheese, much bigger than your head!
Medicus | (Stomachum tangit.) Stomachus tuus pede altior est quam heri! || Doctor | (He touches his stomach.) Your stomach is a foot higher than yesterday!
Medicus | Remedium: nūllum cāseum hodiē, nūllum crās, nūllum perendiē! || Doctor | The cure: no cheese today, none tomorrow, none the day after!
Avus | Nūllum cāseum? Īrā clāmō! Sine cāseō vīvere nōn possum! || Grandpa | No cheese? I'm shouting with anger! I can't live without cheese!
Medicus | Sine cāseō… aut sine stomachō. || Doctor | Without cheese… or without a stomach.
Iōannēs | Sed cāseum novum in forō ēmī: colōre flāvō, odōre ācrī! || John | But I bought a new cheese in the forum: yellow in color, with a sharp smell!
Medicus | Cāseum? Dā mihi! Perīculōsus est. Eum mēcum auferō. || Doctor | Cheese? Give it to me! It's dangerous. I'm taking it away with me.
Marcus | Cāseus ablātus est! Cāsus ablātīvus! || Marcus | The cheese has been carried away! The carrying-away case!
Cornēlia | Ecce, per fenestram: medicus in viā sedet et cāseum edit, magnō cum gaudiō! || Cornēlia | Look, through the window: the doctor is sitting in the street eating the cheese, with great joy!
Quīntus | Cāseus… in stomachō… || Quīntus | The cheese… in the stomach of…
Omnēs | …Medicī! || Everyone | …the doctor!
Avus | Nihilō minus… multō melius mihi est! Gaudiō rīdeō! || Grandpa | All the same… I feel much better! I'm laughing for joy!
Avia | Crās medicus nimiō cāseō aegrōtābit. Medice, cūrā tē ipsum! || Grandma | Tomorrow the doctor will be sick from too much cheese. Physician, heal yourself!

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Quā dē causā Avus aegrōtat?* Answer in Latin, and name the job. | ***Nimiō cāseō.*** "From too much cheese": cause (why?). |
| How do Cornēlia and Quīntus describe the doctor? And how does he give his name? | ***Vir magnā barbā et parvīs oculīs… et magnō nāsō***: description (what kind?). ***Nōmine Galēnus***: respect, "Galen by name." |
| Grandma: *multō maiōrem quam caput tuum*. The doctor: *pede altior quam heri*. What do *multō* and *pede* measure? | **By how much**: degree of difference. "**Much** bigger than your head"; "higher **by a foot** than yesterday." (Here *quam* does the "than" work, Chapter 33.) |
| Find the "with whom"s and the "how"s. | Company: ***cum Marcō, cum puerīs, cum aegrōtō, mēcum***. Manner: ***magnā cum fēstīnātiōne, magnō cum gaudiō*** (and *gaudiō rīdeō* is cause: "**for** joy"). |
| Marcus's joke: *Cāseus ablātus est! Cāsus ablātīvus!* Explain both halves. | ***Ablātus est***: "has been carried away" (Chapter 31's perfect passive of Chapter 32's *auferō*): the doctor said *mēcum auferō*. ***Cāsus ablātīvus***: "the carrying-away case," today's title. *Cāseus* and *cāsus* even look alike. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Cum amīcīs, cum gaudiō! || With friends, with joy! (teaching line)

1. Say the ablative singular and plural of *mēnsa, hortus, rēx, manus, diēs*, with every long vowel.
2. The three Laws in your own words: the Three Old Jobs, the Three Withs, the Six Questions.
3. Three "with"s about your day: one company (*cum*), one manner (*magnā cum…*), one tool (plain).
4. Say why: *metū tremō · gaudiō clāmō · dolōre dormīre nōn possum · nimiō cāseō aegrōtō*.
5. Compare with a gap: *Marcus Quīntō pede altior est · multō melius · paulō post · nihilō minus*.
6. Describe three people or animals with ablatives: *vir magnā barbā · canis magnīs auribus · puella magnā virtūte*, and say who is *nātū maximus* in your family.
7. The real lines: *Dominus vōbīscum. Et cum spīritū tuō. · Magnā cum laude · Abiit in montāna cum fēstīnātiōne · Rēgnī cupiditāte inductus · Quantō magis vōs, modicae fideī? · Hī omnēs linguā, īnstitūtīs, lēgibus inter sē differunt · Excelsā statūrā, colōre candidō, nigrīs vegetīsque oculīs*.
8. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part (the doctor too). Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
