# Caput XXVIII: Quī, Quae, Quod

*Liber III, Fābulae · Chapter 28*

*In which Dad and John come home with a new cheese, and for once the dog is innocent (for a while). Back in Chapter 9 you prayed* Pater noster, quī es in caelīs *and were told that* quī *"comes much later." Later is now. Today you learn the little word that lets one sentence describe a noun inside another: "the man **who** loves cheese," "the cheese **that** Dad bought," "the dog **whose** name is Cheese." It has one law, and you can say it in a breath. Speaking goal: describe people and things, "the man who…"*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Avus est vir quī cāseum amat.* · *Cāseus quem Pater ēmit…* · *Puella cui cāseum dedī…* · *Mēnsa in quā cāseus erat…* · *Is quī cāseum ēmit…* · *Quis est? Est vir quī…*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** clause (a little sentence with its own verb); relative pronoun (a pointing word that starts a describing clause); antecedent (the word it points back to); relative clause (the whole describing clause)
    - **Forms:** *quī, quae, quod* in every case and both numbers: *cuius, cui, quem, quam, quō, quā* · *quōrum, quārum, quibus, quōs, quās*; *quōcum, quācum, quibuscum*
    - **Law:** the Law of the Relative
    - **Words:** *vir, virī*; *servō, servāre, servāvī, servātum*; *is quī, id quod*
    - **Reading:** *Cāseus quem Pater ēmit* (the new cheese comes home… mostly)
    - **Speaking:** describe a person or a thing with "who," "which," "whose," "to whom," "with whom"

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep two old friends handy: Chapter 19's *is, ea, id* (with *huius, huic*) and Chapter 10's *Cuius est?* Today's word declines a lot like them.

---

## 1. *Quī es in caelīs*

:: Pater noster, quī es in caelīs. || Our Father, who art in heaven. (the Lord's Prayer; Chapter 9)

Avus est vir. Vir cāseum amat. Avus est vir quī cāseum amat.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 9's motto: *Pater noster, quī es in caelīs.* Chapter 9 said *quī* "comes much later." Guess what it means here. | **"Who."** "Our Father, **who** art (are) in heaven." *Quī* points back to *Pater* and tells you something about him. |
| New word: *vir, virī* (m.), "man; husband." It goes like *puer, puerī* (Chapter 10): *vir, virī, virō, virum, virō*. Two sentences: *Avus est vir. Vir cāseum amat.* Glue them into one: "Grandpa is a man who loves cheese." | ***Avus est vir quī cāseum amat.*** *Quī* takes the place of the second *vir*. |
| *Quī cāseum amat* has its own verb, *amat*. Plain English: a group of words with its own verb is a **clause**, a little sentence inside a bigger one. How many clauses in *Avus est vir quī cāseum amat*? | **Two.** *Avus est vir* could stand alone: that's the **main clause**. *Quī cāseum amat* can't stand alone; it hangs on *vir*. |
| Plain English: *quī* is a **relative pronoun**: a pointing word that starts a describing clause. Why "relative"? | It **relates**, carries you back, to a word already said. Latin *relātum*, "carried back": *re-* (back) + *lātum*, key 4 of Chapter 25's *ferō, ferre, tulī, lātum*. **Pronoun**: a word standing in for a noun (Chapter 21's *ego, tū*). |
| Plain English: the word it points back to is the **antecedent**. Latin *antecēdēns*, "going before" (Chapter 27's *anteā*, before). What's the antecedent in *Avus est vir quī cāseum amat*? | ***Vir.*** It usually sits right before the relative. |
| The whole describing clause, *quī cāseum amat*, is a **relative clause**. What does it do for *vir*? | It **describes** him, like an adjective: *vir bonus*, a good man · *vir quī cāseum amat*, a cheese-loving man. A relative clause is a long adjective with its own verb. |
| English says "who" for people, "which" or "that" for things. Latin? "The dog that eats cheese." | ***Canis quī cāseum edit.*** Latin has one word for people, animals, and things alike. It changes only to match its noun, as you'll see next. |
| Find the antecedent, the relative pronoun, and the relative clause: *Ecce vir quī cāseum ēdit!* | Antecedent ***vir*** · relative pronoun ***quī*** · relative clause ***quī cāseum ēdit***. "Look, the man who ate the cheese!" |

## 2. *Quī, quae, quod*

:: Terra quae fluit lacte et melle. || A land which flows with milk and honey. (Exodus 3:8, Vulgate; Chapter 14)

Puer quī legit · puella quae cantat · mālum quod dulce est.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Marcus is a boy who reads." | ***Marcus est puer quī legit.*** |
| "Cornēlia is a girl who runs." Will *quī* still do? | No: ***Cornēlia est puella quae currit.*** A feminine antecedent takes ***quae***. |
| "This is an apple which is sweet." (*Mālum*: neuter. *Dulcis, dulce*: Chapter 20.) | ***Hoc est mālum quod dulce est.*** A neuter antecedent takes ***quod***. That *-d* is Chapter 19's neuter *-d*, as in *id, illud, istud*. |
| So where does the relative get its gender? | From its **antecedent**: *vir quī* (m.), *puella quae* (f.), *mālum quod* (n.). The word it points back to sets the gender. |
| Chapter 14's Exodus line: *terra quae fluit lacte et melle.* (*Terra*: land, f. *Fluō*: flow.) Why *quae*? | The antecedent *terra* is feminine and singular. "A land **which** flows with milk and honey." |
| Plural: "Marcus and Quīntus are boys who read." | ***Marcus et Quīntus sunt puerī quī legunt.*** Masculine plural is ***quī*** again, and the verb inside goes plural, *legunt*, because the antecedent is plural. |
| "Girls who sing" and "apples which are sweet." | ***Puellae quae cantant*** · ***māla quae dulcia sunt.*** The plural set: ***quī, quae, quae***. |
| *Quae* now turns up three ways. Which? | Feminine singular (*puella quae*), feminine plural (*puellae quae*), neuter plural (*māla quae*). The antecedent and the verb's number tell you which, just like Chapter 19's *haec*. |

## 3. The cheese that Dad bought

:: Arma virumque canō, Trōiae quī prīmus ab ōrīs… || Arms and the man I sing, who first from the shores of Troy… (Vergil, *Aeneid* 1.1)

Cāseus in mēnsā est. Pater cāseum ēmit. Cāseus quem Pater ēmit in mēnsā est.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Two sentences: *Cāseus in mēnsā est. Pater cāseum ēmit.* In the second, what job does *cāseum* do? | It's the **object** (accusative): Dad bought it. |
| Glue them: "The cheese **which** Dad bought is on the table." Is it *quī*? | No: ***Cāseus quem Pater ēmit in mēnsā est.*** Inside its own clause the relative is the object of *ēmit*, so accusative: ***quem***. (Same form as Chapter 4's *quem?*, "whom?") |
| But *cāseus* is the subject of *est*. Doesn't *quem* have to match it? | Not in case. **Gender and number** come from the antecedent (*cāseus*: masculine, singular). **Case** comes from the relative's own job inside its own clause (object of *ēmit*: accusative). |
| Same antecedent, two jobs: "Grandpa is a man **who** loves the dog" and "Grandpa is a man **whom** the dog loves." | ***Avus est vir quī canem amat*** (he does the loving: subject, *quī*) · ***Avus est vir quem canis amat*** (he gets loved: object, *quem*). |
| Feminine: "The girl whom Marcus sees is Cornēlia." | ***Puella quam Marcus videt Cornēlia est.*** ***Quam***: feminine, object. |
| Neuter: "The apple which Quīntus ate was sweet." | ***Mālum quod Quīntus ēdit dulce erat.*** Neuter subject and object are the same, ***quod*** (Chapter 4's Neuter Twins). |
| Plural objects: "the boys whom I see," "the girls whom I see," "the apples which I see." | ***Puerī quōs videō*** · ***puellae quās videō*** · ***māla quae videō***. *Quōs, quās, quae*: endings like *bonōs, bonās, bona*. |
| Vergil opens the *Aeneid*: *Arma virumque canō, Trōiae quī prīmus ab ōrīs…* (*Canō*: I sing. *Trōiae*: of Troy. *Prīmus*: first. *Ab ōrīs*: from the shores.) *Virum* is accusative. Why is *quī* nominative? | Inside its own clause the man is the **subject**: "who first (came) from the shores of Troy…" (the verb, *vēnit*, waits until the next line). Antecedent *virum* gives masculine singular; his own job gives the case. The Law, in the first line of Rome's great epic. |

!!! success "The Law of the Relative"
    The relative pronoun takes its **gender and number from its antecedent**, the word it points back to.
    It takes its **case from its own job inside its own clause**.
    *Cāseus **quem** Pater ēmit*: masculine singular because of *cāseus*; accusative because Dad bought it.

## 4. Whose, and to whom

:: Cuius regiō, eius religiō. || Whose the land, his the religion. (a later summary of the Peace of Augsburg, 1555)

Puer cuius canis cāseum ēdit… Avus, cui cāseum dedimus…

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 10: *Cuius est?* "Whose is it?" The relative uses the same word. "The boy **whose** dog ate the cheese." | ***Puer cuius canis cāseum ēdit.*** ***Cuius***: "whose," the owner case (genitive). |
| Does *cuius* change for a girl or a thing? | No: *puer cuius*, *puella cuius*, *mālum cuius*. One form for all three genders, like *huius* and *eius* (Chapter 19's Law of the Pointing Words, surprise 1). |
| Chapter 19 gave you *huic*, "to this," and said its rhyme *cui*, "to whom," would come in Chapter 29. Here it is a chapter early, as the relative. "The girl **to whom** I gave the cheese." | ***Puella cui cāseum dedī.*** ***Cui***: dative, the receiver (Chapter 11), one form for all genders, one beat like *huic*. (Its question use, "to whom?", comes next chapter.) |
| "Grandpa, to whom we gave the cheese, is happy." (*Fēlīx*, Chapter 20.) | ***Avus, cui cāseum dedimus, fēlīx est.*** *Avus* is the subject of *est*; *cui* is the receiver inside its own clause. |
| Plural "whose": *quōrum, quārum, quōrum*. Aeneas, still homeless, watches Carthage being built: *Ō fortūnātī, quōrum iam moenia surgunt!* (*Fortūnātī*: lucky ones. *Moenia*: city walls. *Surgunt*: rise.) | "O lucky people, **whose** walls are already rising!" *Quōrum*: masculine plural, pointing back to the people. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 1.437) |
| "The girls whose books are on the table." | ***Puellae quārum librī in mēnsā sunt.*** |
| Plural "to whom": "the kids to whom Grandma gives cookies." | ***Līberī quibus Avia crustula dat.*** ***Quibus***: dative plural, all genders. |
| *Cuius regiō, eius religiō.* (*Regiō*: region, land; short e, from *regō*, rule. *Religiō*: religion.) Find the pair. | *Cuius*, "whose" (relative) · *eius*, "his" (Chapter 19): "**Whose** the land, **his** the religion." Under the Peace of Augsburg (1555), each ruler chose the church of his own land. |

## 5. In which, with whom

:: Hic est Fīlius meus dīlēctus, in quō mihi complacuī. || This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased. (Matthew 3:17, Vulgate; Chapter 19)

Mēnsa in quā cāseus erat… amīcus quōcum ambulō…

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "The table **on which** the cheese was." *In* + place where takes which case (Chapter 7)? | Ablative: ***mēnsa in quā cāseus erat.*** ***Quā***: feminine ablative. |
| Masculine and neuter ablative is ***quō***. "The garden in which the dog sleeps" and "the bed in which Grandpa snores." (*Lectus*, Chapter 27.) | ***Hortus in quō canis dormit*** · ***lectus in quō Avus stertit.*** |
| Chapter 7 glued *cum* onto the end: *mēcum, tēcum*. The relative can do it too. "The friend **with whom** I walk." | ***Amīcus quōcum ambulō*** (also *cum quō*). Feminine ***quācum***, plural ***quibuscum***. |
| Chapter 19 read the first half of Matthew 3:17. Now the end: *in quō mihi complacuī.* (*Mihi complacuī*: "I am well pleased," a set phrase built on a perfect.) | "This is my beloved Son, **in whom** I am well pleased." *In quō*: ablative, masculine singular, pointing back to *Fīlius*. |
| Plural ablative: "the roads on which we walk." (*Via*, Chapter 8. *Ambulō*, Chapter 5.) | ***Viae in quibus ambulāmus.*** ***Quibus*** does double duty, dative and ablative plural, like every *-ibus* (Chapter 13's Law of Double Duty). |
| Chant the masculine singular in case order: subject, of, to, object, by/with/from. | ***Quī, cuius, cui, quem, quō.*** |
| Now feminine and neuter. | ***Quae, cuius, cui, quam, quā*** · ***quod, cuius, cui, quod, quō***. Only subject, object, and ablative change; *cuius* and *cui* never do. |

!!! success "Quī, quae, quod: the whole family"
    | | m. | f. | n. |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | **sg. subject** | *quī* | *quae* | *quod* |
    | **of** | *cuius* | *cuius* | *cuius* |
    | **to/for** | *cui* | *cui* | *cui* |
    | **object** | *quem* | *quam* | *quod* |
    | **by/with/from** | *quō* | *quā* | *quō* |
    | **pl. subject** | *quī* | *quae* | *quae* |
    | **of** | *quōrum* | *quārum* | *quōrum* |
    | **to/for** | *quibus* | *quibus* | *quibus* |
    | **object** | *quōs* | *quās* | *quae* |
    | **by/with/from** | *quibus* | *quibus* | *quibus* |

    Plural *-ōrum, -ārum, -ōs, -ās* look like *bonus, bona, bonum*. Singular *cuius, cui* and neuter *-d* look like *is, ea, id*.

## 6. The one who, the thing which

:: Nusquam est quī ubīque est. || Whoever is everywhere is nowhere. (Seneca, *Letters* 2.2; Chapter 8)

Is quī… ea quae… id quod…

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 8's motto: *Nusquam est quī ubīque est.* Where's the antecedent? | **None written.** Latin can leave it out: "(He) who is everywhere is nowhere" = **whoever**. Fill it in with Chapter 19's *is*: *Nusquam est is quī ubīque est.* |
| *Is quī, ea quae, id quod*: "the one who, the woman who, the thing which." "The one who ate the cheese is the dog." | ***Is quī cāseum ēdit canis est.*** |
| Chapter 18's motto: *Quae volumus, et crēdimus libenter.* Chapter 18 glossed *quae* as "the things that." Which form is it now? | Neuter plural, **object** of *volumus*: "(the things) **which** we want." The antecedent *ea* is left out. "What we want, we also gladly believe." (Caesar, *Civil War* 2.27) |
| Chapter 16's motto, Ovid on his boyhood: *Et quod temptābam scrībere, versus erat.* | *Quod* = "the thing which," so "what(ever)": "And **whatever** I tried to write came out as verse." Inside, *quod* is the object of *scrībere*; the whole clause is the subject of *erat*. |
| Chapter 25's tombstone: *Fuī quod es, eris quod sum.* | "I was **what** you are; you will be **what** I am." Twice the *id* is left out. |
| Chapter 4's motto (and many a homework motto since): *Age quod agis.* | "Do **what** you're doing" = *Age id quod agis*: keep your mind on the job. |
| Martial opens his book: *Hic est quem legis ille, quem requīris…* (*Requīrō*: look for. *Hic… ille*: Chapter 19.) | "Here he is, the one **you're reading**, the one **you're looking for**": Martial introducing himself. *Quem* is the object of *legis* and of *requīris*. (Martial, *Epigrams* 1.1) |
| Back to Chapter 9: *Pater noster, quī es in caelīs.* Why *es* and not *est*? | The antecedent is the person spoken **to** ("you"), so the verb in the clause is "you" too: *es*. A relative clause also takes the **person** of its antecedent: *Ego, quī cāseum amō…* · *Tū, quī cāseum amās…* |

## 7. The man who…

:: Beātus vir quī nōn abiit in cōnsiliō impiōrum. || Blessed is the man who has not walked in the counsel of the wicked. (Psalm 1:1, Vulgate)

Quis est? Est vir quī…

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The first line of the Psalms: *Beātus vir quī nōn abiit in cōnsiliō impiōrum.* (*Beātus*: blessed, happy. *Abiit*: *ab* + *iit*, went off. *Cōnsilium*: advice, counsel. *Impiōrum*: of the wicked.) | "Blessed is the **man who** has not walked in the counsel of the wicked." Today's speaking goal, "the man who…", in the first verse of the book. |
| Chapter 26 retold: "The story which Grandpa told was long." | ***Fābula quam Avus nārrāvit longa erat.*** *Fābula* f. sg. → *quam*, object of *nārrāvit*. |
| Chapter 27: "The dog, who had already eaten the cheese, barked." | ***Canis, quī cāseum iam ēderat, lātrāvit.*** Relative clause + Chapter 27's pluperfect. |
| Chapter 15: "the bird which carried the cheese into the clouds." | ***Avis quae cāseum in nūbēs portāvit.*** *Avis* is feminine (not *avus*!), so *quae*. |
| Chapter 14: "the puppy whose name is Cheese." | ***Catulus cuius nōmen est Cāseus.*** |
| A guessing game. *Quis est?* "He's the man who snores in bed and whose stomach is big." | ***Est vir quī in lectō stertit et cuius stomachus magnus est.*** Answer: *Avus!* |
| Your turn: describe Mother in two clues. "She's the woman who… and to whom…" (*Fēmina*, Chapter 20.) | E.g. ***Est fēmina quae librōs legit et cui Pater cāseum dat.*** |
| Now yourself: "I am John, who read Latin." Which verb form? | ***Ego sum Iōannēs, quī Latīnē legō.*** The antecedent is "I," so *legō*. Compare God's answer to Moses: *Ego sum quī sum*, "I am who I am" (Exodus 3:14, Vulgate). |

## 8. Look-alikes

:: Nōn semper ea sunt quae videntur. || Things are not always what they seem. (Phaedrus 4.2.5; Chapter 3)

Quis an quī? Quae, quae, quae? Quō an quō?

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 3's motto: *Nōn semper ea sunt quae videntur.* (*Videntur*: they seem.) | "Things are not always what they seem." *Ea… quae*: "those things… which," a pointing word plus a relative, both neuter plural. |
| *Quis cāseum ēdit?* vs *Canis quī cāseum ēdit.* | *Quis?* **asks** (Chapter 2): "Who ate the cheese?" *Quī* **points back** to *canis*: "the dog who ate the cheese." Question words and relatives share some forms; Chapter 29 sorts them out. |
| *Quem vidēs?* vs *Vir quem vidēs.* | Same form. Chapter 4's *quem?* asks "Whom do you see?" The relative *quem* points back to *vir*: "the man whom you see." Clues: the question mark, and an antecedent right before it. |
| *Quō īs?* vs *Hortus in quō dormit.* | Chapter 8's *quō?* asks "where to?" The relative *quō* is the ablative after *in*: "the garden in which he sleeps." |
| *Quam* in Chapter 27's *plūs quam perfectum* vs *puella quam videō.* | Two words. *Plūs quam*: "more **than**" (comparisons, Chapter 33). *Puella quam videō*: the relative, feminine object, "the girl whom I see." |

## 9. Fix it

:: Quī errat, discit. || Whoever slips up, learns. (teaching line; *discit*: learns)

Quī an quae? Quem an quod? Cui an cuius?

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "The girl who sings": *puella quī cantat.* | ***Puella quae cantat.*** Feminine antecedent → *quae*. |
| "The cheese that Dad bought": *cāseus quī Pater ēmit.* | ***Cāseus quem Pater ēmit.*** The cheese is the object inside its own clause → *quem*. |
| "The apple which I ate": *mālum quem ēdī.* | ***Mālum quod ēdī.*** Neuter antecedent → *quod* (object and subject alike). |
| "The boy whose book I'm reading": *puer quem librum legō.* | ***Puer cuius librum legō.*** "Whose" = owner = *cuius*. *Quem* would make the boy the thing you read. |
| "The girl to whom I gave the cheese": *puella quam cāseum dedī.* | ***Puella cui cāseum dedī.*** She's the receiver → dative *cui*. |
| "The table on which the cheese was": *mēnsa in quō cāseus erat.* | ***Mēnsa in quā cāseus erat.*** *Mēnsa* is feminine → *quā*. |
| "The boys who read": *puerī quī legit.* | ***Puerī quī legunt.*** The antecedent is plural, so the verb inside is plural too. |

## 10. Lectiō: *Cāseus quem Pater ēmit*

:: Quī cāseum portat, cāseum edit. || Whoever carries the cheese eats the cheese. (Dad's rule, as you'll see)

Pater et Iōannēs cāseum novum ēmērunt. Nunc domum veniunt.

Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. As you read, find every relative pronoun, point to its antecedent, and say why it has its case.

::: dialogue lectio
Avus | Ubi est cāseus novus? Pater et Iōannēs nōndum vēnērunt! || Grandpa | Where's the new cheese? Dad and John haven't come yet!
Avia | Patientia, Ave! || Grandma | Patience, Grandpa!
Marcus | Ecce! Pater venit, quī capsam magnam portat! || Marcus | Look! Here comes Dad, who's carrying a big box!
Pater | Salvēte, omnēs! Hic est cāseus quem ēmimus. || Dad | Hello, everyone! Here's the cheese we bought.
Avus | Dā mihi capsam quam portās! || Grandpa | Give me the box you're carrying!
Avus | Quid? Capsa in quā cāseus erat… paene vacua est! Parva pars sōla manet! || Grandpa | What? The box the cheese was in… is almost empty! Only a little piece is left!

Iōannēs | Est vir cuius vōx nōn dulcis est, quī in viā semper cantat… || John | He's a man whose voice isn't sweet, who always sings on the road…
Līberī | Pater! || The kids | Dad!

Iōannēs | Hanc partem tibi servāvī, Ave. || John | I saved this piece for you, Grandpa.

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Pater venit, quī capsam magnam portat.* Antecedent? Case of *quī*, and why? | *Pater*. *Quī* is the subject inside its clause: he carries the box. (Latin can let a word or two, here *venit*, stand between the antecedent and the relative.) |
| *Capsa in quā cāseus erat.* Why *quā*? | *Capsa* is feminine singular; inside its clause it's the place where, after *in*: ablative. (*Paene*: almost.) |
| *Canis, quī domī mānsit, cāseum nōn ēdit.* Translate, and take *mānsit* back to its keys. | "The dog, who stayed home, didn't eat the cheese." *Mānsit*: perfect of *maneō, manēre, mānsī* (Chapter 26). For once, the dog is innocent. |
| John's riddle: *est vir cuius vōx nōn dulcis est, quī in viā semper cantat.* What gave Dad away? | His voice (*cuius vōx*: whose voice) and his singing. Chapter 20 already said *vōx eius nōn dulcis*, and since Chapter 18 the kids have been begging *Nōlī, Pater!* |
| Dad's rule, *Quī cāseum portat, cāseum edit.* Where's the antecedent? | Left out, like Seneca's *Nusquam est quī…*: "**Whoever** carries the cheese eats it." Grandma rewrites the rule in Grandpa's favor. |
| *Grātiās tibi agō, Iōannēs, quī mē amās!* Why *amās* and not *amat*? | The antecedent is *Iōannēs*, the person spoken to: "you." So the verb inside is "you": *amās*, just like *Pater noster, quī es*. (*Servō, servāre*: save, keep.) |
| *…canis cuius nōmen est Cāseus!* Whose stomach? | The dog's: "the dog **whose** name is Cheese." The dog stayed innocent for exactly one scene. |
| *Dīxeram!* and *Canis quem amō cāseum quem amō ēdit!* | *Dīxeram*: Chapter 27's pluperfect, "I had said it!" = "I told you so!" (back in Chapter 27: *Canis eum iam ēderit!*). Then two relatives, both *quem*, both objects of *amō*: one dog, one cheese, one heartbroken grandpa. |

## Vocābula: Words of this chapter

:: Verba quae legis tua sunt. || The words you read are yours. (teaching line)

New forms:

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| *quī, quae, quod* | who, which, that (relative pronoun) |
| *cuius · cui* | whose · to/for whom, to/for which (all genders) |
| *quem, quam, quod* | whom, which (object) |
| *quō, quā, quō* | by/with/from whom or which; *in quō*, in which |
| *quī, quae, quae* | who, which (plural subject) |
| *quōrum, quārum, quōrum · quibus* | whose (plural) · to/for, by/with/from whom or which (plural) |
| *quōs, quās, quae* | whom, which (plural object) |
| *quōcum, quācum, quibuscum* | with whom |

New words:

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| *vir, virī* (m.) | man; husband |
| *servō, servāre, servāvī, servātum* | save, keep |
| *is quī, id quod* | the one who, the thing which (whoever, whatever) |
| *clause* | a little sentence inside a bigger one, with its own verb |
| *relative pronoun* | a pointing word that starts a describing clause (*quī, quae, quod*) |
| *antecedent* | the word a relative pronoun points back to |
| *relative clause* | the whole describing clause, from the relative to its verb |

Glossed in passing (no card): *terra* land, *fluō* flow, *canō* I sing, *Trōia* Troy, *ōra* shore, *regiō* region, *religiō* religion, *fortūnātus* lucky, *moenia* city walls, *surgō* rise, *complacuī* I have been well pleased, *requīrō* look for, *beātus* blessed, *cōnsilium* counsel, *impius* wicked, *videntur* they seem, *discit* learns, *paene* almost, *patientia* patience.

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīc mihi quod vīdistī. || Tell me what you saw. (teaching line)

1. Chant all three singulars: *quī, cuius, cui, quem, quō* · *quae, cuius, cui, quam, quā* · *quod, cuius, cui, quod, quō*. Then the plurals.
2. Say the Law of the Relative in your own words: gender and number from the antecedent, case from its own job.
3. Glue pairs into one sentence: *Avus est vir. Vir cāseum amat.* · *Cāseus in mēnsā est. Pater cāseum ēmit.* · *Puella cantat. Puellae cāseum dedī.*
4. Same antecedent, two jobs: *Avus est vir quī canem amat* · *Avus est vir quem canis amat.*
5. *Quis est?* Describe three people in the family with two relative clauses each, and let a friend (or Grok Bot) guess.
6. The real lines: *Pater noster, quī es in caelīs* · *Arma virumque canō, Trōiae quī prīmus ab ōrīs* · *Nusquam est quī ubīque est* · *Beātus vir quī nōn abiit…*
7. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part (the dog too). Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
