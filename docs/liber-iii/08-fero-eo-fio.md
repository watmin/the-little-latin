# Caput XXXII: Ferō, Eō, Fīō

*Liber III, Fābulae · Chapter 32*

*In which three tiny verbs get taken apart. Chapter 8 handed you a chant,* eō, īs, it, īmus, ītis, eunt, *and a promise: "Chapter 32 takes this tiny verb apart." Chapter 6 gave you the short command* Fer! *and Chapter 25 its wild perfect* tulī. *Chapter 31 left* Fīat lūx *hanging. Today you meet the rule-breakers properly: "go," "carry," and "become," plus the whole family of "want," "don't want," and "would rather." Along the way: Shakespeare's stage directions turn out to be Latin, the ablative case turns out to be named after "carrying away," and Grandpa decides to make his own cheese. Speaking goal: say where people are going and coming back from, who is carrying what, what is happening, and what you would rather do.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Quō īs? · Abī! · Redī! · Quis cāseum fert? · Fer! · Quid fit? · Nihil fit. · Ex lacte cāseus fit. · Mālō… quam… · Fīat!*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** irregular verb (a rule-breaker verb); compound verb (a verb with a little word glued on the front); impersonal (a verb with no person as its subject)
    - **Forms:** *eō, īs, it, īmus, ītis, eunt* (taken apart) · *ferō, fers, fert, ferimus, fertis, ferunt* · *feror, ferris, fertur* · *fīō, fīs, fit, fīunt, fierī* · *mālō, māvīs, māvult, mālumus, māvultis, mālunt* · *nōlō…* in full
    - **Laws:** the Law of Ī and E · the Law of the Missing Vowel · the Law of the Three Wishes
    - **Words:** *abeō, exeō, redeō, adeō, trānseō*; *auferō, referō*; *Quid fit?*, *Fīat!*
    - **Reading:** *Quid fit?* (a trip to the forum, a heavy cheese, and Grandpa's secret in the kitchen)
    - **Speaking:** say "go away," "come back," "who's carrying it?", "what's going on?", and "I'd rather… than…"

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep three old friends close: Chapter 8's *eō* chant, Chapter 17's *legō* in every tense, and Chapter 18's *volō*. Today's verbs are mostly those three with a twist.

## 1. The rule-breakers

:: Verba quaedam rēgulās nōn servant. || Some verbs don't keep the rules. (teaching line; *rēgula*, rule; *servō*, Chapter 28)

Eō, īs, it. Ferō, fers, fert. Fīō, fīs, fit.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 8 gave you *eō, īs, it, īmus, ītis, eunt* as a chant. Its infinitive *īre* looks like Chapter 6's fourth family (*audīre*). But compare *audiunt* and Chapter 17's *audiam*. Does *eō* really belong there? | **No.** "They go" is *eunt*, not *iunt*; "I will go" is *ībō*, a *bō* future, not an *-am* future like *audiam* (Chapter 17 called it a rebel). *Eō* follows no family all the way. |
| Plain English: an **irregular verb** is a "rule-breaker," a verb that won't follow its family's pattern everywhere (old-school: an "anomalous verb"). Which ones have you already met? | ***Sum*** (Chapter 5), ***possum*** (Chapter 18), ***volō, nōlō, mālō*** (Chapter 18), ***eō*** (Chapter 8), and ***ferō*** (Chapter 6's *fer*, Chapter 25's *tulī*). Today: *eō* and *ferō* taken apart, a new one, ***fīō***, and the whole *volō* family. |
| Why would the commonest verbs break the rules? Think of English "be: am, is, are, was" and "go, went." | Because they are used the most. Words said a thousand times a day keep old shapes and get worn smooth, like coins. Rare words get "fixed" to match the pattern; common ones stay wild. |
| Good news. Chapter 25: *tulī, tulistī, tulit…* and *iī, īstī, iit…* Once you have the third key, do the perfect endings ever break rules? | **Never.** The perfect, pluperfect, and future perfect are regular for every verb. Irregular verbs are irregular mostly in the **present**. So today's job is small: a few presents, and the rest is built from what you know. |

## 2. *Eō* taken apart

:: Ībam forte Viā Sacrā. || I was walking, as it happened, along the Sacred Way. (Horace, *Satires* 1.9.1; Chapter 16)

Eō, eunt. Īs, it, īmus, ītis. Ī! Īte! Īre.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Take Chapter 5's endings off the chant (*-ō, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt*). What is left at the front of each form? | Two shapes of one stem: ***e-*** in *eō* and *eunt*; ***ī-*** in *īs, it, īmus, ītis*. |
| Look at the sound right after the stem. When does *eō* use *e-*? | When the next sound is a **vowel**: *e-ō*, *e-unt*. Before a **consonant** it is *ī-*: *ī-s, ī-mus, ī-tis*. |
| Then why is *it* short, when *īs* is long? | Chapter 16's rule: a long vowel goes short before a final *-t* (*amās* but *amat*). So *ī-t* becomes ***it***, "he goes." |
| The infinitive and the commands (Chapters 8 and 12): *īre*, *Ī!*, *Īte!* Which stem? | ***Ī-*** every time: before a consonant, or before nothing at all. ***Ī!*** "Go!" is a whole sentence in one letter. |
| Chapter 16's *ībam* and *ībō*. Do they fit the rule? | Yes: *ī-* + *-bam*, *ī-* + *-bō* (*b* is a consonant). ***Ībam, ībās, ībat…*** "I was going." ***Ībō, ībis, ībit, ībimus, ībitis, ībunt*** "I will go": the *bō, bi, bu* future of the first two families. |
| The perfect side, from the keys *eō, īre, iī (īvī), itum*: "I went," "I had gone" (Chapter 27), "I will have gone." | ***Iī*** (or *īvī*), ***ieram***, ***ierō***. Regular perfect endings on *i-* (Chapter 25's *iī, īstī, iit, iimus, īstis, iērunt*). |

!!! success "The Law of Ī and E"
    *Eō* has one stem in two shapes:

    - **e-** before a vowel: ***eō, eunt***.
    - **ī-** before a consonant: *īs, īmus, ītis · īre · Ī! Īte! · ībam · ībō*. Before a final *-t* it goes short: ***it***.

    | | now | was going | will go | went |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | I | *eō* | *ībam* | *ībō* | *iī* |
    | you | *īs* | *ībās* | *ībis* | *īstī* |
    | he, she | *it* | *ībat* | *ībit* | *iit* |
    | we | *īmus* | *ībāmus* | *ībimus* | *iimus* |
    | y'all | *ītis* | *ībātis* | *ībitis* | *īstis* |
    | they | *eunt* | *ībant* | *ībunt* | *iērunt* |

## 3. Go away, go out, come back

:: Abiit, excessit, ēvāsit, ērūpit. || He has gone, withdrawn, slipped away, burst out. (Cicero, *Against Catiline* 2.1, 63 BC)

Abī! Exī! Redī! Eō, abeō, exeō, redeō.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Glue Chapter 7's *ab* ("away from") onto *eō*: ***abeō***. What does it mean? | "**I go away.**" Plain English: a **compound verb** is a verb with a little word glued on the front, usually a preposition. The front piece adds the direction; the endings stay the verb's own. |
| So *abeō* goes exactly like *eō*. "You go away · he goes away · they go away." Then "Go away!" to one person and to y'all. | ***Abīs · abit · abeunt*** (*e-* before a vowel, still). ***Abī! · Abīte!*** |
| *Ex* + *eō*: "go out." "He goes out." "They go out." | ***Exit. Exeunt.*** These are Shakespeare's stage directions: printed plays use Latin, *Exit* "he goes out (off stage)," *Exeunt* "they go out." Chapter 7 told you *ex* was "think exit." Now you know *exit* is a whole Latin sentence. |
| *Re-* means "back" (it becomes *red-* before a vowel). *Red* + *eō* = ***redeō***. "Come back!" (to one) · "I'm going back home." | ***Redī!*** · ***Domum redeō.*** (*Domum*, no preposition: Chapter 8.) |
| Two more: ***adeō*** (*ad* + *eō*: go to, approach) and ***trānseō*** (Chapter 8's *trāns* + *eō*: go across, cross). "We are approaching Grandpa." "We are crossing the garden." | ***Ad Avum adīmus.*** · ***Hortum trānsīmus.*** *Trānseō* takes the thing crossed as a plain object: you "cross the garden." |
| Cicero, 63 BC, telling the people of Rome that the plotter Catiline has fled the city: *Abiit, excessit, ēvāsit, ērūpit.* Which word is today's? | ***Abiit***: *ab-* + Chapter 25's *iit*, "**he has gone away**." The other three are other verbs (*excēdō, ēvādō, ērumpō*) heaped up for effect: four ways to say "He's gone!" |

## 4. *Sīc trānsit*: compounds everywhere

:: Sīc trānsit glōria mundī. || Thus passes the glory of the world. (from the old rite of crowning a pope)

Exit, trānsit, redit. Ītur!

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Sīc trānsit glōria mundī.* (*Glōria*: glory. *Mundus*: world, Chapter 30. *Sīc*: thus, Chapter 19.) Find the verb and take it apart. | ***Trāns-it***: "goes across," so "**passes**," passes away. For centuries it was said to a new pope at his crowning, as a bit of flax burned out in front of him: earthly glory doesn't last. |
| English is full of *eō*. *Exit, transit, circuit* (*circum* + *eō*), *initial* (from *ineō*, "go in"). Where is the "going" in each? | ***Exit***: Latin "he goes out," so a way out. ***Transit***: a going-across (*trānseō*). ***Circuit***: a going-around (*circumeō*). ***Initial***: from *initium*, "a going-in," so a **beginning**. |
| Compounds keep every form of *eō*. "They go out." · "They were coming back." · "We will go away." | ***Exeunt.*** · ***Redībant.*** · ***Abībimus.*** |
| Chapter 30's passive, on *eō*? You can't "be gone" the way a cheese is eaten, but Latin can say "there is a going." Vergil: *Ītur in antīquam silvam* (*Aeneid* 6.179; *silva*, Chapter 8). What does it mean? | Word for word: "**There is a going** into an ancient forest," so "**They go** into an ancient wood." Plain English: a verb is **impersonal** when no person is its subject; English supplies "there is" or "they." Just recognize it for now. |

## 5. *Ferō*

:: Omnia fert aetās. || Time carries all things away. (Vergil, *Eclogues* 9.51)

Fer! Ferte! Ferō, fers, fert.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 6's short command ***Fer!***, "Carry! Bring!" Now to y'all, also from Chapter 6. | ***Ferte!*** (*Fer* lost its *-e*, one of Chapter 6's four rebels *dīc, dūc, fac, fer*; *legite* has a vowel that *ferte* doesn't.) |
| Guess "I carry · we carry · they carry" from Chapter 6's *legō, legimus, legunt*. | ***Ferō · ferimus · ferunt.*** Just like *legō*. |
| Now "you carry · he carries · y'all carry." *Legō* has *legis, legit, legitis*. *Ferō* drops the vowel. | ***Fers · fert · fertis.*** Short and wild. |
| The infinitive. *Legō* has *legere*. *Ferō*? | ***Ferre***: *fer-re*, the vowel gone again. (Hold both *r*'s.) |
| Other tenses are pure *legō* (Chapter 17). "I was carrying." "I will carry, you will carry, he will carry." | ***Ferēbam.*** ***Feram, ferēs, feret*** (like *legam, legēs, leget*). And Chapter 25's keys: *ferō, ferre, tulī, lātum*. |
| Vergil, an old shepherd who has forgotten his songs: *Omnia fert aetās, animum quoque.* (*Aetās, aetātis*, f.: age, time of life. *Animus*: mind, memory. *Quoque*: also, Chapter 16.) | "**Time carries off** everything, the mind too." *Fert* can mean "carries away." |

!!! success "The Law of the Missing Vowel"
    *Ferō* is *legō* with a vowel missing. Where *legō* has a short *e* or *i* between its stem and an *s, t,* or *r*, *ferō* has nothing:

    | | *legō* | *ferō* |
    |---|---|---|
    | you | *legis* | ***fers*** |
    | he, she | *legit* | ***fert*** |
    | y'all | *legitis* | ***fertis*** |
    | to … | *legere* | ***ferre*** |
    | … ! | *lege* | ***fer*** |
    | y'all … ! | *legite* | ***ferte*** |

    Everything else matches *legō*: *ferō, ferimus, ferunt, ferēbam, feram, ferēs*. The perfect keys come from elsewhere: *tulī, lātum*.

## 6. *Fertur*, and three cousins

:: Ita fertur. || So it is said. So the story goes. (teaching line)

Cāseus fertur. Afferō, auferō, referō.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 30's passive of *legō*: *legor, legeris, legitur, legimur, legiminī, leguntur*. Apply the Law of the Missing Vowel to *ferō*. | ***Feror, ferris, fertur, ferimur, feriminī, feruntur.*** Only "you" and "he" lose their vowel (*fer-ris*, *fer-tur*). The infinitive "to be carried": ***ferrī***. |
| "The cheese is being carried by John." (*Iōanne*: Chapter 13.) Then Chapter 31: "The cheese has been carried." | ***Cāseus ab Iōanne fertur.*** · ***Cāseus lātus est.*** |
| *Fertur* has a second life: a story "is carried around," so ***fertur*** = "**it is said**," "the story goes." *Ita fertur.* What does it add to a tale (Chapter 26)? | "**So the story goes.**" A storyteller's shrug: I didn't see it, but people say so. (The full "he is said to…" with an infinitive: Chapter 41.) |
| Chapter 27's *afferō, afferre, attulī, allātum*, "bring to." Find *ad* in all four keys. | ***Ad-ferō → af-ferō***, ***ad-tulī → at-tulī***, ***ad-lātum → al-lātum***. The *d* turns into the next letter, because it's easier to say (like Chapter 19's *eum-dem → eundem*). |
| *Ab* + *ferō*: ***auferō, auferre, abstulī, ablātum***, "carry away, take away." Look hard at the fourth key. | ***Ablātum***, "**carried away**." Chapter 7's **ablative** is the Latin *cāsus ablātīvus*, the "carrying-away case," the case of *ab* and *ex*. Chapter 35's title, *Ablātīvus*, is waiting. |
| *Re-* + *ferō*: ***referō, referre, rettulī, relātum***, "bring back; report." Chapter 28 said the relative pronoun is named from *relātum*. Why does that fit? | *Relātum* = "**carried back**." A relative pronoun carries you back to the word it points to (its antecedent). Chapter 28's promise kept. |

## 7. *Fīō*: become, be made, happen

:: Ex lacte cāseus fit. || Cheese is made from milk. (teaching line; Chapter 14's *Cāseus ex lacte est*)

Quid fit? Lac cāseus fit!

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| A new verb: ***fīō***, "I become, I am made, I happen." Chapter 14 said *Cāseus ex lacte est.* Now: *Ex lacte cāseus fit.* | "Cheese **is made** from milk," "cheese **comes to be** from milk." |
| The chant: *fīō, fīs, fit, fīmus, fītis, fīunt.* Which family's endings does it wear? | Chapter 6's ***audiō***: *audiō, audīs, audit, audīmus, audītis, audiunt.* Same endings on *fī-*. (*Fīmus* and *fītis* are rare in real books.) |
| Macron check. *Fīō* and *fīunt* keep a long *ī* even before a vowel, which is unusual. Where does it go short? | In ***fit*** (before final *-t*, like *it*) and in the infinitive ***fierī*** (FI-e-ree), "to become, to be made." *Fierī* looks passive, like Chapter 30's *audīrī*, and that's a clue to §8. |
| Everyday chunk: ***Quid fit?*** And the answer ***Nihil fit***. | "**What's happening? What's going on?**" · "**Nothing's happening.**" |
| *Fīō* = "become," so like *est* it takes a naming-case word after it (Chapter 3's *Mālum magnum est*). "Grandpa is getting tired." · "Cornēlia is becoming wise." | ***Avus fessus fit.*** · ***Cornēlia sapiēns fit.*** *Fessus* and *sapiēns* are nominative, agreeing with the subject, just as after *est*. |
| Other tenses go like *audiō* (Chapter 17): "It was happening." "Tomorrow it will become cheese." | ***Fīēbat.*** · ***Crās cāseus fīet.*** (*Audiēbat, audiet*.) |

## 8. *Faciō, fit, factum est*

:: Fit via vī. || A way is made by force. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 2.494, the fall of Troy)

Faciō. Fit. Factum est.

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| By Chapter 30's rules, "is made" from *faciō* would be *facitur*. Romans almost never said it. What do you think they used instead? | ***Fit.*** *Fīō* works as the present passive of *faciō*: *fit* "**is made, is done**," *fīēbat* "was being made," *fīet* "will be made." |
| And "has been made," Chapter 31? | ***Factum est*** (and *facta est lūx*). So the dictionary lists ***fīō, fierī, factus sum***: *faciō* active; *fit* and *factum est* its passive. Chapter 25's *Factum est* was *fīō*'s perfect all along. |
| Vergil's Greeks break down the door of Priam's palace: *Fit via vī.* (*Vīs*, force; its ablative is *vī*.) Doer or tool? | The **tool**: plain ablative, no *ā/ab* (Chapter 30's Law of the Doer and the Tool). "**A way is made by force.**" Careful: this noun *vīs*, "force," is not Chapter 18's *vīs*, "you want." |
| A proverb: *Ex nihilō nihil fit.* | "**Nothing comes from nothing.**" (A later proverb; the idea is Lucretius's, who wrote *nīl… fierī dē nīlō posse*, "nothing can come from nothing," *On the Nature of Things* 1.205.) |
| Chapter 31's Genesis: *Fīat lūx.* *Fīat* is a mood you'll meet in Liber V (the "let it be" form, Chapter 49). For now, a chunk. What does *Fīat!* say alone? | "**Let it be done! So be it!**" *Fīat lūx*: "Let light be made," "Let there be light." |
| Chapter 9's *Pater noster, quī es in caelīs* goes on (Matthew 6:10): *Fīat voluntās tua.* (*Voluntās, voluntātis*, f.: will, wish.) Where have you seen *vol-*? | In ***volō***, "I want": *voluntās* is "what one wants," a will. "**Thy will be done**": "let your will happen." Which brings us to *volō*. |

## 9. *Volō, nōlō, mālō*

:: Volō, nōlō, mālō. || I want, I don't want, I'd rather. (teaching line)

Quid vīs? Quid māvīs?

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 18's *volō*, from memory, with its infinitive. | ***Volō, vīs, vult, volumus, vultis, volunt***; ***velle***. |
| Chapter 18's *nōlō* (*ne* + *volō*): *nōl-* where *volō* has *vol-*, otherwise *nōn* + the wild form. Chant it. | ***Nōlō, nōn vīs, nōn vult, nōlumus, nōn vultis, nōlunt***; ***nōlle***. |
| *Mālō* is *magis volō*, "I want more": "I prefer, I'd rather." Chapter 18 gave *mālō, māvīs, māvult*. The rule: *vol-* becomes ***māl-***; the *v-* forms get ***mā-*** in front. Finish it. | ***Mālumus, māvultis, mālunt***; infinitive ***mālle***. (*Mā-vīs* = *mā* + *vīs*; *mā-lō* from an old *mā-volō*.) |
| "Which do you prefer, the cheese or the cookies?" (Chapter 17's *an*, "or," in a question.) Then answer: "I prefer the cheese." | ***Cāseum an crustula māvīs?*** · ***Cāseum mālō.*** |
| "The kids prefer cookies · Grandpa prefers cheese · y'all prefer apples." | ***Līberī crustula mālunt · Avus cāseum māvult · Māla māvultis.*** |
| Chapter 18's trap, back again: *Mālō mālum.* | "**I prefer an apple.**" *Mālō* "I prefer" and *mālō* "to/by an apple" (Chapter 18) look the same; the sentence decides. |

!!! success "The Law of the Three Wishes"
    *Volō* is the wild one; *nōlō* and *mālō* copy it. Where *volō* has **vol-**, they have **nōl-** and **māl-**. Where *volō* has a **v-** form, *nōlō* puts ***nōn*** in front and *mālō* puts ***mā-*** in front.

    | | want | don't want | prefer |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | I | *volō* | *nōlō* | *mālō* |
    | you | *vīs* | *nōn vīs* | *māvīs* |
    | he, she | *vult* | *nōn vult* | *māvult* |
    | we | *volumus* | *nōlumus* | *mālumus* |
    | y'all | *vultis* | *nōn vultis* | *māvultis* |
    | they | *volunt* | *nōlunt* | *mālunt* |
    | to … | *velle* | *nōlle* | *mālle* |

    Commands: only ***nōlī, nōlīte*** (+ infinitive), "don't…!" (Chapter 6).

## 10. Other tenses, and "rather than"

:: Errāre… mālō cum Platōne… quam cum istīs vēra sentīre. || I'd rather be wrong with Plato than be right with those people. (Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations* 1.39)

Volēbam. Mālam. Nōluī.

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 18: outside the present, *volō* is tame: *volēbam, volam*. Do the same for *nōlō* and *mālō*. | ***Nōlēbam, nōlam*** (*nōlēs, nōlet…*) · ***mālēbam, mālam*** (*mālēs, mālet…*). Like *legēbam, legam*. |
| The keys: *volō, velle, voluī · nōlō, nōlle, nōluī · mālō, mālle, māluī.* What's missing? | The **fourth key**. These verbs have no passive and no fourth-key adjective: Latin never says "it was wanted" with *volō*. Three keys are all they need. |
| "I wanted to go, but I didn't want to carry the cheese." (Chapter 18's completing infinitive.) | ***Īre voluī, sed cāseum ferre nōluī.*** |
| *Mālō* loves Chapter 28's ***quam***, "than": "I'd rather stay home than go to the forum." | ***Domī manēre mālō quam ad forum īre.*** One infinitive on each side of *quam*. (*Quam* in comparisons: Chapter 33.) |
| Cicero, on Plato's teaching that the soul lives on: *Errāre… mālō cum Platōne… quam cum istīs vēra sentīre.* (*Errō*: be wrong, wander. *Platō, Platōnis*: Plato. *Vērus*: true. *Sentiō*: think, feel.) Find the two sides. | ***Errāre cum Platōne*** vs ***cum istīs vēra sentīre***, hinged on ***mālō… quam***: "I'd rather **be wrong** with Plato than **think true things** with those people." (*Istīs*: Chapter 19's *iste*, "those people of yours," a sneer.) |
| Chapter 6's motto, *Nōlī mē tangere* (John 20:17). What is it, now that you have the whole family? Then: "Don't go away, y'all!" | ***Nōlī***, the command of *nōlō*, + the completing infinitive ***tangere***: "be unwilling to touch me," "**Don't touch me.**" · ***Nōlīte abīre!*** |

## 11. Look-alikes

:: Quid fit? Quid fertur? || What's happening? What's the word? (teaching line)

Fert an ferit? Fit an facit? Eunt an erunt?

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Fert* · *ferit*. | ***Fert***: "he carries" (*ferō*). ***Ferit***: "he **strikes**," a different verb (*feriō, ferīre*). Put the vowel back into *ferō* and you hit someone. |
| *Eunt* · *erunt* (Chapter 16). | ***Eunt***: "they **go**" (now). ***Erunt***: "they **will be**." |
| *Fit* · *facit*. | ***Fit***: "is made, happens" (passive sense). ***Facit***: "makes, does" (active). *Avus cāseum facit · Cāseus fit.* |
| *Ībant* · *ībunt*. | ***Ībant***: "they were going" (*-bā-*). ***Ībunt***: "they will go" (*-bu-*). Chapter 16's Law of the Time Signs. |
| Name the verb and the tense, and translate: *abit · redībunt · fertur · attulit · fīunt · māvult.* | *Abit*: *abeō*, present, "he goes away." *Redībunt*: *redeō*, future, "they will come back." *Fertur*: *ferō*, present passive, "it is carried / it is said." *Attulit*: *afferō*, perfect, "he brought." *Fīunt*: *fīō*, present, "they become / are made." *Māvult*: *mālō*, present, "he prefers." |

## 12. Fix it

:: Errāre hūmānum est. || To err is human. (a later proverb; *hūmānus*, human)

Īunt? Ferit? Nōvult?

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "They go": *Īunt.* | ***Eunt.*** Before a vowel the stem is *e-* (Law of Ī and E). |
| "He carries the cheese": *Cāseum ferit.* | ***Cāseum fert.*** *Ferit* means "he strikes." |
| "Cheese is made from milk": *Ex lacte cāseus facit.* | ***Ex lacte cāseus fit.*** *Facit* is "makes"; "is made" is *fit*. |
| "You prefer cookies": *Crustula mālvīs.* | ***Crustula māvīs.*** Only *mā-* goes on the front of a *v-* form, not *māl-* (Law of the Three Wishes). |
| "He doesn't want to go": *Īre nōvult.* | ***Īre nōn vult.*** *Mālō* glues *mā-* onto *vult*, but *nōlō* doesn't glue: where *volō* has a *v-* form, *nōlō* uses ***nōn*** + that form. |
| "To carry": *ferere.* | ***Ferre.*** The vowel is missing (Law of the Missing Vowel). |

## 13. Lectiō: *Quid fit?*

:: Quid fit? Cāseus fit! || What's going on? Cheese is being made! (teaching line)

Ītis, fertis, redītis. Et Avus?

Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. As you read, hunt the three rule-breakers: every form of *eō* (and its compounds), *ferō*, and *fīō*, and every "want / don't want / would rather."

::: dialogue lectio
Pater | Hodiē ad forum īmus. Quis it mēcum? || Dad | Today we're going to the forum. Who's coming with me?
Līberī | Nōs īmus! Nōs īmus! || The kids | We're going! We're going!
Avus | Ego nōn eō. Domī manēre mālō. || Grandpa | I'm not going. I'd rather stay home.
Avia | Domī? Quid domī fit, Ave? || Grandma | At home? What's going on at home, Grandpa?
Avus | Nihil fit! Īte! Abīte! || Grandpa | Nothing's going on! Go! Go away, y'all!
Iōannēs | In forō cāseum magnum ēmī. Sed quis eum domum fert? || John | In the forum I bought a big cheese. But who's carrying it home?
Marcus | Nōn ego! Ferre nōlō. Nimis gravis est! || Marcus | Not me! I don't want to carry it. It's too heavy!
Quīntus | Ego crustula ferre mālō. || Quīntus | I'd rather carry the cookies.
Cornēlia | Iōannēs et ego cāseum ferimus. Fer, Iōannēs! || Cornēlia | John and I are carrying the cheese. Carry, John!
Pater | Ecce: cāseus ā līberīs fertur. Bene! || Dad | Look: the cheese is being carried by the kids. Good!
Māter | Nunc domum redīmus. || Mom | Now we're going back home.
Iōannēs | Via longa est. Cāseus gravis fit… et ego fessus fīō! || John | The road is long. The cheese is getting heavy… and I'm getting tired!
Cornēlia | Ecce vīlla! Sed quid fit? Fūmus ex culīnā exit! || Cornēlia | There's the house! But what's going on? Smoke is coming out of the kitchen!
Avus | Redīstis! Venīte, venīte! In culīnam īte! || Grandpa | You're back! Come, come! Go into the kitchen!
Māter | Ave, quid in culīnā facis? || Mom | Grandpa, what are you doing in the kitchen?
Avus | Cāseum faciō! Ecce: ex lacte cāseus fit! || Grandpa | I'm making cheese! Look: from milk, cheese is made!
Avia | In culīnā meā?! || Grandma | In my kitchen?!
Avus | Cāseum meum facere mālō quam emere. Iam nōn emam! || Grandpa | I'd rather make my own cheese than buy it. I won't buy any more!
Marcus | Sed ubi est cāseus tuus? || Marcus | But where is your cheese?
Avus | Nōndum cāseus est. Lac est. Crās cāseus fīet! || Grandpa | It isn't cheese yet. It's milk. Tomorrow it'll become cheese!
Quīntus | Ēheu! Canis in culīnam it! || Quīntus | Uh-oh! The dog is going into the kitchen!
Pater | Quid fit? Quid fit? || Dad | What's happening? What's happening?
Cornēlia | Canis lac Avī bibit… tōtum! || Cornēlia | The dog is drinking Grandpa's milk… all of it!
Avus | Abī, Cāsee! Exī ex culīnā! || Grandpa | Go away, Cheese! Get out of the kitchen!
Cāseus | Bau! || Cheese | Woof!
Marcus | Exit canis… cum lacte in stomachō! || Marcus | Exit the dog… with the milk in his stomach!
Avus | Ēheu! Crās cāseus fīet… in stomachō… || Grandpa | Alas! Tomorrow it'll become cheese… in the stomach of…
Omnēs | …Cāseī! || Everyone | …Cheese!
Iōannēs | Nōlī trīstis esse, Ave. Cāseum tibi tulimus. Ecce! || John | Don't be sad, Grandpa. We brought you a cheese. Look!
Avus | Cāseum emptum mālō! Fer eum ad mēnsam! || Grandpa | I prefer bought cheese! Bring it to the table!
Avia | Fīat! || Grandma | So be it!
:::

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Grandpa: *Domī manēre mālō.* Grandma: *Quid domī fit?* Why is she suspicious? | Grandpa **would rather** (*mālō*) stay home, and Grandma asks "**What's going on** at home?" (*fit*, from *fīō*). He answers too fast: *Nihil fit! Īte! Abīte!* "Nothing! Go! Go away!" |
| *Ferre nōlō* · *crustula ferre mālō* · *cāseum ferimus*. Sort the three wishes. | Marcus **doesn't want** to carry (*nōlō* + *ferre*), Quīntus **would rather** carry cookies (*mālō* + *ferre*), Cornēlia and John **are carrying** it (*ferimus*). |
| Dad: *cāseus ā līberīs fertur.* Voice, and why *ā*? | **Passive** present, "is being carried," with the agent ***ā līberīs*** (living doers take *ā/ab*, Chapter 30). |
| *Cāseus gravis fit… et ego fessus fīō!* Why *gravis* and *fessus*, not *gravem* and *fessum*? | After *fīō*, "become," the describing word is **nominative**, agreeing with the subject, just as after *est*: the cheese is getting heavy, John is getting tired. |
| *Abī, Cāsee!* Why not *Cāseus*? | The **vocative**: Chapter 9's *-us → -e* (*Marce, Quīnte*). Calling the dog named Cheese: ***Cāsee!*** |
| *Exit canis* and *Crās cāseus fīet… in stomachō… Cāseī!* What's the joke? | *Exit* is a stage direction, "the dog goes out," like Shakespeare. And Grandpa's milk will "become cheese tomorrow," but inside ***Cāseus***, the dog named Cheese. The cheese ends up in someone's stomach after all. |

## 14. Sound check

:: Ī, dīc, redī! || Go, speak, come back! (teaching line)

Audī et dīc.

{#g-14}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *eō · eunt · īmus · it*? | E-oh · E-unt · EE-mus · IT. Two syllables each in *eō* and *eunt*: not "yo," not "yunt." |
| *abeunt · exeunt · redīstis · trānsit*? | A-be-unt · EK-se-unt · re-DEES-tis · TRAHN-sit. Classical *x* = *ks*. |

| *fīō · fīunt · fit · fierī · fīat*? | FEE-oh · FEE-unt · FIT · FI-e-ree · FEE-at. Long *ī* in *fīō, fīunt, fīat*; short in *fit, fierī*. |
| *mālō · māvīs · māvult · mālle · voluī*? | MAH-loh · MAH-wees · MAH-wult · MAHL-le · WO-lu-ee. Classical *v* = *w*; church Latin says "MAH-vult," "VO-lu-ee." |

## Speak it aloud

:: Ī et refer! || Go, and report back! (teaching line)

1. Chant *eō* in four tenses (*eō…*, *ībam…*, *ībō…*, *iī…*), then *abeō* and *redeō* in the present.
2. Chant *ferō, fers, fert…* and *feror, ferris, fertur…*, then *fīō, fīs, fit…*.
3. Chant *volō*, *nōlō*, and *mālō* side by side, with *velle, nōlle, mālle*.
4. Say the three Laws in your own words: Ī and E, the Missing Vowel, the Three Wishes.
5. Give orders: "Go away! Come back! Go out, y'all! Carry the cheese! Don't go!" (*Abī! Redī! Exīte! Fer cāseum! Nōlī īre!*)
6. Say three things you'd rather do than something else, with *mālō… quam…*.
7. The real lines: *Ībam forte Viā Sacrā · Abiit, excessit, ēvāsit, ērūpit · Sīc trānsit glōria mundī · Omnia fert aetās · Fit via vī · Fīat voluntās tua · Nōlī mē tangere.*
8. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part (the dog too). Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
