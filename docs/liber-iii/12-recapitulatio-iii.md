# Caput XXXVI: Recapitulātiō III

*Liber III, Fābulae · Chapter 36*

*In which nothing is new and every story tool comes back. Book III gave you the four keys and the finished fact, film and snapshot, the step behind the step, the pointing-back word* quī, *the question words and their cousins, the passive in six tenses, the rule-breakers* eō, ferō, fīō, *"taller, tallest," "fast, faster," and the six new jobs of the ablative. Today they all fly together. Chapter 34 promised you'd "meet the whole story soon": here it is, the oldest flying lesson in the world, Daedalus and Icarus, told by Grandpa in Book III Latin and then lived (badly) by Quīntus in the kitchen. Speaking goal: tell a whole story in the past, with who did what, what was done, and how.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"

    - **Reading:** *Daedalus et Īcarus* (Ovid's story, adapted into Book III Latin) and *Diē quārtā* (Quīntus tries it in the kitchen)

## 1. *Vēnī, vīdī, vīcī* — four keys

:: Vēnī, vīdī, vīcī. || I came, I saw, I conquered. (Julius Caesar, 47 BC; Suetonius, *Julius* 37; Chapter 25)

Amō, amāre, amāvī, amātum. Cadō, cadere, cecidī, cāsum.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the Four Keys: what do keys 1–2, key 3, and key 4 each give you? | Keys 1–2: the **present stem** (present, imperfect, future). Key 3: the **perfect stem** (perfect, pluperfect, future perfect active). Key 4: the fourth-key adjective (the finished passive, Chapter 31). (Chapter 25.) |
| Law of the Finished Fact: chant the perfect of *amō*. | *Amāvī, amāvistī, amāvit, amāvimus, amāvistis, amāvērunt.* One set of endings for every family: *-ī, -istī, -it, -imus, -istis, -ērunt*. |
| Key 3 of *habeō, legō, audiō, capiō, faciō, veniō*? | *Habuī · lēgī · audīvī · cēpī · fēcī · vēnī.* Third family and *-iō*: always check the dictionary. |
| The early rule-breakers: "I was," "I went," "I carried," "I gave," "I was able." | *Fuī · iī (īvī) · tulī · dedī · potuī.* (Chapter 25.) |
| Read the blurb's second line. *Cadō* is new ("fall"; Chapter 34 glossed it). Its fourth key hides a word you've used since Chapter 7. | ***Cāsum*** → ***cāsus***, "**a fall**." Roman grammarians copied the Greek word for a case, *ptōsis*, "a falling": each case is one way a noun "falls" (even the naming form, the "upright" *cāsus rēctus*). So *cāsus ablātīvus* (Chapter 35) is, word for word, "the carrying-away fall." Keep *cecidī* ready: someone in today's story falls. |

## 2. *Nox erat* — stage and step

:: Fuimus Trōes, fuit Īlium. || We Trojans are no more; Troy is no more. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 2.325; Chapter 26)

Avus dormiēbat. Subitō canis lātrāvit.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| What is **aspect**? Which tense is film, which is snapshot? | How a verb looks at an action: from **inside** while it runs (imperfect: film) or from **outside** as one finished whole (perfect: snapshot). Tense = when; aspect = how you look. (Chapter 26.) |
| Law of the Stage and the Step: read the blurb. Which verb is the stage? Which is the step? | ***Dormiēbat***: the stage (what was going on). ***Lātrāvit***: the step (what happened). Ask "**And then what?**": a verb that answers it is a perfect. |
| Law of Erat and Fuit: *Nox erat* vs *Urbs antīqua fuit*? | *Erat* paints the scene at the time; *fuit* states a closed fact from outside. *Fuit Īlium*: "Troy has been," so "Troy is over." |
| Habit vs once: *Avus saepe dormiēbat* · *Avus semel dormīvit*? | "Grandpa **used to** sleep often" (habit, imperfect) · "Grandpa slept **once**" (one event, perfect). And the "trying" imperfect: *cāseum capiēbat*, "he **tried** to take the cheese." |
| Story words: once upon a time, suddenly, then, finally. And Grandpa's favorite question. | ***Ōlim, subitō, deinde (tum), tandem.*** · ***Quid tum?*** "And then what?" (Chapter 26.) |

## 3. *Iam ēderat* — the step behind the step

:: Ut sēmentem fēceris, ita metēs. || As you sow, so shall you reap. (Cicero, *Dē Ōrātōre* 2.261; Chapter 27)

Avus vēnit. Canis cāseum iam ēderat.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of Eram and Erō: build "I had loved" and "I will have loved." | Perfect stem + *eram…*: ***amāveram, amāverās, amāverat…*** · perfect stem + *erō…*: ***amāverō, amāveris, amāverit…*** (Chapter 27.) |
| The trap: *amāvērunt · amāverint · amāverant · erunt*? | They loved · they will have loved (**-erint**, never *-erunt*) · they had loved · they will be. |
| Law of the Earlier Step: read the blurb. Why is *ēderat* pluperfect? | The dog ate it **before** Grandpa came. Before a past step → pluperfect. "Grandpa came. The dog **had** already eaten the cheese." |
| "If you eat the cheese, Grandma will shout." Which tense for "eat"? | ***Sī cāseum ēderis, Avia clāmābit.*** Future perfect: the eating comes first. English hides it behind a present; Latin keeps the clock. |
| The motto. Which verb comes first? | ***Fēceris*** (future perfect, "you will have sown") comes before ***metēs*** (future, "you will reap"). |

## 4. *Quī, quae, quod* — pointing back

:: Ego sum quī sum. || I am who I am. (Exodus 3:14, Vulgate; Chapter 28)

Cāseus quem Pater ēmit. Vir cuius vōx nōn dulcis est.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| What are a **relative pronoun**, an **antecedent**, and a **relative clause**? | A pointing word that starts a describing clause · the word it points back to · a little sentence that works like a long adjective. (Chapter 28.) |
| Law of the Relative: *cāseus quem Pater ēmit*. Why masculine singular? Why accusative? | Gender and number from the antecedent ***cāseus***; case from its own job: Dad **bought it** (object). |
| Chant the singular, all three genders. | *Quī, cuius, cui, quem, quō · quae, cuius, cui, quam, quā · quod, cuius, cui, quod, quō.* |
| "The man who loves cheese. The dog whose name is Cāseus. The boys to whom I gave the cheese." | ***Vir quī cāseum amat. Canis cuius nōmen est Cāseus. Puerī quibus cāseum dedī.*** |
| "The house in which we live; the friends with whom I walk." | ***Domus in quā habitāmus · amīcī quibuscum ambulō*** (*cum* glued on, like Chapter 35's *mēcum*). (*Habitō*: live, Chapter 34.) |

## 5. *Quis? Aliquis? Quisque?* — question words and cousins

:: Quis custōdiet ipsōs custōdēs? || Who will guard the guards themselves? (Juvenal, *Satires* 6.347–348; Chapter 29)

Quis venit? Quī vir? Aliquis venit. Vir quīdam venit.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the Question Words: which two shapes are new, and where does the rest come from? | Only ***quis*** (who?) and ***quid*** (what?). The rest is borrowed from the relative: *cuius, cui, quem, quō* and the whole plural. (Chapter 29.) |
| Law of Alone and Company: *Quis venit?* vs *Quī vir venit?* | **Alone**: *quis, quid*. **With a noun**: *quī, quae, quod*, agreeing with it. "Who is coming?" · "Which man is coming?" |
| Law of the Cousins: someone, a certain man, each one. | ***Aliquis · vir quīdam · quisque*** (*ali-quis, quī-dam, quis-que*: the question word with a tag). |
| Crack: *Cui bonō? · Quid novī? · Suum cuique.* | "Who stood to gain?" (to whom as a good) · "What's new?" (what of new) · "To each his own" (*cuique*: to each). |

## 6. *Amor, amāris* — the passive

:: Laudātur ab hīs, culpātur ab illīs. || He is praised by these, blamed by those. (Horace, *Satires* 1.2.11; Chapter 30)

Cāseus ab Avō editur. Cāseus manū capitur.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| **Voice**: what's the difference between active and passive? | Does the subject **do** it (active, *amat*) or **receive** it (passive, *amātur*)? (Chapter 30.) |
| Law of the Passive Endings: chant *amor*. | *Amor, amāris, amātur, amāmur, amāminī, amantur.* (*-r, -ris, -tur · -mur, -minī, -ntur*.) |
| Law of the Doer and the Tool: read the blurb. | ***Ab Avō***: the doer, *ā/ab* + ablative (agent). ***Manū***: the tool, plain ablative (means). Company would be *cum*. |
| Law of the Passive Clock: "it was being read," "it will be read," "to be read." Watch *legeris / legēris*. | ***Legēbātur · legētur · legī.*** *Legeris* = you are read (now); *legēris* = you will be read (later). (*Legī* "to be read" vs *lēgī* "I read": Chapter 25's long ē.) |

## 7. *Amātus est* — the finished passive

:: Iacta ālea est. || The die has been cast. (Caesar at the Rubicon, in Suetonius, *Julius* 32; Chapter 31)

Cāseus ēsus est. Ālae factae sunt.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the Two Pieces: *Cāseus ēsus est.* What are the two pieces, and which one agrees? | The **fourth-key adjective** *ēsus* (agrees with the subject: masculine, singular, nominative) + a form of ***sum*** (person and time). (Chapter 31.) |
| Read the blurb's second line. *Āla, ālae*: wing. Why *factae*? | ***Ālae*** is feminine plural, so the adjective is ***factae***. "The wings were made / have been made." |
| Law of One Word and Two: perfect, pluperfect, future perfect passive of *amō* ("he"). | ***Amātus est · amātus erat · amātus erit.*** The first three passive tenses are one word; these three are two. |
| Trap: *amātus sum* vs *amor*; *amābor* vs *amātus erō*. | *Amātus sum*: I **was** loved (finished). *Amor*: I **am** loved. *Amābor*: I will be loved. *Amātus erō*: I will **have been** loved. |
| *Cāseus ā cane ēsus erat.* | "The cheese **had been eaten** by the dog." Pluperfect passive + agent. |

## 8. *Eō, ferō, fīō* — the rule-breakers

:: Fit via vī. || A way is made by force. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 2.494; Chapter 32)

Abī! Fer cāseum! Quid fit? Mālō domī manēre.

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of Ī and E: chant *eō* present. "Go away! Go out! Come back!" | *Eō, īs, it, īmus, ītis, eunt* (**e-** before a vowel). · ***Abī! Exī! Redī!*** (Chapter 32.) |
| Law of the Missing Vowel: *ferō* present and infinitive. "It is carried." | *Ferō, fers, fert, ferimus, fertis, ferunt · ferre.* · ***Fertur*** (also "it is said"). |
| *Fīō*: what three meanings? "Cheese is made from milk." | Become · be made · happen. ***Ex lacte cāseus fit.*** (*Fit* is the passive of *faciō*.) |
| Law of the Three Wishes: "you want," "you don't want," "you'd rather." | ***Vīs · nōn vīs · māvīs.*** *Vol-* → *nōl-, māl-*; a *v-* form gets *nōn* or *mā-*. |
| "I'd rather stay home than go to the forum." Fix: *Īunt, nōvult, mālvīs.* | ***Domī manēre mālō quam ad forum īre.*** · *Eunt, nōn vult, māvīs.* |

## 9. *Melior et optimus* — comparing things

:: Exēgī monumentum aere perennius. || I have finished a monument more lasting than bronze. (Horace, *Odes* 3.30.1; Chapter 33)

Marcus altior est quam Quīntus. Marcus Quīntō altior est.

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Name the three **degrees of comparison** of *altus*. Law of the Comparative: how is *altior* built, and how does it decline? | **Positive** *altus* · **comparative** *altior, altius* · **superlative** *altissimus*. *-ior / -ius* on the genitive stem; declined like *rēx / corpus* (*altiōris, cum puerō altiōre*), not an i-stem. (Chapter 33.) |
| Law of *Quam* and the Ablative: read the blurb two ways. | ***Quam*** + the same case (*quam Quīntus*), or the "than" ablative with no *quam* (*Quīntō*). "Marcus is taller than Quīntus." |
| Law of the Superlative: superlatives of *fortis, ācer, facilis*? | ***Fortissimus · ācerrimus · facillimus*** (*-issimus*; *-er* → *-errimus*; six *-ilis* → *-illimus*). |
| Law of the Five Rebels: good, bad, big, small, much, in three degrees. | *Bonus, melior, optimus · malus, peior, pessimus · magnus, maior, maximus · parvus, minor, minimus · multus, plūs, plūrimus.* |
| "More cheese, please!" Why the genitive? | ***Plūs cāseī, quaesō!*** *Plūs* is a neuter noun, "more **of** cheese" (Chapter 33; Chapter 22's Law of the Part). |

## 10. *Celeriter!* — how-words

:: Sat citō, sī sat bene. || Soon enough, if well enough. (Cato the Elder, as quoted by Saint Jerome, *Letters* 66.9; Chapter 34)

Lentē. Celeriter. Celerius. Celerrimē. Quam celerrimē!

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| What is an **adverb**? | A **how-word** (*ad verbum*, "next to the verb"): it tells how, when, where, or how much, and never changes its ending. (Chapter 34.) |
| Law of the How-Word: adverbs from *lentus, fortis, celer, sapiēns, facilis*. | ***Lentē · fortiter · celeriter · sapienter · facile.*** (*-us* → long *-ē*; third family *-iter*; *-nt-* → *-nter*; *facile* is the neuter.) |
| Law of the Running Steps: "faster," "very fast," "as fast as possible." | ***Celerius*** (the neuter comparative) · ***celerrimē*** (superlative + *-ē*) · ***quam celerrimē***. |
| Law of the Five Rebels Running: well, badly, much, little, greatly, in three degrees. | *Bene, melius, optimē · male, peius, pessimē · multum, plūs, plūrimum · parum, minus, minimē · magnopere, magis, maximē.* |
| Adjective or adverb? *Cornēlia ___ est* (fast) · *Cornēlia ___ currit* (fast). | ***Celeris*** (describes her: adjective) · ***celeriter*** (describes the running: adverb). |

## 11. *Magnā cum cūrā* — the ablative map

:: Summā cum laude. || With highest praise. (the top honor on an American diploma; Chapter 35)

Cum Avō. Magnā cum cūrā. Metū. Multō maior. Nātū maior. Vir magnā barbā.

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the Three Old Jobs: which three old cases share the ablative's coat? | **From** (*ex, ab, domō*, the doer, the "than") · **with / by** (*cum*, the tool, the price, most of Chapter 35's jobs) · **in / at** (*in* + where, the hour). (Chapter 35.) |
| Law of the Three Withs: with whom, how, with what? | ***Cum Avō*** (company) · ***magnā (cum) cūrā*** (manner) · ***baculō*** (the tool: plain, never *cum*). |
| Law of the Six Questions: ask the blurb's last four. | ***Metū***: why? (cause) · ***multō maior***: by how much? (degree of difference) · ***nātū maior***: in what respect? (respect) · ***vir magnā barbā***: what kind? (description). |
| "Grandpa is sick from too much cheese." "A little later." "All the same." | ***Avus nimiō cāseō aegrōtat. · Paulō post. · Nihilō minus.*** |
| Chapter 34's two loose ends, untied in Chapter 35: *magnopere* and *quōmodo*? | ***Magnō opere***, "with great work" → greatly. ***Quō modō***, "in what way?" → how? Two ablatives of manner glued into adverbs. |

## 12. Lectiō: *Daedalus et Īcarus*

:: Et caelum certē patet; ībimus illāc. || And the sky, at least, lies open: that's the way we'll go. (Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 8.186, Daedalus speaking)

Ōlim in īnsulā Crētā erat vir quīdam, nōmine Daedalus.

Grandpa tells the story; the kids interrupt. Scenes I and II are **adapted** from Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 8.183–235: the plot and a few phrases are Ovid's, but the sentences are rebuilt in Book III Latin (Ovid's own words come in §13). Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. New words: *īnsula*, island; *Crēta*, Crete; *Mīnōs*, King Minos; *penna*, feather; *cēra*, wax; *āla*, wing; *caelum*, sky (Chapter 9's *in caelīs*); *sōl*, sun (Chapter 3's *sub sōle*); *mollis*, soft; *moneō*, warn; *cadō*, fall (Chapter 34 glossed it). Glossed: *teneō*, hold, keep; *pateō*, lie open; *colligō*, gather; *līnum*, thread; *iungō*, join; *interim*, meanwhile; *lūdō*, play; *sīcut*, just like; *fugiō, fūgī*, flee, escape (Chapter 6); *āēr*, air (ablative *āere*); *unda*, wave; *uterque*, each of the two; *ōsculum*, kiss; *piscātor*, fisherman; *pāstor*, shepherd; *audāx*, bold; *relinquō, relīquī*, leave behind; *nūdus*, bare; *miser*, poor, wretched.

### I. *Ālae*

::: dialogue lectio-1
Avus | Ōlim in īnsulā Crētā erat vir quīdam, nōmine Daedalus. Faber erat, omnium sapientissimus. || Grandpa | Once upon a time, on the island of Crete, there was a certain man, Daedalus by name. He was a craftsman, the wisest of all.
Avus | Mīnōs, rēx Crētae, eum in īnsulā tenēbat. Daedalus cum fīliō suō, Īcarō, marī clausus erat. || Grandpa | Minos, the king of Crete, was keeping him on the island. Daedalus, with his son Icarus, had been shut in by the sea.
Marcus | Quā dē causā nōn nāve fūgērunt? || Marcus | For what reason didn't they escape by ship?
Avus | Nāvēs omnēs ā mīlitibus rēgis custōdiēbantur. Tum Daedalus dīxit: "Terra clausa est, et mare clausum est. Sed caelum patet! Per caelum ībimus!" || Grandpa | All the ships were being guarded by the king's soldiers. Then Daedalus said: "The land is shut, and the sea is shut. But the sky lies open! We'll go through the sky!"
Quīntus | Per caelum? Quōmodo? || Quīntus | Through the sky? How?
Avus | Daedalus pennās avium collēgit: prīmum minimās, deinde maiōrēs, tandem maximās. || Grandpa | Daedalus gathered birds' feathers: first the smallest, then bigger ones, finally the biggest.
Avus | Pennae līnō et cērā iungēbantur. Ita ālae factae sunt, sīcut ālae avium. || Grandpa | The feathers were being joined with thread and wax. In this way wings were made, just like birds' wings.
Avus | Puer Īcarus, quī nōndum sapiēns erat, interim lūdēbat: pennās capiēbat et cēram digitīs tangēbat. || Grandpa | The boy Icarus, who was not yet wise, was playing meanwhile: he kept grabbing feathers and poking the wax with his fingers.
Avia | Sīcut Quīntus noster! || Grandma | Just like our Quīntus!
Avus | Tandem ālae parātae erant. Daedalus ipse prīmus volāvit, et in āere mānsit. || Grandpa | At last the wings were ready. Daedalus himself flew first, and stayed up in the air.
Cornēlia | Volāvit?! Sīcut avis? || Cornēlia | He flew?! Like a bird?
:::

### II. *Inter utrumque volā!*

::: dialogue lectio-2
Avus | Deinde pater fīlium monuit: || Grandpa | Then the father warned his son:
Daedalus | "Audī, Īcare! Sī dēmissius volābis, undae pennās gravēs facient. Sī altius volābis, sōl cēram mollem faciet." || Daedalus | "Listen, Icarus! If you fly lower, the waves will make the feathers heavy. If you fly higher, the sun will make the wax soft."
Daedalus | "Inter utrumque volā! Post mē volā, et nōlī altius volāre!" || Daedalus | "Fly between the two! Fly behind me, and don't fly higher!"
Avus | Pater fīliō ōscula dedit, et manūs eius tremēbant. || Grandpa | The father gave his son kisses, and his hands were trembling.
Avus | Prīmum lentē et magnā cum cūrā volābant: pater ante, fīlius post. || Grandpa | At first they flew slowly and with great care: the father in front, the son behind.
Avus | Piscātor quīdam et pāstor eōs vīdērunt et clāmāvērunt: "Deī sunt!" || Grandpa | A certain fisherman and a shepherd saw them and shouted: "They're gods!"
Avus | Sed puer audācior fīēbat. Patrem relīquit, et altius, altius volāvit. || Grandpa | But the boy was getting bolder. He left his father behind and flew higher, higher.
Īcarus | "Ecce, Pater! Altior sum quam avēs! Altissimē omnium volō!" || Icarus | "Look, Father! I'm higher than the birds! I'm flying the highest of all!"
Avus | Sōl, quī calidissimus erat, cēram mollīvit. Pennae, quae cērā iungēbantur, cecidērunt. || Grandpa | The sun, which was very hot, softened the wax. The feathers, which were held together by the wax, fell.
Avus | Īcarus bracchia nūda movēbat, sed iam nōn volābat. "Pater! Pater!" clāmābat. || Grandpa | Icarus kept moving his bare arms, but he wasn't flying anymore. "Father! Father!" he kept shouting.
Avus | Et in mare cecidit, quod nunc ā nōmine eius "Īcarium" appellātur. || Grandpa | And he fell into the sea, which is now called "Icarian" after his name.
Daedalus | "Īcare! Īcare! Ubi es?" || Daedalus | "Icarus! Icarus! Where are you?"
Avus | Pater miser pennās in undīs vīdit. Fīlium iam nōn vīdit. || Grandpa | The poor father saw feathers on the waves. His son he did not see anymore.
Quīntus | Trīstis fābula! Quā dē causā Īcarus patrem nōn audīvit? || Quīntus | A sad story! Why didn't Icarus listen to his father?
Avus | Quia audāx erat, et nimis altē volāvit. Nē quid nimis, Quīnte! || Grandpa | Because he was bold, and he flew too high. Nothing too much, Quīntus!
:::

### III. *Diē quārtā*

::: dialogue lectio-3
Avia | Hodiē, diē quārtā, Avus rūrsus cāseum edere potest… sed paulum! || Grandma | Today, on the fourth day, Grandpa can eat cheese again… but just a little!
Avus | Tandem! Ubi est cāseus quem ēmistī? || Grandpa | At last! Where's the cheese you bought?
Avia | In armāriō altissimō, quod canis tangere nōn potest. Ā mē servātur. || Grandma | In the highest cupboard, which the dog can't touch. It's being kept by me.
Avus | Ēheu! Armārium altius est quam caput meum, et pennās nōn habeō! || Grandpa | Oh no! The cupboard is higher than my head, and I don't have feathers!
Quīntus | Ego pennās habeō! Ecce! || Quīntus | I have feathers! Look!
Avia | Quīnte! Illae pennae ē pulvīnō meō sunt! || Grandma | Quīntus! Those feathers are out of my pillow!

## 13. Ovid's own words

:: Inter utrumque volā. || Fly between the two. (Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 8.206)

"Īcare," dīxit, "Īcare," dīxit, "ubi es?"

The real story is in Ovid's *Metamorphoses* (all but finished by AD 8, when Augustus sent Ovid into exile), Book 8, lines 183–235. Ovid writes in verse, with subjunctives and participles you'll meet in Books IV and V, so §12 rebuilt his sentences. But some of his lines are already yours.

## Speak it aloud

:: Nārrandō discimus. || We learn by telling. (Chapter 26)

1. Say the four keys of *amō, videō, legō, capiō, veniō, ferō, cadō*.
2. Chant *amāvī…*, *amāveram…*, *amāverō…*; then *amor…*, *amātus sum…*.
3. Glue three sentence pairs with *quī, quae, quod*; then ask *Quis? Quid? Cui?* about the myth.
4. Flip three sentences from the myth to the passive with *ā/ab*: *Daedalus ālās fēcit → Ālae ā Daedalō factae sunt*.
5. *Eō, ferō, fīō, mālō* in the present, aloud; three commands: *Abī! Fer! Redī!*
6. Compare: Icarus flew higher than the birds, the highest of all; Daedalus flew more slowly and more carefully.
7. Ask the ablatives: *nōmine · cum fīliō · marī · līnō et cērā · magnā cum cūrā · nimiō cāseō*.
8. Tell the whole myth in your own Latin in eight to ten lines (stage, steps, warning, fall). Then read the *Lectiō* aloud, all three scenes, and hide the Latin and rebuild it.
