# Caput XXXI: Amātus Est

*Liber III, Fābulae · Chapter 31*

*In which the cheese is finally, officially, eaten. Chapter 30 gave you the passive for things happening now, then, and later: "the cheese is being eaten," "was being eaten," "will be eaten." But it left one sentence on the table: "the cheese **was eaten**," finished, a snapshot. That needs Chapter 25's fourth key, the one you have been writing in every Vocābula list since (*amātum, ēsum, factum*). Today it turns into an adjective, joins up with* sum, *and does the job:* Cāseus ēsus est. *Chapter 25's motto* Factum est *opens up, and so do Caesar's* Gallia est omnis dīvīsa, *the first light of Genesis, and Caesar's die at the Rubicon. Speaking goal: say what has been done, what had been done, and what will have been done, and by whom.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Cāseus ēsus est.* · *Ā quō ēsus est?* · *Factum est.* · *Capsa aperta est!* · *Cāseus iam ēsus erat.* · *Hōrā tertiā cāseus ēsus erit.* · *Vīsus sum!*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** participle (a verb-adjective); perfect passive participle (the fourth-key adjective: "loved," "eaten," "done"); perfect, pluperfect, and future perfect passive (done, done before then, done by a later time)
    - **Forms:** *amātus, -a, -um* · *amātus sum, es, est · amātī sumus, estis, sunt* · *amātus eram* · *amātus erō*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Two Pieces · the Law of One Word and Two
    - **Words:** *pōnō, claudō, aperiō, mittō* with their four keys; *inveniō*'s fourth key
    - **Reading:** *Cāseus ēsus est!* (a new cheese, a shut box, an open box, and a bed with a surprise in it)
    - **Speaking:** say "the cheese has been eaten," "the box had been shut," and "by three o'clock it will have been eaten," with *ā/ab*

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep three old friends close: Chapter 25's fourth key, Chapter 3's agreement (*magnus, magna, magnum*), and Chapter 16's and 27's *sum, eram, erō*. Today they work together.

## 1. The fourth key

:: Factum est. || It has been done. It is done. (Chapter 25's motto)

Cāseus ēsus est. Tandem!

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 30 ended with a promise. "The cheese **was eaten**," a finished snapshot, could not be said with *-r, -ris, -tur*. What did it need? | Chapter 25's **fourth key** plus *sum*. Today. |
| Chapter 25's keys for "eat": *edō, edere, ēdī, ēsum.* Which key have you never used? | The fourth, ***ēsum***. Every Vocābula list since Chapter 25 has carried one (*amātum, factum, emptum*), banked for later. Later is now. |
| Change *ēsum*'s ending to match a masculine noun, the way Chapter 3's *magnum* becomes *magnus*: "the eaten cheese." | ***Cāseus ēsus***, "the eaten cheese." The fourth key is an adjective in disguise: ***ēsus, ēsa, ēsum***, "eaten." |
| Plain English: a **participle** is a verb that works as an adjective, a "verb-adjective" (Latin *participium*, "taking part," because it takes part in both). English has them too: "eaten," "loved," "done." Is "the **eaten** cheese" a verb or an adjective? | Both at once: it comes from the verb "eat," but it describes the cheese like an adjective. That is a participle. |
| Grammar books call *ēsus, amātus, factus* the **perfect passive participle**: "perfect" because the action is finished, "passive" because the noun received it. In this book it is simply **the fourth-key adjective**. (More participles: Chapter 37.) What does *amātus* mean? | "**Loved**," "having been loved." *Cāseus amātus*, "the loved cheese." |
| Now add Chapter 2's *est*: *Cāseus ēsus est.* | "**The cheese was eaten**," or "**has been eaten**." The finished passive, at last. Chapter 25's *Factum est* is the same build: *factum* (from *faciō*, fourth key *factum*) + *est*: "**it has been done**," "it is done." |

## 2. *Ēsus, ēsa, ēsum*

:: Gallia est omnis dīvīsa in partēs trēs. || Gaul, as a whole, is divided into three parts. (Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.1)

Cāseus ēsus est. Pars ēsa est. Māla ēsa sunt.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Ēsus* is an adjective, so it agrees with its noun like *magnus*. "A piece (*pars*, f.) has been eaten." | ***Pars ēsa est.*** Feminine, so *ēsa*. |
| "The apple (*mālum*, n.) has been eaten." | ***Mālum ēsum est.*** Neuter, so *ēsum*. (Now *ēsum* is both the dictionary key and the neuter adjective.) |
| Plural. "The cheeses have been eaten." Two pieces change: the adjective and the *sum*. | ***Cāseī ēsī sunt.*** *Ēsī* agrees with *cāseī* (masculine plural); *sunt* agrees with *cāseī* (they). |
| "The apples have been eaten." · "The pieces have been eaten." | ***Māla ēsa sunt.*** (neuter plural *-a*, Chapter 4's Neuter Twins) · ***Partēs ēsae sunt.*** (feminine plural *-ae*: *partēs* is third family, but *ēsae* is first-second, Chapter 20's Two Families) |
| Chapter 15's Caesar at last: *Gallia est omnis dīvīsa in partēs trēs.* (*Dīvidō, dīvidere, dīvīsī, dīvīsum*: divide.) Find the two pieces, and why *dīvīsa*? | ***Est … dīvīsa***, with *omnis* in between: the pieces can stand apart. *Dīvīsa* is feminine singular to agree with ***Gallia***. Word for word: "Gaul has been divided," so "is divided": the dividing is done, and the result stands. (You can also read *dīvīsa* as a plain adjective, "divided"; either way it is the fourth key, agreeing with *Gallia*.) |
| *Cāseus ēsus est.* Which piece tells you **what** happened, and which tells you **the time**? | ***Ēsus*** tells what: eaten, and it agrees with the cheese. ***Est*** tells the time and the person. |

!!! success "The Law of the Two Pieces"
    The finished passive is **two words**:

    1. **The fourth-key adjective**, which agrees with the subject in gender and number (and is nominative), like *magnus, -a, -um*: *ēsus, ēsa, ēsum · ēsī, ēsae, ēsa*.
    2. **A form of *sum***, which tells the person and the time.

    *Cāseus ēsus est · Pars ēsa est · Mālum ēsum est · Cāseī ēsī sunt.* The pieces may stand apart (*est … dīvīsa*) or swap places (*est ēsus*).

## 3. *Amātus sum*: all six people

:: Amātus sum. || I was loved; I have been loved. (teaching line; not "I am loved")

Amātus sum. Amāta sum. Amātī sumus.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 5's *sum, es, est, sumus, estis, sunt*, after *amātus*. Chant it (a man speaking). | ***Amātus sum, amātus es, amātus est, amātī sumus, amātī estis, amātī sunt.*** "I was (have been) loved, you were loved, he was loved, we were loved, y'all were loved, they were loved." |
| Grandpa says *Amātus sum.* Does he mean "I am loved"? | **No.** "I am loved" is Chapter 30's ***amor***. *Amātus sum* is "**I was loved**," "I have been loved": finished. The *sum* looks like "am," but with the fourth key it makes the perfect. |
| Cornēlia says "I have been loved by Grandma." The adjective agrees with the speaker. | ***Ab Aviā amāta sum.*** Feminine: *amāta*. |
| Mom and Cornēlia together: "We have been loved by Dad." | ***Ā Patre amātae sumus.*** Two women, so feminine plural *-ae*. (A mixed group, men and women, takes the masculine *amātī*.) |
| "Y'all have been loved by the dog" (to the boys). | ***Ā cane amātī estis.*** |
| Grandpa, caught in the kitchen at night: "I have been seen!" (*Videō*, fourth key *vīsum*.) | ***Vīsus sum!*** "I've been seen!" *Vīsum* → *vīsus*. |

## 4. Fourth keys from every family

:: Iacta ālea est. || The die has been cast. (Caesar at the Rubicon, in Suetonius, *Julius* 32)

Liber lēctus est. Cāseus ā mē emptus est!

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 25: *legō, legere, lēgī, lēctum.* "The book has been read." | ***Liber lēctus est.*** (*Lēctus*: long ē, like *lēgī*.) |
| *Audiō, audīre, audīvī, audītum.* "Dad's song (*cantus*, m.) has been heard by everyone." | ***Cantus Patris ab omnibus audītus est.*** (*Cantus*, song, from *cantō*.) |
| *Capiō, capere, cēpī, captum.* "The box (*capsa*, f.) has been taken." | ***Capsa capta est.*** |
| *Emō, emere, ēmī, emptum.* Chapter 30's promise was *Crās cāseus ā mē emētur.* It's the next day. Report it done. | ***Cāseus ā mē emptus est!*** "A cheese has been bought by me!" Promise kept. |
| *Videō, vidēre, vīdī, vīsum.* Chapter 30: *videor* means "be seen" and so "seem." What can *Canis fessus vīsus est* mean? | "The dog **was seen** (to be) tired," so "the dog **seemed** tired." *Vīsus est* = "was seen" or "seemed." |
| *Dō, dare, dedī, datum* (short *a*) and *ferō, ferre, tulī, lātum.* "The cheese was given to Grandpa." "The cheese was carried." | ***Cāseus Avō datus est.*** · ***Cāseus lātus est.*** *Lātum* looks nothing like *ferō*: that is why the dictionary prints four keys. (Don't confuse *lātus* "carried" with the adjective *lātus* "wide"; the sentence decides.) |
| *Pōnō, pōnere, posuī, positum*: put, place (*posuī* and *positum* are another surprise). "The cheese has been put on the table." | ***Cāseus in mēnsā positus est.*** (*In* + ablative: where it now sits, Chapter 7.) |
| The motto. *Iaciō, iacere, iēcī, iactum*: throw. *Ālea, āleae* (f.): a die, a game of dice. Find the two pieces. | ***Iacta … est***, around *ālea*: "the die **has been thrown**." Feminine *iacta* agrees with *ālea*. Caesar said it in 49 BC, crossing the Rubicon into Italy with his army: no going back. (Suetonius writes *Iacta ālea est*; the famous version is *Ālea iacta est*. Same pieces, new order.) |

## 5. *Ā quō ēsus est?*

:: Laudātus est ab omnibus. || He has been praised by everyone. (teaching line; Chapter 30's *laudō*)

Cāseus ab Avō ēsus est? Ā quō? Ā cane!

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 30's Law of the Doer and the Tool still works. "The cheese has been eaten **by Grandpa**." | ***Cāseus ab Avō ēsus est.*** *Ā/ab* + ablative for the doer. |
| "The cheese has been eaten **with teeth**." (*Dentibus*, Chapter 15's *dēns*.) | ***Cāseus dentibus ēsus est.*** The tool: plain ablative, no preposition. |
| Ask it: "By whom was the cheese eaten?" | ***Ā quō cāseus ēsus est?*** |
| Flip a perfect active (Chapter 25): *Marcus Quīntum vīdit.* Three steps, as in Chapter 30, but the verb becomes two pieces. | ***Quīntus ā Marcō vīsus est.*** "Quīntus was seen by Marcus." *Quīntum* → *Quīntus*; *Marcus* → *ā Marcō*; *vīdit* → *vīsus est*. |
| *Līberī cāseum ēdērunt.* Flip it. Careful: which word does *ēsus* agree with? | ***Cāseus ā līberīs ēsus est.*** *Ēsus* and *est* agree with the **new** subject, *cāseus*: singular, though the eaters are many. |
| *Avia capsam aperuit.* (*Aperiō, aperīre, aperuī, apertum*: open, Chapter 30's Matthew 7:7.) Flip it. | ***Capsa ab Aviā aperta est.*** "The box was opened by Grandma." Feminine *aperta* for *capsa*. |

## 6. Genesis and the Gospel of John

:: Fīat lūx. Et facta est lūx. || Let there be light. And light was made. (Genesis 1:3, Vulgate)

Facta est lūx. Factum est Verbum. Facta sunt omnia.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Lūx, lūcis* (f.): light. *Fīat lūx*, "let there be light," is a chunk (*fīō*: Chapter 32). Then: *Et facta est lūx.* Why *facta*, not *factus*? | *Lūx* is **feminine**, so the fourth key of *faciō* goes feminine: ***facta est***, "light **was made**," "light came to be." |
| John 1:14: *Et Verbum carō factum est.* (*Carō, carnis*, f.: flesh. *Verbum*: Chapter 16's *In prīncipiō erat Verbum*.) Which noun does *factum* agree with? | ***Verbum***, neuter: "And the Word **was made** flesh" (became flesh). *Carō* is what it became, not the subject, so *factum* doesn't follow it. |
| John 1:3: *Omnia per ipsum facta sunt.* (*Omnia*: Chapter 20. *Ipsum*: Chapter 21.) Why *facta sunt*? | *Omnia*, "all things," is **neuter plural**, so ***facta*** (neuter plural *-a*) and ***sunt***: "All things **were made** through him." |
| Same verse, right after: *et sine ipsō factum est nihil quod factum est.* (*Nihil*: nothing, neuter.) | "and without him **was made** nothing that **was made**." *Factum est* twice, neuter singular for *nihil* and for *quod* (Chapter 28's relative). |
| Chapter 25's *Factum est* ("it is done") and Genesis's *facta est lūx*: same verb, same build. What changes? | Only the **ending of the adjective**: *factum* (neuter "it") vs *facta* (feminine "light"). The *est* is the same. |

## 7. "Is done": the finished state

:: Cōnsummātum est. || It is finished. (John 19:30, Vulgate)

Capsa clauditur. Capsa clausa est.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| John 19:30, Jesus's last words on the cross in John's Gospel: *Cōnsummātum est.* (*Cōnsummō, cōnsummāre*: finish, complete.) Word for word, then good English. | "It has been finished," so "**It is finished**." English often says "is …ed" for something done and now standing done. |
| *Claudō, claudere, clausī, clausum*: shut. Chapter 30's present passive: *Capsa clauditur.* And today's *Capsa clausa est.* The difference? | ***Clauditur***: "the box **is being shut**" (right now, the lid moving). ***Clausa est***: "the box **has been shut**," so "the box **is shut**" (done; it stays shut). |
| So *Capsa aperta est!* can be said two ways in English. | "The box **has been opened**!" or "The box **is open**!" Both describe a finished opening. |
| The test, when English wants "is": is it **happening** (Chapter 30, one word) or **done** (today, two words)? *Cāseus editur* · *Cāseus ēsus est.* | *Editur*: "is being eaten," still chewing. *Ēsus est*: "has been eaten," gone. |
| Chapter 8's motto, the end of the Latin Mass: *Īte, missa est.* (*Mittō, mittere, mīsī, missum*: send.) What does *missa est* look like now? | A fourth-key adjective, feminine, plus *est*: "**she has been sent**" (*missa* from *missum*). Scholars still argue what it meant to the first hearers: "Go, [the assembly] has been sent," or "Go, it is the dismissal," with *missa* as a late noun. Either way it grew from *mittō*. |
| Chapter 19's *Hic est Fīlius meus dīlēctus.* (*Dīligō, dīligere, dīlēxī, dīlēctum*: love, cherish.) What is *dīlēctus*? | A fourth-key adjective used plainly as an adjective: "**beloved**" ("loved, cherished"). No *est* belongs to it here; *est* goes with *hic*: "This **is** my **beloved** Son." |

## 8. *Amātus eram*: had been

:: Cāseus iam ēsus erat. || The cheese had already been eaten. (teaching line; Chapter 27)

Sērō! Cāseus iam ēsus erat.

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 16's *eram* is "I was"; Chapter 27's pluperfect is "the older snapshot." Swap *sum* for *eram*: *amātus eram.* Meaning? | "**I had been loved.**" Done before some other past moment. |
| Chant it. | ***Amātus eram, amātus erās, amātus erat, amātī erāmus, amātī erātis, amātī erant.*** |
| Chapter 27's ending: *Sērō! Canis cāseum iam ēderat.* Flip it to passive. | ***Sērō! Cāseus ā cane iam ēsus erat.*** "Too late! The cheese **had already been eaten** by the dog." |
| *Capsa clausa erat, sed nunc aperta est.* | "The box **had been shut**, but now it **has been opened**." Older snapshot, newer snapshot. |
| Chapter 27's *nōndum*: "The cheese had not yet been found." (*Inveniō, invenīre, invēnī, inventum*: find, Chapter 17.) | ***Cāseus nōndum inventus erat.*** |
| Active and passive pluperfect side by side: *Avus cāseum ēderat* · *Cāseus ab Avō ēsus erat.* Where does each keep its "had"? | Active: ***-erat*** glued on (*ēd-erat*, one word). Passive: ***erat*** as its own word after the fourth key. Same *eram* family, used two ways. |

## 9. *Amātus erō*: will have been

:: Sī cāseus ēsus erit, Avus clāmābit. || If the cheese gets eaten, Grandpa will shout. (teaching line; Chapter 27)

Hōrā tertiā cāseus ēsus erit.

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 27's future perfect is "will have." Swap in *erō*: *amātus erō.* | "**I will have been loved.**" Done by some future moment. |
| Chant it. Watch the "they." | ***Amātus erō, amātus eris, amātus erit, amātī erimus, amātī eritis, amātī erunt.*** "They will have been": ***erunt***, plain *sum* future (Chapter 16), not the active's *-erint*. |
| "By three o'clock the cheese will have been eaten." (Chapter 22's *hōrā tertiā*.) | ***Hōrā tertiā cāseus iam ēsus erit.*** |
| Chapter 27's *Sī cāseum ēderis, Avia clāmābit* used the future perfect where English says the present. Do the same in the passive: "If the cheese gets eaten, Grandpa will shout." | ***Sī cāseus ēsus erit, Avus clāmābit.*** English "gets eaten" (or "is eaten"); Latin "will have been eaten." |
| Careful: *amātus erō* vs Chapter 30's *amābor*. | ***Amābor***: "I **will be** loved" (one word, the plain future). ***Amātus erō***: "I **will have been** loved" (two words, done by then). |

!!! success "The Law of One Word and Two"
    Six tenses in the passive. The first three are **one word** (Chapter 30); the last three are **two words** (today): the fourth-key adjective + *sum*, *eram*, or *erō*.

    | | one word | | two words |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | present | *amātur* is (being) loved | perfect | ***amātus est*** was loved, has been loved |
    | imperfect | *amābātur* was being loved | pluperfect | ***amātus erat*** had been loved |
    | future | *amābitur* will be loved | future perfect | ***amātus erit*** will have been loved |

    The *sum* part shows where you stand to look back at the finished deed: **now** (*sum*), **then** (*eram*), **later** (*erō*). So *amātus sum* is never "I am loved," and *amātus erō* is never "I will be loved."

## 10. Reading tips and look-alikes

:: Verba lēcta sunt. || The words have been read. (teaching line)

Amātur an amātus est? Amāvit an amātus est?

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Amāvit* and *amātus est*: both are "finished." What is the difference? | ***Amāvit***: active, "he **loved**" (he did the loving). ***Amātus est***: passive, "he **was loved**" (someone loved him). Chapter 25's perfect stem vs today's fourth key. |
| Name voice and tense: *amātur* · *amātus est* · *amātus erat* · *amāverat* · *amātus erit* · *amābitur*. | *Amātur*: passive present. *Amātus est*: passive perfect. *Amātus erat*: passive pluperfect. *Amāverat*: **active** pluperfect, "he had loved." *Amātus erit*: passive future perfect. *Amābitur*: passive future. |
| In the dictionary, the fourth key is always written *amātum*, *ēsum*. In a sentence, what happens to it? | It **agrees**: *amātus, amāta, amātum, amātī, amātae, amāta*, to match its subject. *Cāseus ēsum est* is wrong; *Cāseus ēsus est* is right. |
| A reader's note: you will sometimes meet *amātus fuit* or *amātus fuerat*, with Chapter 25's *fuit* in place of *est*. How to read them? | Almost like ***amātus est*** and ***amātus erat***. Read them that way for now. |
| Sometimes the *est* is left out, as in English headlines: *Cāseus ēsus!* | "Cheese eaten!" Supply the *est* in your head. (Participles on their own, without *sum*, get their own chapter: Chapter 37.) |
| A last look-alike: Chapter 2's *nōnne* question with today's forms: *Nōnne cāseus ēsus est?* | "**The cheese has been eaten, hasn't it?**" (expects "yes"). Answer: ***Ita, ēsus est.*** |

## 11. Fix it

:: Errāta corrēcta sunt! || The mistakes have been corrected! (teaching line; *errātum*, mistake; *corrigō*, correct, fourth key *corrēctum*)

Ēsus an ēsum? Est an erit?

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "The cheese has been eaten": *Cāseus ēsum est.* | ***Cāseus ēsus est.*** The fourth key agrees with *cāseus*. |
| "The box has been opened": *Capsa apertus est.* | ***Capsa aperta est.*** *Capsa* is feminine. |
| "The apples have been eaten": *Māla ēsa est.* | ***Māla ēsa sunt.*** Plural subject, plural *sum*. |
| "I am loved by Grandma" (Cornēlia speaking): *Ab Aviā amāta sum.* | ***Ab Aviā amor.*** *Amāta sum* is "I **was** loved." "I am loved" is the one-word present. |
| "The cheese had been eaten by the dog": *Cāseus ā cane ēsus est.* | ***Cāseus ā cane ēsus erat.*** "Had been" needs *erat*. |
| "The cheese was eaten by Grandpa": *Cāseus Avō ēsus est.* | ***Cāseus ab Avō ēsus est.*** A person doing it needs *ā/ab*. |

## 12. Lectiō: *Cāseus ēsus est!*

:: Factum est. Fuit cāseus! || It's done. The cheese is history! (teaching line; Chapter 28's *Fuit cāseus*)

Capsa clausa est. Capsa aperta est! Ā quō?

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Grandma's two lines: *cāseus in capsā pōnitur* · *cāseus in capsā positus est.* What changed? | One word → two. *Pōnitur*: "**is being put**" (happening, Chapter 30). *Positus est*: "**has been put**" (done). Same for *clauditur* → *clausa est*. |
| *Capsa aperta est!* · *Ā quō capsa aperta est?* Why *aperta*? | *Capsa* is feminine. "The box **has been opened**! By whom **was** the box opened?" |
| Dad: *Canis in hortum ductus erat.* Tense? | **Pluperfect** passive: "had been taken out," before the night's events. (*Dūcō*, fourth key *ductum*: lead, take a person or animal.) |
| *In capsā aliquid scrīptum est.* Why *scrīptum*? | *Aliquid* ("something," Chapter 29) is neuter, so ***scrīptum*** (*scrībō*, fourth key *scrīptum*): "something **has been written**." |
| Grandpa: *Ōlim amātus sum…* and John's answer. Why does John correct him? | *Amātus sum* = "I **was** loved" (finished, in the past), as if no one loves him now. John: *Amāris!* "You **are** loved!" (Chapter 30's one-word present.) |
| *Cāseus ā Cāseō ēsus est!* Agent or means, and why *ā*? | The agent: ***ā Cāseō***, the dog named Cheese, a living doer, so *ā/ab* (Chapter 30). The cheese is the subject, so *ēsus* is masculine singular. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Dictum, factum! || Said and done! (teaching line)

1. Chant *amātus sum…*, *amātus eram…*, *amātus erō…*; then Cornēlia's *amāta sum…*.
2. Say the two Laws in your own words: Two Pieces (the adjective agrees, *sum* tells the time) and One Word and Two (*amātur / amātus est*).
3. Say these aloud, then flip each to the finished passive with *ā/ab*: *Avus cāseum ēdit · Avia capsam aperuit · Marcus librum lēgit · Līberī Avum amāvērunt.*
4. Say "the box is being shut / has been shut / had been shut / will have been shut."
5. Say the trap aloud: *amor* (I am loved) vs *amātus sum* (I was loved); *amābor* vs *amātus erō*.
6. The real lines: *Factum est.* · *Gallia est omnis dīvīsa in partēs trēs.* · *Iacta ālea est.* · *Fīat lūx. Et facta est lūx.* · *Verbum carō factum est.* · *Cōnsummātum est.*
7. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part (the dog too). Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
