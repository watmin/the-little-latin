# Caput LI: Ut Discam

*Liber V, Modus Subiūnctīvus · Chapter 51*

*In which the verb learns to aim. Way back in Chapter 11 the lawyers' motto* Dō ut dēs *was translated "I give so that you may give," and Chapter 4 opened with* Ēsse oportet ut vīvās, nōn vīvere ut edās. *Chapter 49 found the subjunctive hiding in both (*dēs, vīvās, edās*) and promised: "Why* ut *wants this mood: Chapter 51." Here it is. Today: the little word* ut, *"so that," and its "not" twin* nē, *which point a sentence at a goal; and a* quī *that does the same job. Along the way: a Roman lawyer's four kinds of swap, a slogan about eating, Cicero on why we obey laws, Ovid's love advice, a lamp on a lampstand, and a thief in John's Gospel. Speaking goal: say why you do things.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Dō ut dēs · Edō ut vīvam · Ad forum eō ut cāseum emam · … nē canis eum edat · … nē quis tangat · Marcum mittō quī cāseum emat*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** purpose clause (the "so that / in order to" clause) · relative purpose clause (a *quī*-clause that tells what someone is sent *for*)
    - **Forms:** no new forms: Chapter 49's present subjunctive does all the work
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Goal · the Law of *Nē* · the Law of the Sending *Quī*
    - **Words:** *ut* (two jobs), *nē* ("so that not"), *idcircō, fūror, gustō, ōsculum, amābilis*
    - **Reading:** *Dō ut dēs* (day nineteen: a new cheese from the forum, a deal with Grandma, and a deal with the dog)
    - **Speaking:** say why you do five things; say what you do so something *won't* happen; send someone to do a job

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep Chapter 49's Law of the Swapped Vowel close (*amem, habeam, dūcam, capiam, audiam, sim*) and Chapter 50's *nē* for wishes. Today, too, adds no new endings: one new job, the goal.

## 1. *Dō ut dēs*, at last

:: Dō ut dēs. || I give so that you may give. (Roman law; Chapter 11)

Dō ut dēs.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 11's motto: *Dō ut dēs.* Chapter 49 spotted the new mood in it. Which word? | ***Dēs***, "you may give": the present subjunctive of *dō* (*dem, dēs, det…*). The fact would be *dās*. |
| *Dō* is a plain fact: "I give." What does *ut dēs* add? | **Why** I give: "**so that you may give**," "**in order that** you give (back)." It names my **goal**. |
| Why the subjunctive and not the fact *dās*? | Because when I give, you **haven't given yet**. Your giving is only aimed at, hoped for. Not a fact: the "maybe" mood. |
| A Roman lawyer, Paulus (in Justinian's *Digest* 19.5.5), sorts all swaps into four kinds: *aut enim dō tibi ut dēs, aut dō ut faciās, aut faciō ut dēs, aut faciō ut faciās.* (*Aut … aut*: either … or; *enim*: for.) Translate. | "For either I give to you **so that you may give**, or I give **so that you may do** (something), or I do **so that you may give**, or I do **so that you may do**." |
| Which of the four is "I'll fix your roof if you give me cheese"? | ***Faciō ut dēs***: I do (a job) so that you may give (cheese). |

## 2. A goal gets its own clause

:: Ut discam. || So that I may learn. (teaching line; the chapter's title)

Ut + subiūnctīvus.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 28 called a little sentence with its own verb a **clause**. How many clauses in *Dō ut dēs*? | **Two**: *dō* (the main clause) and *ut dēs* (a clause that hangs on it). |
| Grammar books call *ut dēs* a **purpose clause** (*prōpositum*, aim). Plain words? | **The "so that / in order to" clause**: it tells **why**, for what goal, the main verb happens. Old-school books also say "**final clause**," from *fīnis*, "end, goal." |
| What starts it, and what mood is its verb? | It starts with ***ut*** ("so that"; Section 7 adds its "not" twin) and its verb is **subjunctive**, always: the goal hasn't happened yet. |
| *Discō, discere, didicī*: learn (Chapter 48's *Quid didicistī?*). The title, *Ut discam.* Which form is *discam*? | Present subjunctive, "I" form: ***discam***, "**so that I may learn**." (The same spelling is also the future, "I will learn"; after *ut* it's the subjunctive.) |
| *Latīnē legō ut discam.* | "I read Latin **so that I may learn**," "I read Latin **to learn**." |

## 3. "Eat to live"

:: Ēsse oportet ut vīvās, nōn vīvere ut edās. || One ought to eat so that one may live, not to live so that one may eat. (*Rhetoric for Herennius* 4.39; Chapter 4)

Ēsse ut vīvās.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 4's very first motto, *Ēsse oportet ut vīvās, nōn vīvere ut edās.* Chapter 44 cracked *oportet* ("one ought") and *ēsse* ("to eat"). Find the two purpose clauses. | ***Ut vīvās***, "so that you may live," and ***ut edās***, "so that you may eat." |
| Translate, word for word, then like English. | "One ought to eat **so that you may live**, not to live **so that you may eat**." English: "**Eat to live; don't live to eat.**" |
| Whose "you" is *vīvās*? John's? | **Anyone's**: the general "you" of Chapter 50's *crēdās*. "One." |
| Quintilian (*The Orator's Education* 9.3.85) quotes the same idea turned around, as "I": *nōn ut edam vīvō, sed ut vīvam edō.* | "I don't live **to eat**, but I eat **to live**." *Edam* and *vīvam*: present subjunctives after *ut*. |
| English says "to eat," "to live" with a plain "to." Does Latin? | **No.** Latin prose never uses a plain infinitive for a goal (a few poets bend this). "**Eat to live**" is *Ede ut vīvās*: ***ut* + subjunctive**. |

## 4. Why do you do it?

:: Edō ut vīvam. || I eat to live. (teaching line)

Cūr? Ut…

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "I'm going to the forum to buy cheese." (*Emō, emere*: buy, Chapter 6.) | ***Ad forum eō ut cāseum emam.*** |
| "Marcus is going to the forum to buy cheese." | ***Marcus ad forum it ut cāseum emat.*** The *ut* verb follows its own subject: Marcus → *emat*. |
| "We sing so that Grandpa may sleep." (Two different people!) | ***Cantāmus ut Avus dormiat.*** The main verb and the *ut* verb can have **different** subjects, as in *Dō ut dēs*. |
| "Stay home, children, so that y'all may learn." | ***Manēte domī, līberī, ut discātis.*** (*Discātis*: the y'all form.) |
| *Cūr cāseum edis?* Answer with a goal: "So that I may be strong." | ***Ut fortis sim.*** An *ut*-clause can stand alone as an answer, like English "To be strong." |

## 5. Real goals

:: Ego vēnī ut vītam habeant. || I have come so that they may have life. (John 10:10)

Ut vītam habeant.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| John 10:10. Jesus contrasts himself with a thief: *Fūr nōn venit nisi ut fūrētur et mactet et perdat.* (*Fūr*: thief, Chapter 13; *fūror, fūrārī*: steal, a deponent; *nisi*: except; *mactō*: slaughter; *perdō*: destroy.) | "The thief **comes only to steal** and slaughter and destroy." Word for word: "does not come except **so that he may steal**…" |
| Which form is *fūrētur*? | Present subjunctive of the deponent ***fūror*** (first family, so *a* → *e*: *fūrātur* → ***fūrētur***), like Chapter 49's *laetēmur*. |
| The verse goes on: *ego vēnī ut vītam habeant et abundantius habeant.* (*Vēnī*: "I have come," Chapter 25; *abundantius*: more abundantly.) | "I have come **so that they may have life**, and have it more abundantly." |
| Cicero, *For Cluentius* 146, on why Romans obey the law: *lēgum dēnique idcircō omnēs servī sumus ut līberī esse possīmus.* (*Lēx, lēgis*: law; *dēnique*: in short; *idcircō*: for this reason; *servus*: slave; *līber*: free, Chapter 10.) | "In short, we are all slaves of the laws **for this reason: so that we may be able to be free**." |
| What job does *idcircō* do before *ut*? | It **points ahead** to the goal: "for this reason … so that." Like English "**that's why** … : so that." Handy, but optional. |

## 6. A purpose first

:: Ut amēris, amābilis estō. || To be loved, be lovable. (Ovid, *The Art of Love* 2.107)

Ut amēris…

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Ovid, *The Art of Love* 2.107: *ut amēris, amābilis estō.* (*Amābilis*: lovable.) Find the purpose clause. | ***Ut amēris***, "**so that you may be loved**." Here it comes **first**. |
| What form is *amēris*? | Present subjunctive **passive**, "you": *amāris* (you are loved, Chapter 30) → ***amēris***. Chapter 49's swapped vowel, passive ending. |
| And *estō*? | Chapter 47's "shall" command of *sum*: "**be**," "you shall be." "**To be loved, be lovable.**" |
| Matthew 5:15: *Neque accendunt lucernam et pōnunt eam sub modiō, sed super candēlābrum, ut lūceat omnibus quī in domō sunt.* (*Accendō*: light; *lucerna*: lamp; *modius*: a grain bucket; *candēlābrum*: lampstand; *lūceō*: shine, Chapter 31's *lūx*.) | "Nor do they light a lamp and put it under a bucket, but on a lampstand, **so that it may shine** for all who are in the house." |

!!! success "The Law of the Goal"
    A **purpose clause** (the "so that / in order to" clause) tells **why**. It starts with ***ut*** and its verb is **subjunctive**: the goal isn't a fact yet.

    | Latin | English |
    |---|---|
    | *Dō ut dēs.* | I give so that you may give. |
    | *Edō ut vīvam.* | I eat to live. |
    | *Ad forum eō ut cāseum emam.* | I'm going to the forum to buy cheese. |
    | *Ut amēris, amābilis estō.* | To be loved, be lovable. |

    English "to …" for a goal is never a Latin infinitive in prose. Today every main verb is "now," "later," a command, or "have done" (John's *vēnī*, "I have come"); a goal in a past story ("I gave so that you *would* give") needs a new form: Chapter 52.

## 7. "So that … not": *nē*

:: Cāseum claudō nē canis eum edat. || I shut the cheese away so the dog won't eat it. (teaching line)

Ut… nē!

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapters 49 and 50: *nē* is the "not" of "let's," "let him," and wishes. A goal is not a fact either. Guess: "so that the dog may **not** eat it." | ***nē canis eum edat.*** The "not" goal is ***nē***, alone: no *ut* in front. |
| Full sentence: *Cāseum claudō nē canis eum edat.* (*Claudō*: shut, Chapter 31.) | "I shut the cheese away **so that the dog won't eat it**," "**so the dog doesn't** eat it." |
| Older English had a one-word "so that … not": **lest**. Translate with it. | "I shut the cheese away **lest** the dog eat it." Old-fashioned, but it fits *nē* exactly. |
| "We speak quietly so that Grandpa doesn't wake up." (*Submissē*: quietly; *expergīscor*: wake up, a deponent.) | ***Submissē loquimur nē Avus expergīscātur.*** |
| "Go slowly, so that you don't fall!" (*Lentē*: slowly, Chapter 34; *cadō*.) | ***Ī lentē, nē cadās!*** |

## 8. *Nē quis*: so that nobody

:: Nē quis cāseum tangat! || So that nobody touches the cheese! (teaching line)

Nē quis… nē quid…

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 29: after *sī* the *ali-* drops off *aliquis* (*sī quis*, "if anyone"). The same happens after ***nē***. "So that **anyone** may **not**…" = "so that **nobody**…" | ***nē quis***: "so that **no one**…" And ***nē quid***: "so that **nothing**…" |
| *Cāseum claudō nē quis eum tangat.* | "I shut the cheese away **so that no one touches it**." |
| Why not *ut nēmō*? | Romans prefer ***nē quis*** ("lest anyone") for a goal. (*Ut nēmō* has a different job in Chapter 52.) |
| *Avus tacet nē quid dīcat.* | "Grandpa keeps quiet **so as not to say anything**," "so that he says nothing." |

## 9. The Bible's *ut nōn*

:: Nōlīte iūdicāre, ut nōn iūdicēminī. || Judge not, that ye be not judged. (Matthew 7:1)

Ut nōn? Nē!

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Matthew 7:1: *Nōlīte iūdicāre, ut nōn iūdicēminī.* (*Iūdicō*: judge, Chapter 39; *iūdicēminī*: y'all may be judged, passive.) Translate. | "**Don't** (y'all) judge, **so that y'all may not be judged**." King James: "Judge not, that ye be not judged." |
| Is *ut nōn* the classical way to say a "not" goal? | **No.** Cicero would write ***nē iūdicēminī***. The Latin Bible often uses *ut nōn* for this. Read it, but when you speak, say ***nē***. |
| Matthew 26:41, in the garden: *Vigilāte et ōrāte, ut nōn intrētis in tentātiōnem.* (*Vigilō*: stay awake; *tentātiō*: temptation.) Make it classical. | ***Vigilāte et ōrāte, nē in tentātiōnem intrētis.*** "Stay awake and pray, so that y'all don't enter into temptation." |

!!! success "The Law of *Nē*"
    A goal that should **not** happen takes ***nē*** + subjunctive: "so that … not," "lest."

    | Latin | English |
    |---|---|
    | *Cāseum claudō nē canis eum edat.* | I shut the cheese away so the dog won't eat it. |
    | *Ī lentē, nē cadās!* | Go slowly, so you don't fall! |
    | *nē quis · nē quid* | so that no one · so that nothing |

    Classical prose never writes *ut nōn* for a "not" goal (the Bible sometimes does). *Ut nōn* has a different job: Chapter 52.

## 10. Sending someone *quī*…

:: Marcum mittō quī cāseum emat. || I'm sending Marcus to buy cheese. (teaching line)

Mittō… quī…

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 28's relative pronoun *quī, quae, quod* points back to a noun. Normally its verb is a fact: *Marcus, quī cāseum emit*, "Marcus, **who buys** cheese." Now: *Marcum mittō quī cāseum emat.* What's different? | ***Emat*** is subjunctive. It isn't who Marcus *is*; it's what he's sent **for**: "I'm sending Marcus **to buy** cheese," word for word "**who is to buy**." |
| Grammar books call this a **relative purpose clause**. Plain words? | **A *quī*-clause that tells a goal**: "who is to …," "to …." It works like *ut is cāseum emat*, "so that he may buy cheese": *quī* stands in for *ut is*. |
| Where does it usually come? | After verbs of **sending** and **choosing**: *mittō* (send, Chapter 31), *ēligō* (choose). |
| *Avia Cornēliam mittit quae aquam ferat.* | "Grandma sends Cornelia **to fetch water**." *Quae* is feminine for Cornelia. |
| "The king sends messengers to say this." (*Nūntius*: messenger.) | ***Rēx nūntiōs mittit quī hoc dīcant.*** Plural antecedent → *quī … dīcant*. |

## 11. *Quī* or *ut*?

:: Quīntum mittō quī tēcum eat. || I'm sending Quintus to go with you. (teaching line)

Quī… quae… quī…

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Marcum mittō quī cāseum emat* and *Marcum mittō ut cāseum emat.* Same meaning? | **Yes**, both "I'm sending Marcus to buy cheese." *Quī* is common when the goal is about **the person sent**. |
| Fact or goal? *Puer quī cantat meus est.* | **Fact** (indicative *cantat*): "The boy **who is singing** is mine." No sending, no goal. |
| Fact or goal? *Puerum mittō quī cantet.* | **Goal** (subjunctive *cantet*): "I'm sending a boy **to sing**." |
| "Grandma chooses a boy to guard the cheese." (*Ēligō*: choose; *servō*: guard, Chapter 50.) | ***Avia puerum ēligit quī cāseum servet.*** |

!!! success "The Law of the Sending *Quī*"
    After **sending** or **choosing**, *quī, quae, quod* + **subjunctive** tells what the person is for.

    | Latin | English |
    |---|---|
    | *Marcum mittō quī cāseum emat.* | I'm sending Marcus to buy cheese. |
    | *Rēx nūntiōs mittit quī hoc dīcant.* | The king sends messengers to say this. |
    | *Puer quī cantat* (fact) · *Puerum mittō quī cantet* (goal) | the boy who is singing · I send a boy to sing |

## 12. Two ways to go somewhere for a reason

:: Īte in hortum lūsūrī! || Go into the garden to play! (teaching line; Chapter 39)

Lūsūrī… ut lūdant.

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 39's Grandma: *Īte in hortum lūsūrī!* "Go into the garden **to play**." Say it with *ut*. | ***Īte in hortum ut lūdātis!*** |
| Which works after **any** verb, *ut* or the future participle? | ***Ut.*** The *-ūrus* "to" lives mostly after verbs of **going and coming**. *Ut* fits every verb: *Dō ut dēs* has no going in it. |
| "He comes to eat cheese." Fix: *Venit cāseum edere.* | ***Venit ut cāseum edat.*** (Or Chapter 39: *Venit cāseum ēsūrus.*) No plain infinitive for a goal. |
| "I'm saving money in order to buy a horse." (*Pecūniam servō*; *equus*.) | ***Pecūniam servō ut equum emam.*** |

## 13. Two jobs for *ut*

:: Ut dīxī… || As I said… (teaching line)

Ut + indicātīvus?

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Ut dīxī, cāseus parvus est.* Is *dīxī* subjunctive? | **No**: *dīxī* is a plain fact, "I said" (Chapter 25). With a **fact** verb, *ut* means "**as**": "**As I said**, the cheese is small." |
| So how do you know which *ut* you have? | **Look at its verb.** *Ut* + subjunctive: "**so that**." *Ut* + indicative: "**as**." |
| *Ut vidēs, canis dormit.* | "**As you see**, the dog is asleep." Indicative *vidēs*. |
| *Lucernam accendō ut videās.* | "I light the lamp **so that you can see**." Subjunctive *videās*. |

## 14. Build them

:: Discimus ut loquāmur. || We learn so that we may speak. (teaching line)

Cūr? Ut… Nē…

{#g-14}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "John studies Latin so that he may read Vergil." (*Studeō* + dative, Chapter 44; *Vergilius*.) | ***Iōannēs Latīnae studet ut Vergilium legat.*** |
| "We hide the cheese so that Grandpa won't find it." (*Abdō*: hide; *inveniō*: find.) | ***Cāseum abdimus nē Avus eum inveniat.*** |
| "Grandma sends Quintus to call Dad." (*Vocō*.) | ***Avia Quīntum mittit quī Patrem vocet.*** |

| "We keep quiet so as not to wake the baby." (*Īnfāns*: baby; *excitō*: wake.) | ***Tacēmus nē īnfantem excitēmus.*** |

## 15. Traps and fix-its

:: Errāre licet; corrigere melius. || Making a mistake is allowed; correcting it is better. (teaching line; Chapter 50)

Ut nōn? Nē!

{#g-15}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "I eat to live": *Edō vīvere.* | ***Edō ut vīvam.*** A goal is *ut* + subjunctive, not an infinitive. |
| "I give so that you may give": *Dō ut dās.* | ***Dō ut dēs.*** *Dās* is a fact; a goal needs the subjunctive. |
| "I hide the cheese so the dog won't eat it": *Cāseum abdō ut canis eum nōn edat.* | ***Cāseum abdō nē canis eum edat.*** A "not" goal is *nē*. |
| "I'm sending the boys to buy bread": *Puerōs mittō quī pānem emunt.* | ***Puerōs mittō quī pānem emant.*** *Emunt* would be "who buy" (a fact). |
| "So that nobody sees": *ut nēmō videat.* | ***nē quis videat.*** |

## 16. Lectiō: *Dō ut dēs*

:: Edō ut vīvam! || I eat to live! (teaching line)

Cūr? Ut…

Diē ūndēvīcēsimā: the day after Chapter 50. The family cheese survived yesterday, but not much of it is left. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. Hunt every *ut*, *nē*, and sending *quī*, and ask: what's the goal? New in passing: *ūndēvīcēsimus*, nineteenth; *interrogō*, quiz, question (Chapter 48); *armārium*, cupboard; *mercātor*, merchant (Chapter 13); *abdūcō*, lead away; *lambō*, lick. Old friends: *cārus* (Chapter 44); *prīmum*, first; *tandem*, at last.

Avia | Canī pānem dō, nē cāseum petat. || Grandma | I give the dog bread, so he won't go after the cheese.
Iōannēs | Canis pānem edit… et Avum spectat. Avus canī partem parvam cāseī dat. Canis Avum lambit. || John | The dog eats the bread… and watches Grandpa. Grandpa gives the dog a small piece of cheese. The dog licks Grandpa.
Avus | Ecce! Ego cāseum dō, canis ōsculum dat. Dō ut dēs! || Grandpa | Look! I give cheese, the dog gives a kiss. I give so that you may give!
Omnēs | Dō ut dēs! || Everyone | I give so that you may give!
Avia | Utinam canis quoque Latīnē discat… || Grandma | If only the dog would learn Latin too…

{#g-16}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Why does Marcus go to the forum? Why does Cornelia stay home? Answer in Latin. | ***Ut novum cāseum emat. · Ut discat.*** |
| Find the sending *quī*. | *Quīntum, **quī tēcum eat***: "Quintus, to go with you." Subjunctive *eat* (Chapter 49's *eam, eās, eat*). |
| Find four "so that … not" lines. | *nē tōtum cāseum in viā edās · nē quis eum tangat · nē cāseum meum edat · nē cāseum petat.* |
| *Nunc omnēs edāmus, ut fortēs sīmus!* Two subjunctives, two jobs? | ***Edāmus***: "**let's** eat" (hortatory, Chapter 49). ***Sīmus***: "**so that** we may be" (purpose). |
| Grandpa's two deals: what does he give, and what does he get? | To Grandma, a kiss for cheese: he gets **bread**. To the dog, cheese: he gets **a kiss**. *Dō ut dēs!* |

## 17. Sound check

:: Clārē loquāmur! || Let's speak clearly! (teaching line; Chapter 49)

Audī et dīc.

{#g-17}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *ut · nē · Dō ut dēs*? | ut (short *u*, as in "put") · NAY · DOH ut DAYS. Don't stretch *ut* into "oot." |
| *discam · discātis · fūrētur*? | DIS-kam · dis-KAH-tis · foo-RAY-tur. The emphasis (stress) jumps to a long next-to-last syllable. |
| *idcircō · possīmus · amēris · amābilis*? | id-KIR-koh · pos-SEE-mus · a-MAY-ris · a-MAH-bi-lis. |
| *iūdicēminī · ūndēvīcēsimā*? | yoo-di-KAY-mi-nee · oon-day-wee-KAY-si-mah. Short *i*'s on both sides of the long *ē*. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Loquāmur Latīnē! || Let's speak Latin! (teaching line)

1. In your own words: what is a purpose clause, and why is its verb subjunctive?
2. Say why you do five things, with *ut* (*Edō ut vīvam. Latīnē legō ut discam…*).
3. Say three things you do so that something *won't* happen, with *nē*, one of them with *nē quis*.
4. Send three people to do three jobs, with *quī / quae* + subjunctive.
5. Say one sentence with *ut* meaning "as" and one with *ut* meaning "so that."
6. The three Laws in your own words: the Goal, *Nē*, the Sending *Quī*.
7. The real lines: *Dō ut dēs · aut dō ut faciās, aut faciō ut dēs · Ēsse oportet ut vīvās, nōn vīvere ut edās · nōn ut edam vīvō, sed ut vīvam edō · Fūr nōn venit nisi ut fūrētur · ego vēnī ut vītam habeant · lēgum … omnēs servī sumus ut līberī esse possīmus · ut amēris, amābilis estō · ut lūceat omnibus quī in domō sunt · Nōlīte iūdicāre, ut nōn iūdicēminī.* Then read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part, and rebuild it from the English.

## Vocābula: Words of this chapter

|---|---|

| *nē* (+ subjunctive) | so that … not, lest |
| *idcircō* | for this reason, that's why |
| *fūror, fūrārī, fūrātus sum* | steal |
