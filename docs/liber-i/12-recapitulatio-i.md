# Caput XII: Recapitulātiō I

*Liber I, Prīma Verba · Chapter 12*

*In which nothing is new and everything comes back. The six cases line up with their adjectives, every verb family chants its six persons, and every little question word gets asked again. Then the whole family walks to Rome: the forum, the teacher, a shop, and one very big cheese. At the end you stand up and introduce yourself, in Latin, for two whole minutes. And Grandpa, at long last, gets his cheese.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Salvēte! Nōmen mihi est Iōannēs.* · *In vīllā habitō.* · *Cāseum amō.* · *Rōmam īmus!* · *Hodiē mihi, crās tibi!*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** nothing new. This is a **recapitulātiō**, a "re-heading": going back over the main points (English "recap")
    - **Forms:** all six cases of the 1st and 2nd declensions *with adjectives*; the present of all four verb families, plus *sum, eō, dō*; every command; every place form
    - **Laws:** all of Book I's, side by side: the Switch, the Call, the Owner, the Twins, the Receiver, the Look-alikes
    - **Words:** four new ones, for the trip: shopkeeper, tired, at last, in Latin
    - **Reading:** *Omnēs viae Rōmam dūcunt* (a day trip to Rome, in three scenes)
    - **Speaking:** a two-minute self-introduction, all yours

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. This chapter goes fast on purpose: if a group feels shaky, its answers name the chapter to go back to. The last two sections are the real exam: the story and your own speech.

---

## 1. *Puer parvus, puerō parvō*

:: Nōn multa, sed multum. || Not many things, but much. (after Pliny the Younger: read deeply, not widely)

Puer parvus. Puerī parvī. Puerō parvō. Puerum parvum. Cum puerō parvō. Puer parve!

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Name the six cases in order, each with its job. | Nominative (naming, subject) · genitive (of, whose) · dative (to, for) · accusative (object; where to) · ablative (where, from, with) · vocative (calling). Six jobs, one noun. |
| Decline *puer parvus*, "the little boy," singular, nominative to ablative. The adjective follows its noun. | *puer parvus, puerī parvī, puerō parvō, puerum parvum, puerō parvō.* |
| Now call him. *Puer* stays *puer* (Chapter 9). Does *parvus*? | *Puer parve!* *Parvus* is an *-us* word, so it takes the Law of the Call: *-e*. Same case, different letters. |
| The plural, all six. | *puerī parvī, puerōrum parvōrum, puerīs parvīs, puerōs parvōs, puerīs parvīs, puerī parvī.* |
| *Puella bona*, singular, all six. | *puella bona, puellae bonae, puellae bonae, puellam bonam, puellā bonā, puella bona.* |
| "I give a cookie to my friend." Then "to my friends." | *Crustulum amīcō meō dō.* · *Crustulum amīcīs meīs dō.* *Meus* bends just like *bonus*. |
| "We give money to the good farmer." Careful! | *Pecūniam agricolae bonō damus.* *Agricola* bends like *puella* (*-ae*), but he's a man, so *bonus* goes masculine (*-ō*). Agreement means same case, number, and gender, not same letters. |
| "In our book." "Of our books." | *In librō nostrō.* · *Librōrum nostrōrum.* *Noster* drops its e, just like *liber*. |
| *Fīliīs* means "to the sons" or "to the daughters." How could a Roman tell them apart? | When it mattered (in legal and formal phrases), an old form: *fīliābus*, "to (or from) the daughters." So *fīliīs et fīliābus*: "to the sons and the daughters." *Deābus*, "to the goddesses," is the other one you'll meet. Just a curiosity: everywhere else, *-īs*. |

## 2. *Quis? Quid? Cuius?*

:: Quis es? Unde venīs? Quō īs? || Who are you? Where are you from? Where are you going? (a traveler's three questions)

Quis es? Quid edis? Ubi habitās? Unde venīs? Quō īs? Cuius est?

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Quis* or *quid*: which is for people? | *Quis?*, "who?" (Chapter 2). *Quid?* is "what?" *Quis est? Marcus est.* · *Quid est? Cāseus est.* |
| And *quem*? (Chapter 4) | "Whom?": *quis* as the object. *Quem vidēs? Marcum videō.* |
| Ask and answer, about the forum: where are you, where are you coming from, where are you going? | *Ubi es? In forō sum.* · *Unde venīs? Ā forō veniō.* · *Quō īs? In forum eō.* Where and from where: ablative. Where to: accusative. |
| *Cuius est stilus?* (It's the teacher's.) | *Magistrī est.* (Chapter 10) |
| "Is the cheese good?" Ask it three ways: open, expecting yes, expecting no. | *Estne cāseus bonus?* · *Nōnne cāseus bonus est?* · *Num cāseus bonus est?* Answers: *Ita!* or *Minimē!* (Chapter 2) |
| The motto: three questions. Answer them as Marcus on the road. | *Marcus sum. Ē vīllā veniō. Rōmam eō!* |

## 3. *Amō, habeō, legō, audiō, capiō*

:: Cōgitō, ergō sum. || I think, therefore I am. (Descartes)

Amō. Habeō. Legō. Audiō. Capiō. Sum. Eō. Dō.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chant *amō* in all six persons, then "to love." | *amō, amās, amat, amāmus, amātis, amant · amāre.* |
| *Habeō*. | *habeō, habēs, habet, habēmus, habētis, habent · habēre.* |
| *Legō*. | *legō, legis, legit, legimus, legitis, legunt · legere* (LE-ge-re: the middle e is short, so the emphasis falls on the first syllable). |
| *Audiō*. | *audiō, audīs, audit, audīmus, audītis, audiunt · audīre.* |
| *Capiō*. | *capiō, capis, capit, capimus, capitis, capiunt · capere.* Like *legō*, but it keeps its i in *capiō* and *capiunt*. |
| Which forms give each family away? | "We" and "they": *amāmus, amant* (ā) · *habēmus, habent* (ē) · *legimus, legunt* (short i, then u) · *audīmus, audiunt* (ī, then iu) · *capimus, capiunt* (short i, then iu). And every family shares the person endings *-ō, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt* (only *sum* says *-m* for "I"). |
| The odd ones: *sum*, *eō*, *dō*. | *sum, es, est, sumus, estis, sunt* · *eō, īs, it, īmus, ītis, eunt* · *dō, dās, dat, damus, datis, dant* (short a except *dās*). |
| "We read books. Y'all hear Marcus. They grab the cheese." | *Librōs legimus. Marcum audītis. Cāseum capiunt.* |
| The motto, *Cōgitō, ergō sum.* | *Cōgitō*, "I think" (Chapter 5); *ergō*, "therefore"; *sum*, "I am." The philosopher René Descartes wrote it in Latin in 1644: the one thing he couldn't doubt was that he was doing the doubting. |

## 4. *Audī! Venī! Dā!*

:: Audī, vidē, tacē. || Listen, look, keep quiet. (medieval proverb: the rest is *sī vīs vīvere in pāce*, "if you want to live in peace")

Venī! Sedē! Lege! Audī! Dā mihi! Nōlī timēre!

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Tell one person: "Love!", "Sit!", "Read!", "Listen!", "Grab!" Then tell y'all. | *Amā! Sedē! Lege! Audī! Cape!* Take the *-re* off the infinitive (Chapter 6). Y'all: *Amāte! Sedēte! Legite! Audīte! Capite!*, with Chapter 1's *-te*. |
| The four short ones, and their plurals. | *Dīc! Dūc! Fac! Fer!* · *dīcite, dūcite, facite, ferte.* |
| "Give!" and "Go!", one and many. | *Dā! Date!* (short a) · *Ī! Īte!* |
| "Don't eat the cheese!" to Marcus, then to both boys. | *Nōlī cāseum edere!* · *Nōlīte cāseum edere!* *Nōlī* + "to do it" (Chapter 6). |
| "Marcus, bring me the bread, please!" | *Marce, fer mihi pānem, quaesō!* A calling, a command, a receiver, an object, and a please: five chapters in five words. |
| The motto, *Audī, vidē, tacē.* | Three commands to one person: "hear, see, be quiet" (*audiō, videō, taceō*). It's the start of a medieval rhyme: *Audī, vidē, tacē, sī vīs vīvere in pāce.* |

## 5. *Ubi? Unde? Quō?*

:: Ubi bene, ibi patria. || Where life is good, there is my homeland. (proverb, after Cicero)

In vīllā sum. Ē vīllā veniō. In forum eō. Rōmam eō!

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *In culīnā* vs *in culīnam*? | "In the kitchen" (ablative: where you are) · "into the kitchen" (accusative: where you're going). The Law of the Switch (Chapter 8). |
| "Out of the garden, away from the forum, with my friends, without cheese." | *Ex hortō* (or *ē hortō*), *ā forō* (or *ab*), *cum amīcīs meīs, sine cāseō.* All ablative (Chapter 7). The short *ē* and *ā* go only before a consonant: always *ex ātriō*. |
| "To the shop, through the streets, around the forum, across the road." | *Ad tabernam, per viās, circum forum, trāns viam.* All accusative (Chapter 8). |
| "I'm going home. I'm at home. I'm coming from home." | *Domum eō. Domī sum. Domō veniō.* No preposition. |
| The same three with Rome. | *Rōmam eō. Rōmae sum. Rōmā veniō.* Town names behave like *domus*. |
| "Marcus goes from home to Rome with Quīntus." | *Marcus cum Quīntō domō Rōmam it.* |
| The motto, *Ubi bene, ibi patria.* | "Where (it's) well, there (is my) homeland." *Patria*: fatherland, homeland (first family). Cicero quotes an old poet for the idea: home is wherever life is good. |

## 6. *Quīnte! Liber Quīntī. Quīntō dō.*

:: Nōn scholae sed vītae discimus. || We learn not for school but for life. (after Seneca)

Quīnte, venī! Liber Quīntī est. Quīntō librum dō.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| One name, four jobs. Call Quīntus, say "Quīntus's book," "I give it to Quīntus," "I see Quīntus." | *Quīnte! · liber Quīntī · Quīntō librum dō · Quīntum videō.* |
| Now Cornēlia: call her, "Cornēlia's doll," "I give Cornēlia a cookie." | *Cornēlia! · pūpa Cornēliae · Cornēliae crustulum dō.* *Cornēliae* twice: "of" and "to" look alike in the first family. |
| *Puerī* can mean what? | "Boys" (subject), "of the boy," or "boys!" (calling). The Law of the Twins (Chapter 10). |
| *Puerō*: "to the boy," or something else? | Either the dative, "to/for the boy," or the ablative after a preposition: *cum puerō*, "with the boy." The Law of the Look-alikes: check the company (Chapter 11). |
| *Marcus puerīs librōs magistrī dat.* Sort every word. | *Marcus* gives (subject) *librōs* (the object) *magistrī* (the teacher's) *puerīs* (to the boys). "Marcus gives the teacher's books to the boys." |
| *Pila Marcī est* vs *Pila Marcō est*? | "The ball is Marcus's" (whose?) · "Marcus has a ball" (what does he have?). |
| "My son!" "Of my son." "To my son." | *Mī fīlī!* · *fīliī meī* · *fīliō meō.* |
| The motto, *Nōn scholae sed vītae discimus.* Find the datives. | *Scholae*, "for school," and *vītae*, "for life." *Discimus*, "we learn" (like *legimus*). Seneca actually wrote it the other way round, as a complaint: *Nōn vītae sed scholae discimus*, "we learn for school, not for life" (*Letters* 106). Teachers flipped it into a motto. |

## 7. Lectiō: *Omnēs viae Rōmam dūcunt*

:: Omnēs viae Rōmam dūcunt. || All roads lead to Rome. (medieval proverb)

Three scenes, three places: *Domī*, *Rōmae*, *Domum*. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. New words: *tabernārius*, shopkeeper (a *taberna* man); *fessus*, tired; *tandem*, at last. In the title, *omnēs*, "all," is third family (Book II).

### I. *Domī*

::: dialogue lectio-1
Māter | Līberī! Hodiē Rōmam īmus! || Mother | Kids! Today we're going to Rome!
Quīntus | Rōmam? Optimē! || Quīntus | To Rome? Excellent!
Pater | Iōannēs, tū quoque venī! || Father | John, you come too!
Iōannēs | Grātiās tibi agō! Sed ubi est avus? || John | Thank you! But where's Grandpa?
Avia | Avus domī manet. Fessus est. || Grandma | Grandpa is staying home. He's tired.
Avus | Ita. Domī maneō et dormiō. Sed, Iōannēs, amābō tē: fer mihi cāseum Rōmā! || Grandpa | Yes. I'm staying home and sleeping. But, John, be a dear: bring me some cheese from Rome!
Iōannēs | Cāseum magnum tibi portō, ave! || John | I'll bring you a big cheese, Grandpa!
Marcus | Et ego cāseum portō… in stomachō meō! || Marcus | And I'll carry some cheese… in my stomach!
Māter | Marce! Nōlī cāseum avī edere! || Mother | Marcus! Don't eat Grandpa's cheese!
Pater | Age, līberī! Per viam longam Rōmam ambulāmus. || Father | Come on, kids! We're walking to Rome along the long road.
:::

### II. *Rōmae*

::: dialogue lectio-2
Cornēlia | Ecce Rōma! Ecce forum! Magnum est! || Cornēlia | Look, Rome! Look, the forum! It's huge!
Quīntus | Et templa alba! Et tabernae! Viae plēnae sunt! || Quīntus | And white temples! And shops! The streets are full!
Pater | Marce, ubi est magister tuus? || Father | Marcus, where's your teacher?
Marcus | Ibi est, in forō, cum discipulīs. || Marcus | There he is, in the forum, with his students.
Magister | Salvē, Marce! Salvē, Iōannēs! Quid agitis? || Teacher | Hello, Marcus! Hello, John! How are you both?
Marcus | Optimē, magister! Hodiē Rōmae sumus! || Marcus | Great, sir! We're in Rome today!
Magister | Bene! Et ubi est liber tuus, Marce? || Teacher | Good! And where's your book, Marcus?
Marcus | Liber meus? Domī est… in capsā… fortasse. || Marcus | My book? It's at home… in my book box… maybe.
Magister | Semper Marcus! Valēte! || Teacher | Always Marcus! Goodbye, y'all!
Māter | Nunc ad tabernam īmus. || Mother | Now we go to the shop.
Tabernārius | Salvēte! Quid emitis? || Shopkeeper | Hello, y'all! What are you buying?
Iōannēs | Cāseum avō emō: dōnum est. Cāseum magnum, quaesō. || John | I'm buying a cheese for Grandpa: it's a present. A big cheese, please.
Tabernārius | Ecce cāseus magnus et optimus. Dā mihi pecūniam, quaesō. || Shopkeeper | Here's a big cheese, the best. Your money, please.
Iōannēs | Ecce pecūnia. Grātiās tibi agō! || John | Here's the money. Thank you!
Quīntus | Pater, amābō tē, eme nōbīs crustula! || Quīntus | Dad, pretty please, buy us cookies!
Pater | Crustula? Minimē! Cēna domī est. || Father | Cookies? No way! Dinner is at home.
Cornēlia | Ego pūpam novam emō. Pecūnia mihi est! || Cornēlia | I'm buying a new doll. I have money!
Pater | Nunc domum īmus. Valē! || Father | Now we're going home. Goodbye!
:::

### III. *Domum*

::: dialogue lectio-3
Māter | Ecce vīlla nostra. Domī sumus! || Mother | Here's our house. We're home!
Avus | Salvēte, līberī! Salvē, Iōannēs! Ubi est cāseus meus? || Grandpa | Hello, kids! Hello, John! Where's my cheese?
Iōannēs | Ecce, ave: cāseum tibi dō. Dōnum est. || John | Here, Grandpa: I'm giving you the cheese. It's a present.
Avus | Grātiās tibi agō, Iōannēs! Magnus est, et optimus! || Grandpa | Thank you, John! It's big, and the best!
Marcus | Ave, amābō tē… dā mihi cāseum! || Marcus | Grandpa, pretty please… give me the cheese!
Quīntus | Et mihi! Et mihi! || Quīntus | Me too! Me too!
Avia | Nōlīte, puerī! Cāseus avī est. || Grandma | Don't, boys! The cheese is Grandpa's.
Avus | Ita: cāseus meus est. Sedēte et spectāte! || Grandpa | Right: it's my cheese. Sit down and watch!
Iōannēs | Ecce: avus cāseum edit. Et rīdet! || John | Look: Grandpa is eating the cheese. And he's smiling!
Quīntus | Ave… et nōs? || Quīntus | Grandpa… and us?
Avus | Hodiē mihi, crās tibi! || Grandpa | Today it's mine; tomorrow, yours!
Avia | Ave! Dā cāseum līberīs! || Grandma | Grandpa! Give the cheese to the kids!
Avus | Bene, bene. Cāseus familiae est. Līberī, cāseum vōbīs dō. Et tibi, mea Cornēlia. Et tibi, Iōannēs. || Grandpa | All right, all right. The cheese is the family's. Kids, I'm giving the cheese to y'all. And to you, my Cornēlia. And to you, John.
Puerī | Grātiās tibi agimus, ave! || The boys | Thanks, Grandpa!
Iōannēs | Tandem! Cāseus familiae in stomachō avī est, et in stomachīs nostrīs. || John | At last! The family's cheese is in Grandpa's stomach, and in ours.
Quīntus | Pater, crāsne iterum Rōmam īmus? || Quīntus | Dad, are we going to Rome again tomorrow?
Pater | Minimē! Crās domī manēmus. || Father | No way! Tomorrow we're staying home.
:::

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The three scene titles, *Domī, Rōmae, Domum*: what does each say? | "At home," "at Rome," "(to) home." Two locatives and a place-to-which, no prepositions (Chapter 8). The whole trip in three words. |
| *Fer mihi cāseum Rōmā!* What's *Rōmā*? | "From Rome": ablative, no preposition, because Rome is a town. |
| *Nōlī cāseum avī edere!* Could *avī* be "to Grandpa"? | No: "to Grandpa" is *avō*. *Avī* sits beside *cāseum*, the thing owned: "Grandpa's cheese." |
| *Per viam longam Rōmam ambulāmus.* Two accusatives without a fight. Why? | *Viam longam* after *per*, "through, along the long road." *Rōmam*, a town, "to Rome," with no preposition. |
| Roman teachers really did hold school in the forum, in shop-fronts (Livy 3.44). Find Marcus's excuse. | *Liber meus? Domī est… in capsā… fortasse.* (Chapter 10's *capsa*, and Chapter 7's *fortasse*.) |
| *Cāseum avō emō.* Is Grandpa the receiver? | Yes, the one it's **for**: dative (Chapter 11's *Cēnam tibi parō*). |
| *Eme nōbīs crustula!* What form is *eme*? | A command to one, from *emere* (third family, like *lege*). Quīntus wants cookies **for us**: *nōbīs*. |
| *Hodiē mihi, crās tibi!* Where have you heard it? | Chapter 11's tombstone motto. Grandpa turns it into a joke: my turn today, yours tomorrow. |

## 8. *Nōmen mihi est Iōannēs*

:: Salvēte! Nōmen mihi est Iōannēs. || Hello, y'all! My name is John.

Now it's your turn. Build your speech one line at a time, then put it together.

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Greet a room full of people, then say your name, two ways. | *Salvēte, amīcī!* · *Nōmen mihi est Iōannēs.* · *Iōannēs sum.* (Add *ego* to stress it: *Ego Iōannēs sum.*) |
| Where do you live? Try "in a small town," "in a big house." | *In oppidō parvō habitō.* · *In vīllā magnā habitō.* *In* + ablative: where (Chapter 7). If you lived in Rome: *Rōmae habitō*, no *in*. |
| Who's in your life? Use *est/sunt* + *mihi*. "I have a son," "I have good friends." | *Fīlius mihi est.* · *Amīcī bonī mihi sunt.* Swap in what's true: *fīlia, līberī, familia magna…* (Chapter 11). |
| What do you do? "I work. I read books. I often walk in the garden." | *Labōrō. Librōs legō. Saepe in hortō ambulō.* |
| What do you love? "I love cheese. I love red wine too." | *Cāseum amō. Vīnum rubrum quoque amō.* |
| One new word for a proud line: ***Latīnē***, "in Latin" (la-TEE-nay). "I read and write in Latin." | *Latīnē legō et scrībō.* |
| Turn it around: ask someone their name and where they live. | *Et tū? Quid est nōmen tibi? Ubi habitās?* |
| Close. | *Grātiās vōbīs agō. Valēte!* (y'all) |

!!! example "Ōrātiō tua: your two-minute self-introduction"
    Fill in each line with what's true for you. Say it slowly: two minutes is about twenty short sentences.

    1. **Greeting:** *Salvēte, amīcī!*
    2. **Name:** *Nōmen mihi est ___. ___ sum.*
    3. **Home:** *In ___ habitō.* (*in vīllā parvā, in vīllā magnā, in oppidō parvō, in oppidō antīquō*: the adjective matches)
    4. **People:** *___ mihi est.* · *___ mihi sunt.* (*fīlius, fīlia, līberī, amīcī bonī*)
    5. **A day:** *Saepe ___. Semper ___. Hodiē ___.* (*labōrō, ambulō, legō, scrībō, cantō, dormiō*)
    6. **Loves:** *___ amō.* (*cāseum, vīnum, librōs, pōma*)
    7. **Places:** *Saepe ___ eō. Hodiē domī sum.* (*ad tabernam, in forum, in hortum, in silvam*)
    8. **Latin:** *Latīnē legō et scrībō.*
    9. **A joke:** *Nōn bene cantō!* or *Cāseus meus saepe in stomachō amīcī est!*
    10. **Questions back:** *Et tū? Quid est nōmen tibi? Ubi habitās?*
    11. **Close:** *Grātiās vōbīs agō. Valēte!*

Here's one model, all Book I Latin. Hide the English, read the Latin aloud, then swap in your own facts.

::: dialogue oratio
Iōannēs | Salvēte, amīcī! || John | Hello, friends!
Iōannēs | Nōmen mihi est Iōannēs. Iōannēs sum, nōn Marcus! || John | My name is John. I'm John, not Marcus!
Iōannēs | In vīllā habitō. Vīlla mea nōn magna est, sed bona. || John | I live in a house. My house isn't big, but it's good.
Iōannēs | Hortus mihi est. In hortō saepe ambulō et cōgitō. || John | I have a garden. I often walk and think in the garden.
Iōannēs | Familia mihi est, et amīcī bonī mihi sunt. || John | I have a family, and I have good friends.
Iōannēs | Saepe labōrō. Hodiē nōn labōrō: hodiē Latīnē legō! || John | I often work. Today I'm not working: today I'm reading Latin!
Iōannēs | Librōs amō. Saepe legō et scrībō. || John | I love books. I often read and write.
Iōannēs | Vīnum rubrum amō, et cāseum. Cāseum semper amō! || John | I love red wine, and cheese. I always love cheese!
Iōannēs | Sed cāseus meus saepe in stomachō Marcī est. || John | But my cheese is often in Marcus's stomach.
Iōannēs | Nōn bene cantō. Amīcī meī rīdent! || John | I don't sing well. My friends laugh!
Iōannēs | Rōmam nōn saepe eō, sed Rōmam amō. || John | I don't often go to Rome, but I love Rome.
Iōannēs | Et tū? Quid est nōmen tibi? Ubi habitās? || John | And you? What's your name? Where do you live?
Iōannēs | Grātiās vōbīs agō. Valēte! || John | Thank you all. Goodbye!
:::

## 9. *Quid didicistī?*

:: Fēstīnā lentē. || Make haste slowly. (Augustus's favorite saying)

*Quid didicistī?* means "What have you learned?" (a past form, Book III; just enjoy it). Nine quick ones from all over Book I. Answer aloud, then check. Seven right? Book I is yours.

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Mālum* or *malum*? | Long ā, *mālum*: an apple. Short a, *malum*: a bad thing. (Chapter 1) |
| "The girls are good." | *Puellae bonae sunt.* (Chapters 2–3) |
| "Marcus eats the apples." | *Marcus māla edit.* (Chapter 4) |

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking.

1. Decline *puer parvus* and *puella bona* through all six cases, singular and plural, at walking pace.
2. Chant *amō, habeō, legō, audiō, capiō*, then *sum, eō, dō*. Then the commands: *amā, sedē, lege, audī, cape* and their *-te* plurals.
3. Walk through your house and narrate: *In culīnam eō · In culīnā sum · Ē culīnā veniō.* Then *Domum eō · Domī sum · Domō veniō.*
4. Pick one name and run it through every job: *Quīnte! · liber Quīntī · Quīntō dō · Quīntum videō · cum Quīntō.*
5. Ask the traveler's three questions and answer them for yourself: *Quis es? Unde venīs? Quō īs?*
6. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, all three scenes, playing every part. Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
7. Give your two-minute speech, slowly. Time it. Then give it again without looking.
