# Caput IX: Quīnte!

*Liber I, Prīma Verba · Chapter 9*

*In which, after eight chapters of talking about Marcus, somebody finally talks **to** him. We meet his family, learn the "calling" form of a name, and learn to say my, your, our, and y'all's. A small brother goes missing. So does the cheese.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Salvē, Marce!* · *Venī hūc, mī fīlī!* · *Ecce familia mea.* · *Pater noster* · *in stomachum tuum*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** vocative (the "calling" case); possessive (a "whose" word)
    - **Forms:** *Marcus → Marce*, *fīlius → fīlī*; *meus, tuus, noster, vester* with every ending you know; *mī*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Call, the Law of the Owned Thing
    - **Words:** family: son, daughter, children, grandfather, grandmother, father, mother, brother, sister; master and mistress of the house
    - **Reading:** *Quīnte!* (Where is Quīntus? Where is the cheese?)
    - **Speaking:** call people by name; say whose things are

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. This time, *call*: say every name as if the person were across the garden.

---

## 1. *Marce!*

:: Et tū, Brūte? || You too, Brutus? (Shakespeare, *Julius Caesar*: Latin in an English play)

Marce! Ubi es, Marce? Venī hūc, Marce!

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| For eight chapters we have talked *about* Marcus: *Marcus cāseum edit. Marcus domī dormit.* Has anyone ever talked *to* him? | No. We avoided it on purpose. Today he gets called. |
| Marcus is in the garden. You want him: "Marcus, come here!" Guess the Latin. | *Marce, venī hūc!* Not *Marcus*: when you call a Marcus, the *-us* becomes **-e**. MAR-ke. |
| This calling form has a name: the **vocative**. Any English words you hear in it? | *Vocāre*, "to call": vocal, invoke, vocation. The vocative is the form for the person you're talking *to*. |
| "Hello, Marcus!" | *Salvē, Marce!* |
| "How are you, Marcus?" | *Quid agis, Marce?* A name you call is set off with commas, and Romans often tuck it in after the first word or two. |
| Chapter 1's first motto was *Salvē, amīce!* Now you know *amīcus*, "friend" (Chapter 7). Why *amīce*? | Same change: *amīcus → amīce.* You've been saying a vocative since page one. |
| Chapter 8's motto, in full: *Domine, quō vādis?* *Dominus* is "lord, master." Which form is *Domine*? | The vocative. Peter is speaking *to* the Lord. |
| *Quīntus* is a Roman first name (it means "fifth"; Horace was a Quīntus). Call him. | *Quīnte!* KWEEN-te. |
| "Listen, student!" (*discipulus*) | *Audī, discipule!* |
| "Marcus, don't eat the cheese!" Does anything change besides the name? | *Marce, nōlī cāseum edere!* Only the person you're calling changes. *Cāseum* is still the thing eaten. |
| Now the motto, *Et tū, Brūte?* *Brūtus* was one of the men who stabbed Julius Caesar. Which word is the vocative? | *Brūte*, from *Brūtus*. *Et tū*: "you too" (*et* can mean "also"). Shakespeare's Caesar says it as he falls. |

!!! info "Grammar words, in plain English"
    **Vocative** (from *vocāre*, "to call"): the form of a noun used for the person or thing you are speaking **to**. Old grammars call it the "case of address." English shows it only with a comma ("Marcus, come here!"); Latin often changes the ending too.

## 2. *Iūlia, puer, Iōannēs*

:: Salvēte, discipulī! || Hello, students! (Chapter 1)

Salvē, Iūlia! Salvē, puer! Salvēte, amīcī!

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 1: *Salvē, Iūlia!* Did *Iūlia* change? | No. *-a* names were already the right shape: *Iūlia, Cornēlia.* The *-a* is short, as in the naming form (not the long *-ā* of Chapter 7's ablative). |
| Chapter 1 also: *Salvē, magister!* Did *magister* change? | No. Only *-us* words of the *hortus* family change. *-er* words like *magister* and *puer* stay as they are. |
| "Hello, girl!" "Hello, boy!" | *Salvē, puella! Salvē, puer!* |
| A shiny apple sits on the table. Greet it. | *Salvē, mālum!* (Odd, but good Latin.) Neuters don't change either. |
| Iūlia greets you. Does *Iōannēs* change? | No: *Salvē, Iōannēs!* Your name comes from Greek (and Hebrew before that) and belongs to a different family of nouns, the third (Book II). There the calling form is the same as the naming form, so every chapter so far already had it right. |
| Two or more people: "Hello, boys!" | *Salvēte, puerī!* In the plural, the vocative is **always** the same as the naming form. |
| So *Salvēte, discipulī!*, the motto, was already a vocative. "Hello, friends!"? | *Salvēte, amīcī!* Not *amīce*: that's one friend. |
| Sort these: *Marcus, Iūlia, puer, amīcus, mālum, Quīntus.* Which change when you call them? | *Marce, amīce, Quīnte* change. *Iūlia, puer, mālum* stay. |
| Chapter 8's *domus* ends in *-us* too. Does it become *dome*? | No. *Domus* belongs to another family (Chapter 8 said so), and its calling form is *domus*. The *-e* rule is only for *hortus*-family words like *Marcus, amīcus, dominus*. |

## 3. *Fīlī, Cornēlī*

:: Tū quoque, mī fīlī? || You too, my son? (Caesar's last words, in a later Latin version)

Venī, fīlī! Venī, Cornēlī! Venīte, fīliī!

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Fīlius* is "son" (think "filial"). FEE-li-us. Call him: "Come here, son!" Guess: *fīlie*? | *Venī hūc, fīlī!* The *-ius* words squeeze *-ie* down to a long **-ī**. FEE-lee. |
| *Cornēlius* is a great Roman family name. Call a Cornēlius. | *Cornēlī!* kor-NAY-lee. The stress stays on the long ē, where it was. |
| Notice anything about *Cornēlius* and *Cornēlia*? | A Roman daughter took her father's family name with *-a*. The daughter of a Cornēlius is a Cornēlia; of a Iūlius, a Iūlia. So our Cornēlia's father is a Cornēlius. |
| *Fīlia* is "daughter." "Come here, daughter!" | *Venī hūc, fīlia!* Unchanged, like every *-a* word. |
| "Come here, sons!" | *Venīte, fīliī!* Plural, so the naming form: two i's, FEE-li-ee. Don't confuse it with *fīlī*, one son. |
| The motto, *Tū quoque, mī fīlī?* Which word is a vocative of an *-ius* word? | *Fīlī*. *Tū quoque*: "you too" (Chapter 5). *Mī* is "my," in its calling form (section 5). |
| Who said it? | Julius Caesar, to Brutus, as the story goes. The historian Suetonius says Caesar died with only a groan, though some claimed he said, in Greek, "You too, child?" *Tū quoque, mī fīlī* is a later Latin version; Shakespeare's *Et tū, Brūte?* is another. |

!!! success "The Law of the Call"
    The vocative (calling form) is the same as the naming form, **except** for singular *-us* words of the *hortus* family:

    | naming form | calling form | example |
    |---|---|---|
    | *-us* | **-e** | *Marcus → Marce, amīcus → amīce* |
    | *-ius* (nouns and names) | **-ī** | *fīlius → fīlī, Cornēlius → Cornēlī* |
    | anything else | same | *Iūlia, puer, magister, mālum, domus, Iōannēs* |
    | any plural | same | *puerī, puellae, amīcī* |

## 4. *Familia*

:: Avē, ave! || Hello, Grandpa!

Ecce familia: pater, māter, fīlius, fīlia, avus, avia.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Familia*: family, and more: the whole household under one roof. Say it. | fa-MI-li-a. Stress on *mi*, because *li* is short. |
| *Avus* is "grandfather," *avia* "grandmother." A-wus, A-wi-a. "Hello, Grandpa!" with Chapter 1's *avē*. | *Avē, ave!* The first is "hello" (long ē, hold it); the second is "Grandpa!" (*avus → ave*, short e, clip it). |
| "Hello, Grandma!" | *Salvē, avia!* (or *Avē, avia!*) |
| *Līberī*: children, the kids of a family. Plural only. LEE-be-ree. Which Chapter 2 word must you not mix it up with? | *Librī*, "books" (short i, no e). *Līberī* has a long ī. |
| Four core family words come from the third family of nouns: *pater, māter, frāter, soror*. Guess them from English. | Father, mother, brother, sister: paternal, maternal, fraternal, sorority. |
| Their other endings wait for Book II. For now, use them only as the **subject** or to call someone. What is their calling form? | The same as the naming form, like *Iōannēs*: *Pater! Māter! Frāter! Soror!* |
| Masculine or feminine? | By meaning: *pater, frāter* masculine; *māter, soror* feminine. |
| "Mom, where's Quīntus?" | *Māter, ubi est Quīntus?* |
| "Father and mother are in the garden." | *Pater et māter in hortō sunt.* |
| *Dominus* and *domina*: the master and mistress of the house. Which Chapter 8 word hides inside? | *Domus*, house. A *dominus* is the man of the *domus*. And *Domine* was the motto's "Lord." |

## 5. *Meus, tuus*

:: Mī frāter, mī frāter, mī frāter! || My brother, my brother, my brother! (Cicero, to his brother Quīntus, 58 BC)

Fīlius meus. Fīlia mea. Mālum meum!

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Meus* is "my." *Fīlius meus*: my son. "My daughter"? | *Fīlia mea.* *Meus* changes like Chapter 3's *magnus*: *meus, mea, meum.* |
| "My apple"? | *Mālum meum.* |
| Where does *meus* usually go? | After its noun: *fīlius meus.* Put it first to shout it: *Meum mālum est!* "It's **my** apple!" |
| *Tuus* is "your" (one person's): *tuus, tua, tuum.* "Your cheese, your cup"? | *Cāseus tuus, pōculum tuum.* |
| Plurals, like *magnī, magnae, magna*: "my friends, your apples"? | *Amīcī meī, māla tua.* |
| Iūlia says "my son." *Fīlius meus* or *fīlius mea*? Iūlia is a woman. | *Fīlius meus.* *Meus* matches the **son**, not Iūlia. |
| Marcus says "my mother." | *Māter mea.* Feminine, because *māter* is. Marcus being a boy doesn't matter. |
| Calling someone "my son!" | *Mī fīlī!* MEE FEE-lee. *Mī* is the calling form of *meus*. |
| "My daughter!" (calling her) | *Mea fīlia!* Only the masculine *meus* has a special calling form. |
| "My friend!" (calling a man) | *Mī amīce!* or *amīce mī.* |
| The motto: Cicero (a Marcus!) writing from exile to his little brother Quīntus. Why *mī* and not *meus*? | He's calling to him: *mī frāter*, "my brother!" And *frāter*, a third-family word, keeps its naming form. |
| Chapter 1: *Nōmen mihi est Iōannēs.* Could you say "my name" with *meus*? | Yes: *Nōmen meum est Iōannēs.* *Nōmen* is neuter, so *meum*. But *nōmen mihi est* is the Roman favorite; keep using it. |

!!! success "The Law of the Owned Thing"
    A possessive (*meus, tuus, noster, vester*) agrees with the **thing owned**, not with the owner:

    - Iūlia: *fīlius meus* (son: masculine)
    - Marcus: *māter mea* (mother: feminine)
    - anyone: *mālum meum* (apple: neuter)

    It takes the owned thing's gender, number, and case, just like *magnus*.

!!! info "Grammar words, in plain English"
    **Possessive**: a word that tells **whose** something is: my, your, our. Latin's possessives are adjectives, so they agree like *magnus*. Old grammars call them "possessive adjectives."

## 6. *Noster, vester*

:: Pater noster, quī es in caelīs. || Our Father, who art in heaven. (the Lord's Prayer)

Vīlla nostra magna est. Ubi est vīlla vestra?

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Noster* is "our." Like Chapter 3's *niger, nigra, nigrum*, it drops the e: *noster, nostra, nostrum.* "Our house" (*vīlla*)? | *Vīlla nostra.* |
| "Our garden," "our money"? | *Hortus noster, pecūnia nostra.* |
| *Vester* is "your" when the owners are **y'all**: *vester, vestra, vestrum.* "Y'all's dinner"? | *Cēna vestra.* |
| You're talking to Marcus alone, then to Marcus and Quīntus. "Your cheese" both ways. | *Cāseus tuus* (one owner) · *cāseus vester* (y'all's). Just like *tū* and *vōs*. |
| Spot the family resemblance with Chapter 5's *ego, tū, nōs, vōs* and Chapter 7's *mēcum, tēcum*. | *Meus* with *mē(cum)*, *tuus* with *tū, tē(cum)*, *noster* with *nōs*, *vester* with *vōs*. |
| "Our friends," "y'all's children." | *Amīcī nostrī, līberī vestrī.* |
| The motto: *Pater noster, quī es in caelīs.* Is *Pater* the subject? | No: a vocative. The prayer is spoken **to** the Father. Neither *pater* nor *noster* changes (third family; *-er* word). |
| And *in caelīs*? | Chapter 7's *in* + ablative plural: "in the heavens." *Quī es*: "who are" (*quī* comes much later). |

## 7. *In stomachum tuum*

:: Meā culpā. || Through my fault. (the Confiteor, Latin Mass)

Cāseus in mēnsam it, Marce, nōn in stomachum tuum!

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Possessives take every ending their noun takes. "I see your garden." | *Hortum tuum videō.* Accusative, so *tuum.* |
| "I love my grandmother." | *Aviam meam amō.* |
| "In our kitchen." | *In culīnā nostrā.* Ablative: long ā on both. |
| "With my friends." | *Cum amīcīs meīs.* |
| Chapter 8's Lectiō said "not into your stomach!" in English only. Now say the whole line in Latin, to Marcus. | *Cāseus in mēnsam it, Marce, nōn in stomachum tuum!* |
| What's wrong with *fīlius mea*? | *Fīlius* is masculine: *fīlius meus.* |
| The motto, *Meā culpā*: which case? | Ablative, Chapter 7's long *-ā*, here meaning "through, because of" (a use for later). *Culpa*: fault (think "culprit"). Missals print *mea culpa*, but both a's are long. |

## 8. Lectiō: *Quīnte!*

:: Ubi es, Quīnte? || Where are you, Quīntus?

Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English.

::: dialogue lectio
Iōannēs | Salvē, Marce! Salvē, Cornēlia! || John | Hi, Marcus! Hi, Cornēlia!
Marcus | Salvē, Iōannēs! Venī, amīce. Ecce familia mea! || Marcus | Hi, John! Come in, friend. Here's my family!
Cornēlia | Ecce pater noster et māter nostra. || Cornēlia | Here are our father and our mother.
Iōannēs | Salvēte! || John | Hello, y'all!
Māter | Salvē, Iōannēs! Sed ubi est Quīntus? || Mother | Hello, John! But where's Quīntus?
Iōannēs | Quis est Quīntus? || John | Who's Quīntus?
Cornēlia | Frāter noster. Parvus est, sed semper edit! || Cornēlia | Our brother. He's little, but he's always eating!
Māter | Quīnte! Ubi es, Quīnte? || Mother | Quīntus! Where are you, Quīntus?
Pater | Quīnte! Venī hūc, mī fīlī! || Father | Quīntus! Come here, my son!
Marcus | Fortasse in hortō est. Avē, ave! Ubi est Quīntus? || Marcus | Maybe he's in the garden. Hi, Grandpa! Where's Quīntus?
Avus | Nōn in hortō est. Avia et ego hīc sumus. || Grandpa | He's not in the garden. Grandma and I are here.
Pater | Et ubi est cāseus noster? Nōn in mēnsā est! || Father | And where's our cheese? It isn't on the table!
Māter | Marce! || Mother | Marcus!
Pater | Marce! || Father | Marcus!
Marcus | Quid? Ego? Nōn ego! Hodiē nōn edō! || Marcus | What? Me? Not me! I'm not eating today!
Cornēlia | Mī frāter, semper tū es! || Cornēlia | My brother, it's always you!
Iōannēs | Audīte! Quis in culīnā est? || John | Listen, y'all! Who's in the kitchen?
Māter | Quīnte! In culīnā es! || Mother | Quīntus! You're in the kitchen!
Quīntus | Salvēte! Cāseus noster optimus est! || Quīntus | Hi, y'all! Our cheese is the best!
Marcus | Et tū, Quīnte? Cāseus in stomachum tuum it! || Marcus | You too, Quīntus? The cheese is going into your stomach!
Pater | Quīnte, mī fīlī, nōlī cāseum edere! || Father | Quīntus, my son, don't eat the cheese!
Avia | Rīdeō! Fīlius tuus parvus Marcus est! || Grandma | Ha! Your son is a little Marcus!
Iōannēs | Marce, amīce, nōn semper tū! || John | Marcus, my friend: it isn't always you!
Marcus | Grātiās, Iōannēs! Nunc… ubi est cāseus meus? || Marcus | Thanks, John! Now… where's my cheese?
:::

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Salvē, Marce! Salvē, Cornēlia!* Which name changed, and why? | *Marce*: an *-us* name. *Cornēlia* is an *-a* name and stays. |
| *Venī hūc, mī fīlī!* Who says it, and which words are vocative? | The father, calling Quīntus: *mī fīlī*, "my son!" |
| *Ecce pater noster et māter nostra.* Why *noster*, then *nostra*? | Each matches its noun: *pater* masculine, *māter* feminine. Cornēlia is the owner either way. |
| *Mī frāter*: can *mī* go with a third-family word? | Yes. *Mī* goes with any male you call "my": *mī fīlī, mī amīce, mī frāter.* |
| *Cāseus in stomachum tuum it.* Why *tuum*, and why *-um*? | It matches *stomachum*: masculine, one, and accusative after *in* "into." |
| *Fīlius tuus parvus Marcus est.* Whose son? | The father's: Grandma is talking to him. *Tuus* agrees with *fīlius*. |
| *Et tū, Quīnte?* What is Marcus quoting? | Caesar's *Et tū, Brūte?* Quīntus is the cheese assassin. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking.

1. Call five people, out loud, across the room: *Marce! Quīnte! Cornēlī! Iūlia! Iōannēs!* Say which ones changed.
2. Greet the whole family: *Salvē, pater! Salvē, māter! Avē, ave! Salvē, avia! Salvē, frāter! Salvē, soror!*
3. Call the kids: *Venī hūc, mī fīlī! Venī hūc, mea fīlia! Venīte, līberī!*
4. Point at things in your house and claim them: *mēnsa mea, sella mea, pōculum meum, liber meus.* Then give them away: *mēnsa tua, sella tua…*
5. Same things, shared: *mēnsa nostra … mēnsa vestra.*
6. Say the line Marcus has been waiting for: *Cāseus in mēnsam it, Marce, nōn in stomachum tuum!*
7. Say the mottos and point at each vocative: *Et tū, Brūte? · Tū quoque, mī fīlī? · Mī frāter! · Pater noster.*
8. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing all eight parts. Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
