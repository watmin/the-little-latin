# Caput VII: Ubi est?

*Liber I, Prīma Verba · Chapter 7*

*In which the cheese goes missing, and you learn to say where things are, where they come from, and who they're with. Five little words do all the work, and they all pull the same new ending onto the noun after them.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Ubi est Marcus?* · *In culīnā est.* · *Unde venīs?* · *Venī mēcum!*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** preposition, ablative (the "where / from / with" case)
    - **Forms:** *in mēnsā, in mēnsīs*; *in hortō, in hortīs*; *in forō, in forīs*; *ex / ē*, *ab / ā*, *cum*, *sine*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Long ā, the Law of the Long ō, the Law of *-īs*, the Law of *ē* and *ā*, the Law of the Little Words
    - **Words:** rooms of the house, the town, friends, *ubi, unde, ibi, fortasse*
    - **Reading:** Where is the cheese?
    - **Speaking:** ask and answer "where is it?" and "where are you coming from?"

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Point at real things in your own house as you go: the table, the kitchen, the chair.

---

## 1. *Hīc, hūc… ubi?*

:: Ubi tū es? || Where are you?

*Ubi tū es?* Iōannēs hīc est. Sed ubi est cāseus?

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 6: *Venī hūc!* What did *hūc* mean? | "To here, this way." Where you're headed. |
| And Chapter 5's *hīc*? | "Here," where you already are. *Hīc habitō*: I live here. |
| So *hūc* answers "where to?" Which question does *hīc* answer? | Plain "where?" No motion, just a spot. |
| Latin for "where?" is *ubi*. Say *Ubi est Marcus?* | OO-bi est MAR-kus? "Where is Marcus?" Both vowels in *ubi* short. |
| Answer: he's here. | *Hīc est.* |
| "There" is *ibi*. He's over there. | *Ibi est.* I-bi. |
| *Ubi est cāseus?* A Roman answers *In mēnsā est.* Which word is the table? | *Mēnsā.* But Chapter 2's table was *mēnsa*. Something changed. |

## 2. *In mēnsā*

:: Nūlla diēs sine līneā. || No day without a line. (after Pliny, *Natural History* 35.84, of the painter Apelles)

Mēnsa in culīnā est. Cāseus in mēnsā est.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Mēnsa* vs *mēnsā*. What's different? | Only the last vowel: short *a* vs long *ā*. MAYN-sa vs MAYN-sah. |
| When does it change? | After *in*. The word after *in* takes a new ending. |
| *In mēnsā est* means? | "It's on the table." *In* means "in" **or** "on." |
| *Culīna* is "kitchen" (think "culinary"). "In the kitchen"? | *In culīnā.* in ku-LEE-nah. |
| *Vīlla*: a house, often a big one in the country. "In the house"? | *In vīllā.* WEEL-lah: v is w (church habit: VIL-la). (A town house is *domus*, a rule-breaker that waits for Chapter 8.) |
| "On the chair"? | *In sellā.* |
| *Ubi est puella?* Answer: in the kitchen. | *In culīnā est.* |
| Could your eye tell *mēnsa* from *mēnsā* without a macron? | No. Only your mouth can, and this book's macrons. Chapter 1's Commandment of Length again. |
| The motto has been closing your homework since Chapter 1. Spot the long ā. | *Līneā*, after *sine* ("without"). *Līnea* is "line." "No day without a line." |

!!! success "The Law of the Long ā"
    After *in* (and, as you'll see, after *ex, ab, cum, sine*), an *-a* noun stretches its ending: *mēnsa → in mēnsā*. Short a becomes long ā. You can't see it without the macron, so say it.

!!! info "Grammar words, in plain English"
    **Preposition**: a little word placed *before* a noun to show where, from where, or with what. *In mēnsā*: "on the table." (Pre-position: "put in front.")

    **Ablative**: the case (the noun's job-form, Chapter 2) that comes after *in*, *ex*, *ab*, *cum*, and *sine*. The name means "carried away" (*ablātus*), after its "from" job. For now, think of it as the **where / from / with** case; it picks up more jobs in later books. You now know three cases: nominative, accusative, ablative.

## 3. *In hortō*

:: Multum in parvō. || Much in little.

In hortō sumus. Ubi est Marcus? Nōn in hortō!

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Hortus*: "garden" (think "horticulture"). "In the garden"? Guess. | *In hortō.* HOR-toh, a long ō. |
| *Forum*: the town square. "In the forum"? | *In forō.* |
| So *-us* words and *-um* words both make…? | *-ō*, long. |
| *Oppidum*: "town." Marcus is in town. | *Marcus in oppidō est.* |
| Wine in the cup? (*pōculum*, Chapter 3) | *Vīnum in pōculō.* |
| *Cubiculum*: "bedroom." We sleep in the bedroom. | *In cubiculō dormīmus.* |
| *Liber, librum*: "book." "In the book"? | *In librō.* The *-er* words use their Chapter 2 stem: *libr-ō*, just like *librī*. |
| The motto from Chapter 3: *Multum in parvō*. Why *parvō*? | It's after *in*: "in a little (thing)." An adjective takes the same ending. "Much in little." |

!!! success "The Law of the Long ō"
    After *in*, an *-us* or *-um* noun (and an *-er* noun) ends in long ***-ō***: *hortus → in hortō*, *forum → in forō*, *liber → in librō*.

## 4. *In hortīs*

:: In vīnō vēritās. || In wine, truth. (proverb)

Mēnsa, mēnsae. In mēnsā… in mēnsīs!

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| One table: *in mēnsā*. Many tables: *in mēnsīs*. The ending? | *-īs*, long ī. MAYN-sees. |
| One garden: *in hortō*. Many gardens? Guess. | *In hortīs.* The same *-īs*! |
| "In the cups"? | *In pōculīs.* |
| *Taberna*: a shop, an inn (think "tavern"). "In the shops"? | *In tabernīs.* |
| *Via*: street, road. WI-a (church habit: VEE-a). "In the streets"? | *In viīs.* WI-ees: *via*'s short i, then the long *-īs*. |
| "Wine in big cups." | *Vīnum in pōculīs magnīs.* The adjective wears the same *-īs*. |
| "In a big house." | *In vīllā magnā* (or *in magnā vīllā*). Long ā on both. |
| The motto: *In vīnō vēritās*. Which word is ablative? | *Vīnō*, after *in*. (*Vēritās*, "truth," is from a family you meet in Book II.) |

!!! success "The Law of *-īs*"
    In the plural, both families end the same way after *in*: ***-īs***. *In mēnsīs, in hortīs, in forīs, in librīs.*

    | | *-a* family | *-us* family | *-um* family |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | naming (nom.) | *mēnsa · mēnsae* | *hortus · hortī* | *forum · fora* |
    | object (acc.) | *mēnsam · mēnsās* | *hortum · hortōs* | *forum · fora* |
    | **ablative** | ***mēnsā · mēnsīs*** | ***hortō · hortīs*** | ***forō · forīs*** |

    Singular long *-ā* or long *-ō*; plural long *-īs* for everyone.

## 5. *Ex culīnā*

:: Ab ōvō usque ad māla. || From the egg all the way to the apples: start to finish. (Horace, *Satires* 1.3.6–7)

Marcus ex culīnā currit. Unde venit? Ē culīnā!

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Ubi?* asks "where?" Romans also ask *Unde?*: "from where?" *Unde venīs?* | UN-de WE-nees? "Where are you coming from?" |
| Answer: *Ex culīnā veniō.* What does *ex* mean? | "Out of, from." Think "exit." And *culīnā* has the same long ā as after *in*. |
| So *ex* pulls the same ending as *in*? | Yes. One ending, two jobs so far: where (*in*) and from where (*ex*). |
| Before a consonant, *ex* can shrink to *ē*. "Out of the shop"? | *Ē tabernā* or *ex tabernā*. Both are good Latin. |
| *Ē ātriō* ("out of the front hall")? | No! Before a vowel or *h*, only *ex*: *ex ātriō, ex hortō*. |
| *Ab*: "away from" (think "absent"). Marcus comes from the forum. | *Marcus ā forō venit.* Before a consonant *ab* usually shrinks to *ā*. |
| And from the town (*oppidum*)? | *Ab oppidō.* A vowel needs the full *ab*. |
| *Ex* vs *ab*: what's the picture? | *Ex*: out of the inside. *Ab*: away from the outside or the edge. *Ex culīnā*: out of the kitchen. *Ā culīnā*: away from the kitchen. |
| Old bookplates say *Ex librīs*. Meaning? | "From the books (of…)": this one comes out of someone's library. Long ī: eks LEE-brees. |
| The motto's *ab ōvō*? | "From the egg." A Roman dinner opened with eggs and closed with fruit. (*Ad māla*, "to the apples," is Chapter 8's job.) |

!!! success "The Law of *ē* and *ā*"
    *Ex* and *ab* can go anywhere. Their short forms ***ē*** and ***ā*** go **only before a consonant**: *ē tabernā, ā forō*, but always *ex ātriō, ab oppidō*.

## 6. *Ubi?* or *Unde?*

:: Unde venīs? || Where are you coming from?

Ubi est Marcus? In culīnā. Unde venit? Ē culīnā.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Marcus in culīnā est.* A "where" or a "from where"? | Where. *Ubi est?* In the kitchen. |
| *Marcus ē culīnā currit.* | From where. *Unde currit?* Out of the kitchen. |
| *Culīnā* has the same ending both times. So what tells you the difference? | The little word in front: *in* vs *ē*. |
| *Ubi habitās?* Answer: in a big house. | *In vīllā magnā habitō.* |
| *Unde venīs?* Answer for yourself. | *Ex Americā veniō.* (Modern Latin for America; it's an *-a* noun like *mēnsa*.) |
| *Unde veniunt puerī?* Answer: from the shops. | *Ē tabernīs* (or *ex tabernīs*) *veniunt.* |
| Marcus runs *into* the kitchen. Is that a "where"? | No, a "where to." Latin uses a different ending for that, and Chapter 8 (*Quō īs?*) teaches it. For now: *in* + ablative is sitting still. |

## 7. *Cum et sine*

:: Festīnā lentē, sed nōn sine cāseō. || Make haste slowly, but not without cheese.

Cum amīcīs edimus. Sine amīcīs, nōn est cēna.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Cum* means "with." KUM, u as in "put." *Amīcus* is "friend." "With a friend"? | *Cum amīcō.* a-MEE-koh. |
| "With friends"? | *Cum amīcīs.* |
| "With the girls"? | *Cum puellīs.* |
| Romans drank wine mixed with water. "Wine with water"? | *Vīnum cum aquā.* |
| *Sine* means "without." SI-ne. "Without money"? | *Sine pecūniā.* |
| "Sinecure" is English for a cushy job. Latin *sine cūrā*: what's it say? | "Without care": first a church post with no care of souls, now any job with nothing to worry about. |
| "Bread without cheese? No!" | *Pānis sine cāseō? Minimē!* |
| "Come with me!" Romans glued *cum* onto the end of "me": *Venī mēcum!* "With you"? | *Tēcum.* Treat *mēcum, tēcum* as chunks for now; Book II explains the glue. |
| Count the little words that pull the ablative. | Five: *in* (where), *ex/ē* and *ab/ā* (from), *cum* (with), *sine* (without). |

!!! success "The Law of the Little Words"
    Five prepositions take the **ablative**:

    | word | means | example |
    |---|---|---|
    | *in* | in, on | *in culīnā* |
    | *ex, ē* | out of | *ex hortō, ē vīllā* |
    | *ab, ā* | away from | *ab ātriō, ā forō* |
    | *cum* | with | *cum amīcīs* |
    | *sine* | without | *sine cāseō* |

    Where you are, where you come from, who's with you, what's missing: the ablative.

## 8. Lectiō: *Ubi est cāseus?*

:: Ubi est cāseus? || Where's the cheese?

Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English.

::: dialogue lectio
Iūlia | Iōannēs, ubi est cāseus? || Iūlia | John, where's the cheese?
Iōannēs | Cāseus? In mēnsā est. || John | The cheese? It's on the table.
Iūlia | In mēnsā nōn est! Ecce, mēnsa vacua est. || Iūlia | It's not on the table! Look, the table's empty.
Iōannēs | Fortasse in culīnā est. || John | Maybe it's in the kitchen.
Iūlia | In culīnā nōn est. Et ubi est Marcus? || Iūlia | It's not in the kitchen. And where's Marcus?
Iōannēs | Nesciō. Fortasse in hortō. || John | I don't know. In the garden, maybe.
Iūlia | Venī mēcum! || Iūlia | Come with me!
Iōannēs | Ūvae in hortō sunt, sed nōn Marcus. || John | There are grapes in the garden, but no Marcus.
Iūlia | In ātriō? In cubiculō? Ubi est? || Iūlia | In the front hall? In the bedroom? Where is he?
Cornēlia | Salvēte! Ā forō veniō. || Cornēlia | Hi, y'all! I'm coming from the forum.
Iūlia | Salvē, Cornēlia! Vidēsne Marcum? || Iūlia | Hi, Cornēlia! Do you see Marcus?
Cornēlia | Ita. Marcus in viā est, cum amīcīs. || Cornēlia | Yes. Marcus is in the street, with friends.
Marcus | Salvēte! Ex oppidō venīmus. || Marcus | Hi, everyone! We're coming from town.
Iūlia | Ubi est cāseus? || Iūlia | Where is the cheese?
Marcus | Cāseus? In stomachō est! || Marcus | The cheese? It's in my stomach!
Iōannēs | Rīdeō! Cēna sine cāseō! || John | I'm laughing! Dinner without cheese!
Marcus | Nōn sine cāseō: amīcī cāseum ē tabernā portant. || Marcus | Not without cheese: my friends are bringing cheese from the shop.
Iūlia | Bene! Cēna cum cāseō et cum amīcīs. Optima est! || Iūlia | Good! Dinner with cheese and with friends. It's the best!
:::

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *In mēnsā est.* Why *mēnsā*, not *mēnsa*? | After *in*: ablative, long ā. |
| *Ecce, mēnsa vacua est.* Why short *mēnsa* now? | No preposition: the table is the subject, so it's nominative. |
| *Ā forō veniō.* Why the short *ā*? | *Forō* starts with a consonant, where *ā* is the usual form. (Before a vowel it would have to be *ab*.) |
| *Ex oppidō venīmus.* Could Marcus say *ē oppidō*? | No: *oppidō* starts with a vowel, so only *ex*. |
| *Cāseus? In stomachō est!* So *ubi est cāseus?* | In Marcus's stomach. Case closed. (*Stomachus* is good Latin; Cicero uses it.) |
| *Cēna cum cāseō et cum amīcīs.* Two ablatives. Find their endings. | *Cāseō* (long -ō, one cheese), *amīcīs* (long -īs, many friends). |
| Sort the Lectiō's ablatives: where, from where, with or without? | Where: *in mēnsā, in culīnā, in hortō, in ātriō, in cubiculō, in viā, in stomachō.* From: *ā forō, ex oppidō, ē tabernā.* With, without: *mēcum, cum amīcīs, cum cāseō, sine cāseō.* |

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking.

1. Walk through your house and say where things are: *Mēnsa in culīnā est. Liber in mēnsā est. Ego in cubiculō sum.*
2. Ask and answer: *Ubi est cāseus? In mēnsā est. · Ubi sunt pōcula? In culīnā sunt.*
3. Long or short, out loud, five times each: *mēnsa / in mēnsā · culīna / in culīnā · via / in viā.*
4. Singular to plural: *in hortō → in hortīs · in tabernā → in tabernīs · cum amīcō → cum amīcīs.*
5. *Ē* or *ex*, *ā* or *ab*: *ex ātriō, ē culīnā, ab oppidō, ā forō.* Make up two more of each.
6. *Unde venīs?* Answer three ways: from the kitchen, from the shop, from town.
7. Invite someone: *Venī mēcum!* Then: *Cum amīcīs edō. Sine cāseō? Minimē!*
8. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing all four parts. Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
