# Caput V: Ego et Tū

*Liber I, Prīma Verba · Chapter 5*

*In which you finally get to talk about yourself. Marcus has been doing all the eating; now it's your turn. We meet "I," "you," "we," and "y'all," the six endings that carry them, and the little verb "to be" in all six persons.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Ego sum Iōannēs.* · *Quid agis? Valeō.* · *Cantāmus, sed tū male cantās!*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** person, number (singular, plural), conjugation, pronoun, emphasis
    - **Forms:** the six present endings *-ō, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt*; the *ā* family (*amō*) and the *ē* family (*habeō*); *sum, es, est, sumus, estis, sunt*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Six Endings, the Law of the Family Vowel, the Law of the Silent Pronoun
    - **Words:** walking, working, singing, sitting, thinking, fearing, keeping quiet
    - **Reading:** Iōannēs meets Marcus and Iūlia
    - **Speaking:** say who you are and what you do, and ask others

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check.

## 1. Me, for once

:: Dum spīrō, spērō. || While I breathe, I hope. (proverb)

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| In Chapter 4, *Marcus cantat* would mean what? (*cantat*: sings) | "Marcus sings." KAN-tat. |
| Now you sing. Latin: *cantō*. What changed? | The *-at* became *-ō*. KAN-toh. |
| So what does the *-ō* say? | "I." *Cantō* is "I sing" or "I'm singing." |
| Have you said a verb in *-ō* before? | Yes! Chapter 1: *intellegō*, "I understand," and *grātiās tibi agō*, "I give you thanks." |
| *Ambulō.* Guess. | "I walk." AM-bu-loh. Think "ambulance," "amble." |
| *Labōrō.* | "I work." la-BOH-roh. Think "labor." |
| *Spērō* in the motto? | "I hope." Every *-ō* in the motto is "I": I breathe, I hope. |
| Now *you* sing: *cantās*. What's the signal this time? | *-s*. *Cantās* is "you sing." KAN-tahs. |
| You've met that *-s* too. Where? | *Quid agis?* "How are you doing?" *Agis* is "you do." |
| You walk. You work. | *Ambulās. Labōrās.* |
| One person who isn't me or you walks. | *Ambulat.* Same *-t* as Chapter 4. |

## 2. Six people, six endings

:: Amō, amās, amat. || I love, you love, he loves. (every Latin classroom, ever)

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Who can be talking about doing something? | Me (I), you (the one I'm talking to), or someone else (he, she, it). |
| Grammar has a word for those three. What is it? | **Person.** The speaker is the **first person**; the one spoken to is the **second person**; the one spoken about is the **third person**. |
| Is "person" here a human being? | Not necessarily. It just means "which seat in the conversation." A third-person apple can still "be red." |
| And each person can be one or many. The word for that? | **Number.** One is **singular**, many is **plural**. You already met it: *est / sunt*. |
| "We sing" is *cantāmus*. What's the ending? | *-mus*. kan-TAH-mus. |
| "Y'all sing" (you all, more than one of you) is *cantātis*. Ending? | *-tis*. kan-TAH-tis. |
| Ever met a *-t-* for "you all"? | Chapter 1: *salvēte* and *quid agitis?* The *t* in *-te* and *-tis* points at y'all. |
| "They sing"? | *Cantant.* The Chapter 4 *-nt*. |
| Say all six for *amāre*, "to love." | *Amō, amās, amat, amāmus, amātis, amant.* |
| That six-form chant is called what? | The verb's **conjugation**, from *coniugāre*, "to yoke together." Old schoolbooks say "conjugate the verb" for "chant all six." |
| *Labōrāmus.* | "We work." la-boh-RAH-mus. |
| *Ambulātis.* | "Y'all walk." am-bu-LAH-tis. |
| *Spectant.* (*spectāre*: watch) | "They watch." Think "spectator." |
| Watch the a: *amō, amās, amat*. Long or short in each? | Gone in *amō*, long in *amās*, short in *amat*. |
| And in *amāmus, amātis, amant*? | Long, long, short. It shrinks right before *-t* and *-nt*. |

!!! success "The Law of the Six Endings"
    | | one (singular) | many (plural) |
    |---|---|---|
    | **1st person** (I · we) | *-ō* | *-mus* |
    | **2nd person** (you · y'all) | *-s* | *-tis* |
    | **3rd person** (he, she, it · they) | *-t* | *-nt* |

    Chant it: **ō, s, t, mus, tis, nt.**

!!! info "Grammar words, in plain English"
    **Person**: who's doing it, seen from the conversation. 1st = the speaker, 2nd = the one spoken to, 3rd = anyone or anything else.

    **Number**: one or many. **Singular** = one, **plural** = many.

    **Conjugation** (old school: "the verb's chant"): the set of a verb's forms, and also a **family** of verbs that share them.

## 3. The ē family

:: Timeō Danaōs et dōna ferentēs. || I fear the Greeks, even bearing gifts. (Virgil, Aeneid 2.49)

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "He has" is *habet*. "I have"? | *Habeō.* HA-be-oh. |
| What survived that *amō* lost? | The family vowel. *Amō* swallowed its a; *habeō* keeps its e. |
| Is that e long? | No, short: a vowel right before another vowel is short. HA-be-oh, not ha-BAY-oh. |
| You have? | *Habēs.* HA-bays, long ē. |
| We have, y'all have, they have. | *Habēmus, habētis, habent.* ha-BAY-mus, ha-BAY-tis, HA-bent. |
| Same endings as *amāre*? | Yes. Only the family vowel differs: ā vs ē. |
| *Videō?* | "I see." WI-de-oh. Yes: "video." |
| *Timeō* in the motto? | "I fear." It's Laocoön's warning about the wooden horse. |
| You fear. We fear. | *Timēs. Timēmus.* |
| *Sedeō* is "I sit." They sit. | *Sedent.* SE-dent. |
| *Rīdeō?* | "I laugh, I smile." The Ch4 verb, now in the first person. |
| How do dictionaries list these verbs? | By two forms: "I do it" and "to do it." *amō, amāre* · *habeō, habēre*. |
| What tells you the family? | The second form. *-āre* means the ā family; *-ēre* means the ē family. |
| What are the families called? | The *ā* family is the **first conjugation**; the *ē* family is the **second conjugation**. |
| What about the Ch4 *-it* verbs: *edit, bibit, emit*? | A third family, with tricky vowels. Its full set comes in Chapter 6. For now, use them in the third person only. |

!!! success "The Law of the Family Vowel"
    Each family keeps its vowel through the chant:

    | ā family (1st conj.) | ē family (2nd conj.) |
    |---|---|
    | *amō* | *habeō* |
    | *amās* | *habēs* |
    | *amat* | *habet* |
    | *amāmus* | *habēmus* |
    | *amātis* | *habētis* |
    | *amant* | *habent* |

    The vowel is long except before *-t*, *-nt*, or another vowel. In *amō* it disappears.

## 4. To be: *sum*

:: Homō sum. || I am human. (Terence, *Heautontimorumenos* 77)

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| You know *est* and *sunt*. Which persons are they? | Third: "he/she/it is," "they are." |
| "I am" is *sum*. What ending is that? | *-m*, not *-ō*. *Sum* is old and stubborn. |
| "You are" is *es*. Ending? | *-s*. Short e: ES. |
| "We are" is *sumus*. Ending? | *-mus*. SU-mus. |
| "Y'all are" is *estis*. Ending? | *-tis*. ES-tis. |
| Chant the whole thing. | *Sum, es, est, sumus, estis, sunt.* |
| Irregular, but are the endings strange? | Only the first. After *sum*, it's *s, t, mus, tis, nt* again. |
| Say "I am Iōannēs." | *Sum Iōannēs.* |
| Say "You are a student." | *Discipulus es.* (Or *discipula es*, to a woman.) |
| Why *discipulus* and not *discipulum*? | Being isn't doing something *to* anyone. After *sum*, the noun stays nominative, like *Mālum est* in Chapter 2. |
| "We are farmers." | *Agricolae sumus.* |
| "Y'all are sailors." | *Nautae estis.* |
| "Are you good?" (to one, using *-ne*) | *Esne bonus?* or *Bonane es?* (to a woman) |
| What does the motto's *homō* mean? | A human being. *Homō sum*: "I'm (only) human." |

!!! success "*sum*, to be"
    | | singular | plural |
    |---|---|---|
    | 1st | *sum* (I am) | *sumus* (we are) |
    | 2nd | *es* (you are) | *estis* (y'all are) |
    | 3rd | *est* (he, she, it is) | *sunt* (they are) |

    After *sum*, the noun or adjective that names the subject stays **nominative**: *Ego discipulus sum.*

## 5. *Ego, tū, nōs, vōs*

:: Ōdī et amō. || I hate and I love. (Catullus 85)

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Cantō* already means "I sing." So what is *ego* for? | *Ego* is "I." You can add it, but you don't have to. E-go, short e, short o. |
| Ego, egotist, egomaniac. Where from? | Latin *ego*, "I." |
| "You" (one person)? | *Tū*, long ū. You met it in Chapter 1: *et tū?* |
| "We"? | *Nōs*. Long ō. |
| "You all, y'all"? | *Vōs*. WOHS. |
| What are these four words called? | **Pronouns**: words that stand in for a noun. *Prō nōmine*, "instead of a name." |
| If the ending says who, why say *ego* at all? | For **emphasis**, also called stress: leaning on a word so it stands out. |
| *Ego cantō, tū labōrās.* Feel the difference? | "*I* sing; *you* work." The pronouns set the two people against each other. |
| *Tū cantās?* | "*You* sing?" (surprised). Plain *cantās?* is just "you sing?" |
| The motto, *Ōdī et amō*, has no *ego*. Who hates and loves? | "I." The endings carry it (*ōdī* is an "I" form you'll meet much later). |
| What's "he" or "she"? | Latin usually just uses the name, or nothing: *Marcus cantat*, *cantat*. Real he/she words come later. |
| *Ego quoque.* | "Me too." *Quoque* means "also, too," and it follows the word it leans on. |
| *Nōs quoque labōrāmus.* | "We work too." |
| *Vōs cantātis; nōs tacēmus.* (*tacēre*: be quiet) | "*Y'all* sing; *we* keep quiet." |

!!! success "The Law of the Silent Pronoun"
    The ending already says who. Add *ego, tū, nōs, vōs* only for **emphasis**: *Cantō* (I sing). ***Ego** cantō* (*I* sing, not you).

## 6. Asking each other

:: Et tū, Brūte? || You too, Brutus? (Shakespeare, *Julius Caesar*: not a Roman line at all)

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Quid agis?* now makes sense how? | *Agis*: "you do." "What are you doing?" which became "how are you?" |
| *Quid agitis?* | "How are y'all?" *-tis*, more than one of you. |
| Answer *Quid agis?* with "I'm well." | *Valeō.* WA-le-oh. "I am well, I'm strong." |
| Where have you met that verb before? | *Valē!* "Be well!" = goodbye. Same verb, now in the first person. |
| To two friends: *Quid agitis?* They answer. | *Bene valēmus.* "We're doing well." |
| Turn *cantās* into a yes/no question. | *Cantāsne?* "Do you sing?" |
| Answer yes. | *Ita, cantō.* (Latin answers yes by repeating the verb, or with *ita*.) |
| Answer no. | *Minimē, nōn cantō.* |
| *Labōrātisne hodiē?* (*hodiē*: today) | "Do y'all work today?" HO-di-ay. |
| Answer for the group: "Yes, we work." | *Ita, labōrāmus.* |
| *Habitāsne hīc?* (*habitāre*: live, dwell; *hīc*: here) | "Do you live here?" |
| Answer yes. | *Ita, hīc habitō.* |
| *Quis es?* | "Who are you?" Answer: *Sum Iōannēs.* or *Ego sum Iōannēs.* |
| What does the question's verb tell you to answer with? | The matching person. Asked with *-s*, answer with *-ō*. Asked with *-tis*, answer with *-mus*. |

## 7. A day in the first person

:: Cōgitō, ergō sum. || I think, therefore I am. (Descartes)

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Cōgitō* is "I think." Say "you think." | *Cōgitās.* KOH-gi-tahs. Think "cogitate." |
| In the motto, why *sum* and not *est*? | Descartes is talking about himself: first person. |
| I sit and watch. | *Sedeō et spectō.* |
| We stay here. (*manēre*: stay) | *Hīc manēmus.* |
| Think "remain," "mansion." Same root? | Yes: *manēre*. A mansion is where you stay. |
| The teacher teaches. (*docēre*: teach) | *Magistra docet.* Think "doctor," "docent." |
| I teach; y'all keep quiet. | *Ego doceō, vōs tacētis.* |
| Think "tacit," "taciturn." | Both from *tacēre*: silent, quiet. |
| I answer. (*respondēre*) | *Respondeō.* re-SPON-de-oh. |
| You always answer. (*semper*: always) | *Semper respondēs.* |
| We often walk. (*saepe*: often) | *Saepe ambulāmus.* SAI-pe. |
| Today I like apples. | *Hodiē māla amō.* (For "I eat," wait: *edere* is a third-family verb. Chapter 6.) |
| They prepare dinner, and we laugh. | *Cēnam parant, et nōs rīdēmus.* |
| Where does the verb usually go? | Last, like Chapter 4: *Cēnam parant.* |

## 8. Lectiō: A new friend

:: Audī, vidē, tacē. || Listen, look, keep quiet. (medieval proverb)

Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English.

::: dialogue lectio
Marcus | Salvē! Quis es tū? || Marcus | Hi! Who are *you*?
Iōannēs | Salvē! Ego sum Iōannēs. Et tū? || John | Hi! I'm John. And you?
Marcus | Ego sum Marcus. Ecce Iūlia! || Marcus | I'm Marcus. And here's Iūlia!
Iūlia | Salvē, Iōannēs! Quid agis? || Iūlia | Hello, John! How are you?
Iōannēs | Valeō, grātiās. Et vōs? Quid agitis? || John | I'm well, thanks. And y'all? How are you two?
Marcus | Bene valēmus. || Marcus | We're doing well.
Iōannēs | Habitātisne hīc? || John | Do y'all live here?
Iūlia | Ita, hīc habitāmus. Marcus labōrat, ego cantō. Et tū? Quid agis hodiē? || Iūlia | Yes, we live here. Marcus works, *I* sing. And you? What are you doing today?
Iōannēs | Hodiē ambulō et cōgitō. || John | Today I'm walking and thinking.
Marcus | Cōgitās? Ego nōn cōgitō. Ego cēnam parō! || Marcus | Thinking? *I* don't think. *I* make dinner!
Iūlia | Marcus semper cēnam parat et semper cantat. || Iūlia | Marcus is always making dinner and always singing.
Iōannēs | Tū quoque cantās, Iūlia? || John | You sing too, Iūlia?
Iūlia | Ita, saepe cantō. Sed Marcus male cantat! || Iūlia | Yes, I sing often. But Marcus sings badly!
Marcus | Nōn male cantō! Bene cantō! || Marcus | I don't sing badly! I sing well!
Iōannēs | Ego nōn cantō. Timeō! || John | *I* don't sing. I'm scared!
Iūlia | Timēsne? Nōs nōn rīdēmus. || Iūlia | Are you scared? *We're* not laughing.
Marcus | Ego rīdeō! || Marcus | *I'm* laughing!
Iōannēs | Bene! Nunc cantāmus. || John | Fine! Now we sing.
Iūlia | Iōannēs bene cantat. Sed tū male cantās! || Iūlia | John sings well. But *you* (to Marcus) sing badly!
:::

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Quis es tū?* Why the *tū*? | Emphasis: "who are *you*?" Marcus already knows Iūlia; you're the new one. |
| *Quid agitis?* Why *-tis*? | John asks two people at once: y'all. |
| *Bene valēmus.* Who answers for whom? | Marcus, for himself and Iūlia: *-mus*, we. |
| *Marcus labōrat, ego cantō.* Why *ego*? | Contrast: Marcus works, but *I* sing. |
| *Ego cēnam parō.* Which verb, which family? | *Parāre*, to prepare: the ā family. Ch4's *parat*, now first person. |
| *Timēsne?* Take it apart. | *Timē-s-ne*: fear + you + yes/no question. "Are you afraid?" |
| *Nōs nōn rīdēmus.* Why *nōs*? | Iūlia leans on it: *we're* not laughing (Marcus is another story). |
| *Iōannēs bene cantat.* Why *-t* now? | Iūlia talks *about* John, not to him: third person. |
| Find every first-person plural in the Lectiō. | *valēmus, habitāmus, rīdēmus, cantāmus.* |
| Say "Marcus and I live here." | *Marcus et ego hīc habitāmus.* "Marcus and I" is "we": *-mus*. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking.

1. Chant three verbs, all six persons: *amō…*, *habeō…*, *sum…* Then do *cantō* and *timeō* without looking.
2. Narrate your morning in the first person: *Hodiē ambulō. Labōrō. Cōgitō.* Then as "we," with someone you live with: *Ambulāmus…*
3. Ask and answer yourself: *Cantāsne? Ita, cantō.* · *Labōrātisne? Minimē, nōn labōrāmus.*
4. Swap persons: *cantō → cantās → cantat → cantāmus → cantātis → cantant.* Then the same with *sedeō*.
5. Introduce yourself: *Salvē! Ego sum Iōannēs. Hīc habitō. Saepe ambulō. Et tū?*
6. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing all three parts. Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
