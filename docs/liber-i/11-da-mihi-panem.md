# Caput XI: Dā mihi pānem

*Liber I, Prīma Verba · Chapter 11*

*In which the whole family sits down to dinner and everybody wants something handed to them. We learn the verb "give," the little ending that marks who gets the thing, and two ways to say please. Chapter 1's "my name is" finally makes sense. Grandma gets cookies. The boys get… well, you know who gets the cheese.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Dā mihi pānem, quaesō!* · *Marcus Quīntō cāseum dat.* · *Amābō tē!* · *Liber mihi est.* · *Grātiās tibi agō.*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** dative (the "to/for" case); indirect object (the one who receives); dative of possession ("is to me" = "I have")
    - **Forms:** *dō, dās, dat, damus, datis, dant* (short a!); *dā, date*; "to" endings *-ae, -ō* (one), *-īs* (many); *mihi, tibi, nōbīs, vōbīs*; *mē, tē*; all six cases of the 1st and 2nd declensions
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Receiver, the Law of the Look-alikes
    - **Words:** the dinner table: food, napkin, plate, olive, fish sauce, gift; *quaesō, amābō tē*
    - **Reading:** *Ad mēnsam* (dinner with the family, and one piece of cheese)
    - **Speaking:** ask for things politely, hand them over, say thanks

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. This time, *hand things over*: every time you say a receiver, pass something (a pen, a spoon) across the table.

---

## 1. *Dā mihi!*

:: Dā mihi factum, dabō tibi iūs. || Give me the facts, and I'll give you the law. (old legal maxim)

Marcus pānem habet. Iōannēs pānem nōn habet. "Marce, dā mihi pānem!"

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Marcus has the bread. You say *Marce, dā mihi pānem!* Which word is the command? | *Dā*: "give!" A command, like Chapter 6's *venī* and *audī*. DAH, long ā. |
| And *mihi*? You've said it since Chapter 1. | "To me." *Dā mihi*: "give (to) me." Chapter 1's *Nōmen mihi est* had the same word; this chapter finally explains it. |
| Why *pānem*, not Chapter 1's *pānis*? | It's the object: "give me **bread**." *Pānis* is from the third family (Book II), whose object form ends in *-em*. Keep *dā mihi pānem* as a chunk. PAH-nem. |
| Tell two people (y'all): "Give me bread!" | *Date mihi pānem!* Chapter 6's *-te* for many. |
| "I give" is *dō*; "to give" is *dare*. Like *amō, amāre*, it's first family. Guess "we give" and "y'all give" from *amāmus, amātis*. | You'd guess *dāmus, dātis*. Surprise: ***damus, datis***, short a. *Dō* is the one *-āre* verb whose a is short. |
| Chant all six. | *Dō, dās, dat, damus, datis, dant.* Only the one-syllable *dās* (and the command *dā*) hold a long ā. Don't drag the a in *dare* or *date*: DA-re, not dah-re. |
| "Marcus gives bread." | *Marcus pānem dat.* |
| The motto, *Dā mihi factum, dabō tibi iūs.* Find two "give"s. | *Dā*, "give!", and *dabō*, "I will give" (future, Chapter 16; short a again). *Factum*: a deed, a fact (from *faciō*). *Iūs*: right, law. A judge to a lawyer: "Give me the facts and I'll give you the law." |

!!! note "*Dō, dare*: the short-a verb"
    | | one | many |
    |---|---|---|
    | I · we | *dō* | *damus* |
    | you · y'all | *dās* | *datis* |
    | he, she · they | *dat* | *dant* |
    | give! | *dā* | *date* |

    To give: *dare*. Every a is short except in *dās* and *dā*.

## 2. *Marcus Quīntō cāseum dat*

:: Dō ut dēs. || I give so that you may give. (Roman law's formula for a fair swap)

Marcus cāseum habet. Marcus Quīntō cāseum dat. Nunc Quīntus cāseum habet!

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Marcus Quīntō cāseum dat.* Who gives, what's given, who gets it? | Marcus gives (*Marcus*, the subject). The cheese is given (*cāseum*, the object). Quīntus gets it: *Quīntō*. "Marcus gives Quīntus the cheese," "gives the cheese **to** Quīntus." The usual order: giver, receiver, thing, verb. |
| What happened to *Quīntus*? | *-us* off, **-ō** on: *Quīnt-ō*, "to Quīntus." Long ō. |
| Have you seen an *-ō* like that before? | Chapter 7's ablative: *in hortō, cum amīcō*. Same spelling, different job. |
| *Cum Quīntō edō* vs *Quīntō cāseum dō*. How do you tell them apart? | After *cum*, it's the ablative: "I eat **with** Quīntus." No preposition, next to "give": "I give the cheese **to** Quīntus." (No Latin preposition ever takes the "to" form.) |
| "I give the book to the teacher." | *Librum magistrō dō.* *Magister* drops its e: *magistr-ō*. |
| *Dōnum*: a gift, a "given thing" (*dō, dōnum*). "We give Grandpa a gift." | *Dōnum avō damus.* *Avus → avō.* |
| Chapter 8: *Librum ad magistrum portō.* Now: *Librum magistrō dō.* What's the difference? | *Ad magistrum*: carrying it toward him, a place to go to. *Magistrō*: handing it to him, a person who receives. |
| You know five cases. This "to, for" form is the sixth. Its name? | The **dative**. DAY-tiv in English. |
| The motto, *Dō ut dēs.* | "I give so that you may give." *Dēs* is a "may" form of *dō* (subjunctive, Book V). Roman lawyers used it for swaps: I give you my cheese so you'll give me your cookie. |

!!! info "Grammar words, in plain English"
    **Dative**: the "to/for" case, the receiver's form: *Quīntō cāseum dō*, "I give the cheese to Quīntus." The name comes from Latin *datīvus*, "of giving," from *dare*.

    **Indirect object**: the old-school name for the receiver. In "Marcus gives Quīntus the cheese," the cheese is the **direct object** (what's given; Latin accusative) and Quīntus is the **indirect object** (who gets it; Latin dative). English shows it by word order or "to"; Latin by the ending.

## 3. *Aviae crustulum*

:: Aviae crustula damus. || We give cookies to Grandma.

Cornēlia crustulum habet. Cornēlia aviae crustulum dat.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Cornēlia aviae crustulum dat.* Who gets the cookie? What's the ending? | Grandma: *aviae*. **-ae**: *avi-a → avi-ae*, "to Grandma." |
| "Give the doll to the girl!" | *Dā pūpam puellae!* |
| "Marcus gives money to the farmer." | *Marcus pecūniam agricolae dat.* *Agricola* is masculine, but he bends like *puella*: *-ae*. |
| Count: how many things can *puellae* mean now? | Three: "girls" (Chapter 2), "of the girl" (Chapter 10), and "to, for the girl" (now). |
| *Puellae pūpam dō.* Which one? | "To the girl": "I give the girl a doll." Not "girls": *dō* already has its subject (I). Not "of the girl": no thing owned stands next to it. |
| *Pūpa puellae in mēnsā est.* Which one? | "Of the girl": "The girl's doll is on the table." Right after *pūpa*, it says whose. |
| The motto, *Aviae crustula damus.* | "We give cookies to Grandma": *crustula*, the neuter plural object; *aviae*, to Grandma. (Remember Horace's teachers and their *crustula*, Chapter 10.) |

## 4. *Puerīs et puellīs*

:: Nōn nōbīs, Domine, nōn nōbīs. || Not to us, Lord, not to us. (Psalm 115:1; a motto of the Knights Templar)

Avia puerīs crustula dat. Avia puellīs crustula dat. Avia līberīs crustula dat!

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Avia puerīs crustula dat.* How many boys get cookies? | More than one: *puer-īs*, "to the boys." |
| "To the girls"? Guess. | *Puellīs.* Both families use **-īs** for "to many." Long ī. |
| Seen *-īs* before? | Chapter 7's ablative plural: *in hortīs, cum amīcīs*. A new twin. |
| *Cum amīcīs edimus* vs *Amīcīs crustula damus.* | "We eat **with** friends" (ablative, after *cum*) · "We give cookies **to** friends" (dative). |
| "Grandma gives cookies to the children." (Chapter 9's *līberī*) | *Avia līberīs crustula dat.* |
| "To us," "to y'all": *nōbīs, vōbīs*. Hear the *-īs*? | NOH-bees, WOH-bees. *Dā nōbīs!*, "Give (to) us!" · *Vōbīs dō*, "I give (it) to y'all." |
| The motto: which word is "to us," and which is the vocative? | *Nōbīs*, to us; *Domine*, "Lord!" (Chapter 9). The psalm goes on: the glory belongs to God, not to us. Medieval knights took it as a motto. (The Latin Bible numbers it Psalm 113.) |

!!! success "The Law of the Receiver"
    The one who **receives** (to whom, for whom) goes in the **dative**, with no preposition:

    | | one receiver | many receivers |
    |---|---|---|
    | *-a* family | *-ae* (*puellae, aviae*) | *-īs* (*puellīs*) |
    | *hortus* family | *-ō* (*puerō, Quīntō, mālō*) | *-īs* (*puerīs, līberīs*) |

    Carrying something **toward** someone or somewhere still takes *ad* + accusative (Chapter 8): *librum ad magistrum portō*, but *librum magistrō dō*.

## 5. *Mihi, tibi*

:: Hodiē mihi, crās tibi. || Today it's me; tomorrow, you. (old gravestone saying)

Dā mihi! Tibi dō. Dā nōbīs! Vōbīs dō.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Ego* and *tū* (Chapter 5) are subjects. "To me, to you"? | *Mihi, tibi.* MI-hi, TI-bi, with a light h. Say both i's short (poets sometimes stretch the last one). |
| Line them up with *nōbīs, vōbīs*. | One: *mihi*, to me · *tibi*, to you. Many: *nōbīs*, to us · *vōbīs*, to y'all. The plurals end in this chapter's *-īs*; the singulars are their own shape. Learn them as they are. |
| "I give you the cheese." | *Cāseum tibi dō.* (Or *Tibi cāseum dō.*) |
| "Tell me!" "Bring me wine, please!" (Chapter 6's *dīc, fer*) | *Dīc mihi! Fer mihi vīnum, quaesō!* Telling is giving words, bringing is giving things: the listener and the getter go in the dative. |
| *Cēnam tibi parō.* | "I'm preparing dinner **for** you." The dative is also the one you do something for. |
| Chapter 1's motto, *Valē et mē amā*, and Chapter 6's, *Nōlī mē tangere*. What's *mē*? | "Me" as the object: "love me," "don't touch me." Its partner is ***tē***, "you" as the object: *Marcus tē videt*, "Marcus sees you." |
| Sort them: *ego, mē, mihi, mēcum*. | I (subject) · me (object) · to/for me · with me (Chapter 7). And *tū, tē, tibi, tēcum*. The full set waits for Chapter 21. |
| The motto, *Hodiē mihi, crās tibi.* | "Today (it's) to me, tomorrow to you." *Hodiē*: today (Chapter 5); *crās*: tomorrow. A tombstone speaking to the passer-by: my turn today, yours tomorrow. |

## 6. *Nōmen mihi est*

:: Grātiās agimus tibi. || We give thanks to you. (the *Glōria* of the Mass)

Nōmen mihi est Iōannēs. Liber mihi est. Cāseus mihi nōn est!

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 1: *Nōmen mihi est Iōannēs.* Word for word, now? | "A name is **to me**: Iōannēs." *Nōmen* is the subject, *est* the verb, *mihi* "to me." |
| That's a Roman way to say "have": thing + *est* + dative. *Liber mihi est*? | "A book is to me": "I have a book." |
| Say it with *habeō* (Chapter 5). | *Librum habeō.* Same fact. With *habeō* the book is the object (*librum*); with *est* it's the subject (*liber*). |
| "Marcus has a ball." Both ways. | *Marcus pilam habet.* · *Pila Marcō est.* (Many things take *sunt*: *Crustula nōbīs sunt*, "we have cookies.") |
| Chapter 10's *Pila Marcī est* vs *Pila Marcō est*? | "The ball is Marcus's" (genitive: whose is it?) · "Marcus has a ball" (dative: what does he have?). Close cousins; the question you're answering picks the case. |
| *Quid est nōmen tibi?* | "What is the name to you?": "What's your name?" Chapter 1's chunk, now see-through. |
| Grandpa: "I've got no cheese!" Use *est*. | *Mihi cāseus nōn est!* (Or *Cāseum nōn habeō.*) |
| Chapter 1's *Grātiās tibi agō*. What form is *grātiās*? (Chapter 4's *-ās*) | Accusative plural, the object, of ***grātia***, thanks, favor (first family). *Grātiās agō*, "I give thanks," *tibi*, to you: "I give thanks to you." |
| And *grātīs*, "for free"? | A squeezed *grātiīs*: "for thanks (only)," an ablative plural. Done for thanks, not money: free. Thanks are *grātiās*; free is *grātīs*. |
| The motto, *Grātiās agimus tibi.* | "We give thanks to you": *agimus*, "we do, we give." The *Glōria* of the Mass says it to God. |

!!! info "Grammar words, in plain English"
    **Dative of possession**: *est* (or *sunt*) + a dative = "have." *Liber mihi est*, "a book is to me," "I have a book." The thing owned is the subject; the owner is in the dative. Older grammars call it the **possessive dative**. It's why Chapter 1 says *Nōmen mihi est Iōannēs*.

## 7. *Quaesō, amābō tē*

:: Pānem nostrum cotīdiānum dā nōbīs hodiē. || Give us this day our daily bread. (the Lord's Prayer)

Dā mihi cāseum! Quid dīcis? Dā mihi cāseum, quaesō!

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Marcus barks *Dā mihi cāseum!* His mother: *Quid dīcis?* Fix it with Chapter 1's "please." | *Dā mihi cāseum, quaesō.* |
| *Quaesō*, word for word? | "I ask, I beg." KWAI-sō. It can also sit after the first word: *Dā, quaesō, mihi cāseum.* |
| A warmer please: ***amābō tē***. Word for word? | "I will love you." *Amābō* is "I will love" (future, Chapter 16; learn it as a chunk now); *tē*, you (object). Romans used it like "pretty please," "be a dear": *Amābō tē, dā mihi cāseum!* Plautus's characters say it constantly, and Cicero writes it to his friend Atticus. |
| Ask Grandma for a cookie, both ways. | *Avia, dā mihi crustulum, quaesō.* · *Amābō tē, avia, dā mihi crustulum!* |
| *Mappa*: napkin (MAP-pa). Roman guests brought their own to dinner, and Catullus wrote a whole poem (12) against a guest who pinched his. "Please give me a napkin." | *Dā mihi mappam, quaesō.* |
| *Olīva*: olive (o-LEE-wa). *Patella*: dish, plate. "Please give the boys olives." Then "Give me a plate!" | *Dā puerīs olīvās, quaesō.* · *Dā mihi patellam!* |
| *Garum*: fish sauce, the Romans' ketchup. Ask two people for it, nicely. | *Date mihi garum, quaesō!* |
| You get it. Now what? | *Grātiās tibi agō!* (to one) · *Grātiās vōbīs agō!* (to y'all) |
| The motto: find *dā*, *nōbīs*, *pānem*. | "Give us this day our daily bread": *dā nōbīs*, give (to) us; *pānem nostrum*, our bread, the object (*nostrum* matches *pānis*, masculine); *cotīdiānum*, daily; *hodiē*, today. Chapter 9 began this prayer: *Pater noster*. |

## 8. *Sex cāsūs*

:: Deō grātiās. || Thanks be to God. (the Mass)

Puella, puellae, puellae, puellam, puellā, puella!

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Decline *puella*, singular, with the dative in its place: nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, ablative, vocative. | *puella, puellae, puellae, puellam, puellā, puella.* |
| *Hortus*. | *hortus, hortī, hortō, hortum, hortō, horte.* |
| *Mālum*. | *mālum, mālī, mālō, mālum, mālō, mālum.* |
| Plurals: *puella*, then *hortus*. | *puellae, puellārum, puellīs, puellās, puellīs, puellae · hortī, hortōrum, hortīs, hortōs, hortīs, hortī.* |
| Which dative has a form all its own? | None! Singular *puellae* = genitive (and the plural). Singular *hortō* = ablative. Plural *-īs* = ablative. The sentence decides. |
| Chapter 9's *pater, māter* and your *Iōannēs* are third family. "To Father"? | Their "to" forms wait for Book II. Today, say it to their face: *Tibi dō, pater.* |
| The motto, *Deō grātiās.* | "Thanks (be) to God": *Deō*, dative of *Deus* (Chapter 10's *Deī*). The Mass answers it after the readings; monks said it for everything. |

!!! success "The Law of the Look-alikes"
    The dative borrows its spelling: *-ae* (like the genitive), *-ō* and *-īs* (like the ablative). Sort them by their company:

    - after a preposition (*in, ex, ab, cum, sine*): **ablative**;
    - beside a thing owned (*pūpa puellae*): **genitive**;
    - a person (or thing) receiving, told, or helped (*dō, dīc, fer, parō*): **dative**.

!!! success "Sex cāsūs: the first and second declensions, complete"
    **Singular**

    | case | 1st: *puella* | 2nd: *hortus* | 2nd: *puer* | 2nd: *liber* | 2nd n.: *mālum* |
    |---|---|---|---|---|---|
    | nom. (naming) | *puella* | *hortus* | *puer* | *liber* | *mālum* |
    | gen. (of, whose) | *puellae* | *hortī* | *puerī* | *librī* | *mālī* |
    | dat. (to, for) | *puellae* | *hortō* | *puerō* | *librō* | *mālō* |
    | acc. (object; where to) | *puellam* | *hortum* | *puerum* | *librum* | *mālum* |
    | abl. (where, from, with) | *puellā* | *hortō* | *puerō* | *librō* | *mālō* |
    | voc. (calling) | *puella* | *horte* | *puer* | *liber* | *mālum* |

    **Plural**

    | case | 1st: *puella* | 2nd: *hortus* | 2nd: *puer* | 2nd: *liber* | 2nd n.: *mālum* |
    |---|---|---|---|---|---|
    | nom. | *puellae* | *hortī* | *puerī* | *librī* | *māla* |
    | gen. | *puellārum* | *hortōrum* | *puerōrum* | *librōrum* | *mālōrum* |
    | dat. | *puellīs* | *hortīs* | *puerīs* | *librīs* | *mālīs* |
    | acc. | *puellās* | *hortōs* | *puerōs* | *librōs* | *māla* |
    | abl. | *puellīs* | *hortīs* | *puerīs* | *librīs* | *mālīs* |
    | voc. | *puellae* | *hortī* | *puerī* | *librī* | *māla* |

    - **Vocative** (Chapter 9): same as the nominative, except singular *-us → -e* and *-ius → -ī*.
    - **Locative** (Chapter 8): town names and *domus* only: *Rōmae, Tarentī, domī*.

## 9. Lectiō: *Ad mēnsam*

:: Dā mihi cāseum! || Give me the cheese!

Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English.

::: dialogue lectio
Māter | Ecce cēna! Cibus in mēnsā est. Sedēte, līberī. Iōannēs, tū hīc sedē, mēcum. || Mother | Dinner's ready! The food is on the table. Sit down, kids. John, you sit here, with me.
Iōannēs | Grātiās tibi agō. Dā mihi pānem, quaesō. || John | Thank you. Please pass me the bread.
Pater | Ecce pānis. Et aquam tibi dō. || Father | Here's the bread. And I'll give you some water.
Marcus | Pater! Dā mihi cāseum! || Marcus | Dad! Give me the cheese!
Pater | Quid dīcis, Marce? || Father | What do you say, Marcus?
Marcus | Dā mihi cāseum… quaesō? || Marcus | Give me the cheese… please?
Quīntus | Et mihi! Amābō tē, pater, dā mihi cāseum! || Quīntus | Me too! Pretty please, Dad, give me the cheese!
Pater | Cāseus nōn meus est. Māter cāseum habet. || Father | The cheese isn't mine. Mother has the cheese.
Māter | Cāseum puerīs nōn dō. Semper cāseum edunt! || Mother | I'm not giving the cheese to the boys. They're always eating cheese!
Cornēlia | Avia, ecce: crustulum tibi dō. || Cornēlia | Grandma, here: I'm giving you a cookie.
Avia | Grātiās, mea Cornēlia! Bona es. || Grandma | Thank you, my Cornēlia! You're a good girl.
Quīntus | Et nōbīs, Cornēlia? Dā nōbīs crustula! || Quīntus | And us, Cornēlia? Give us cookies!
Cornēlia | Crustula puerīs nōn dō. Crustula aviae et avō dō. || Cornēlia | I don't give cookies to boys. I give cookies to Grandma and Grandpa.
Marcus | Avia, amābō tē… dā mihi crustulum! || Marcus | Grandma, pretty please… give me a cookie!
Avia | Crustulum Marcō dō, et crustulum Quīntō. || Grandma | A cookie for Marcus, and a cookie for Quīntus.
Puerī | Grātiās tibi agimus, avia! || The boys | Thank you, Grandma!
Avus | Et cāseus? Ubi est cāseus? || Grandpa | And the cheese? Where's the cheese?
Māter | In mēnsā est. Iōannēs, cāseum tibi dō: nōn puerīs! || Mother | It's on the table. John, I'm giving the cheese to you: not to the boys!
Iōannēs | Grātiās tibi agō! Ecce, Marce, ecce, Quīnte: cāseum vōbīs dō. || John | Thank you! Here, Marcus, here, Quīntus: I'm giving the cheese to y'all.
Māter | Iōannēs! Nōlī cāseum puerīs dare! || Mother | John! Don't give the cheese to the boys!
Puerī | Grātiās tibi agimus, Iōannēs! || The boys | Thanks, John!
Avus | Et mihi? Mihi cāseus nōn est! || Grandpa | And me? I've got no cheese!
Avia | Ecce, ave: crustulum tibi. || Grandma | Here, Grandpa: a cookie for you.
Pater | Et pānis nōbīs est. Iōannēs, dā avō pānem, quaesō. || Father | And we have bread. John, please pass Grandpa the bread.
Iōannēs | Ecce, ave: pānem tibi dō. || John | Here, Grandpa: I'm giving you the bread.
Avus | Grātiās tibi agō. Sed… amābō tē, ubi est cāseus? || Grandpa | Thank you. But… pretty please, where is the cheese?
Iōannēs | Ecce: cāseus familiae iterum in stomachīs puerōrum est. || John | Look: the family's cheese is in the boys' stomachs again.
:::

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Dā mihi pānem, quaesō.* What does Iōannēs want, and how does he ask? | Bread, with a please: polite from the first line. (Father has to fish for Marcus's: *Quid dīcis, Marce?*) |
| *Amābō tē, pater:* word for word? | "I will love you, Dad": "pretty please." |
| *Cāseum puerīs nōn dō.* To whom, and how many? | To the boys: *-īs*, many. |
| *Crustula aviae et avō dō.* Two receivers, two endings. Why? | *Avia* is an *-a* word: *aviae*. *Avus* is a *hortus* word: *avō*. |
| *Avia, ecce: crustulum tibi dō.* Is *avia* the receiver? | No: *avia* is calling her (vocative). The receiver is *tibi*, "to you." |
| *Mihi cāseus nōn est!* | "I have no cheese," the dative of possession. Grandpa could also say *Cāseum nōn habeō.* |
| *Nōlī cāseum puerīs dare!* Which form of *dō*? | *Dare*, the infinitive after *nōlī* (Chapter 6). Short a. |

## 10. Sound check

:: Dōnā nōbīs pācem. || Grant us peace. (the end of the *Agnus Deī*)

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking.

1. Chant *dō, dās, dat, damus, datis, dant* and *dā, date*, keeping every a short except in *dās* and *dā*.
2. At your next meal, ask for everything: *Dā mihi pānem, quaesō · Dā mihi aquam, quaesō · Amābō tē, dā mihi cāseum!* Then thank: *Grātiās tibi agō.*
3. Hand things around and narrate: *Marcō pānem dō · Aviae crustulum dō · Puerīs olīvās dō · Tibi mappam dō.*
4. Swap one receiver for many: *puerō → puerīs · puellae → puellīs · amīcō → amīcīs.*
5. "Have" two ways: *Librum habeō → Liber mihi est · Crustula habēmus → Crustula nōbīs sunt.*
6. Decline *puella* and *hortus* through all six cases, singular and plural. Then *puer* and *mālum*.
7. Introduce yourself the Roman way, and explain it to yourself: *Nōmen mihi est Iōannēs* ("a name is to me").
8. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing all eight parts. Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
