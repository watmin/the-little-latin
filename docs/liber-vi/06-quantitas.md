*Liber VI, Ad Auctōrēs · Chapter 66*

Back in Chapter 1 you learned where to put the stress (emphasis), and the book said: "The full rule comes back later." Here it is. ## 2. Two lengths

:: Longam esse duōrum temporum, brevem ūnīus etiam puerī sciunt. || That a long (syllable) has two beats and a short one one, even schoolboys know. (Quintilian, *The Orator's Education* 9.4.47)

Ūnum tempus, duo tempora.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Quintilian, about AD 95: *Longam esse duōrum temporum, brevem ūnīus etiam puerī sciunt.* (*Tempus* here: a beat of time; *longam, brevem*: understand *syllabam*.) Translate. | "**That a long syllable has two beats, and a short one one, even schoolboys know.**" A long syllable lasts about **twice** as long as a short one: a slow clap and a quick one. |
| Grandpa's line ends *sub tegmine fāgī*. The *u* of *sub* has no macron: a short vowel. Grandpa claps *sub* slow. Is he wrong? | No. After that *u* come **two consonants**, *b* and then the *t* of *tegmine*. The syllable can't end until the *b* is said, so it takes the time of a long: *sub-t…* A **short vowel** can sit in a **long syllable**. |
| So there are two lengths. Name them. | **Vowel length**: how long you say the vowel itself (the macron shows it). **Syllable length**: how long the whole syllable lasts (its quantity). A long vowel always makes a long syllable; a short vowel can be in either kind. |
| Then do you stretch the *u* of *sub*? | **No.** Say the vowel **short**, as always (the *u* of "put"). Allen and Greenough (§11b): the syllable is long, "but the vowel is pronounced short." The consonants fill the time. |
| How do you mark syllables? | **–** over a long syllable, **˘** over a short one. The cup's old-school name is the **breve** (English "breev," from *brevis*, "short"). A macron sits on a **vowel** in the spelling; a – or ˘ is a mark on a **syllable**. So *sub* has no macron but gets a –. *Sub tegmine*: **– – ˘ ˘**. |

!!! success "The Law of Two Lengths"
    A **vowel** is long or short: the macron says which, and you say it that way.  
    A **syllable** is long or short too: its **quantity**. A long syllable lasts about twice as long as a short one.  
    A long vowel always makes a long syllable. A short vowel can sit in a long syllable (*sub tegmine*): say the vowel short anyway.

## 3. Born long

:: Diphthongī semper longae sunt. || Diphthongs are always long. (teaching line; Chapter 1)

Nātūrā longa.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| How many syllables in *fīlius*? In *mālum*? In *patulae*? | ***fī-li-us***, three; ***mā-lum***, two; ***pa-tu-lae***, three. A word has as many syllables as it has vowels and diphthongs (Allen and Greenough §7). *Ae* is one sound, so one syllable. |
| A syllable with a long vowel (*mā-*) or a diphthong (*-lae, Cae-*): long or short? | **Long**, always. Its grammar name: **long by nature**. Plain words: **born long**. The syllable is long because of what it **is**, not what comes after it. |
| *De-us, me-us, vi-a, fī-li-a.* Look at the vowel right **before** another vowel. Long or short? | **Short**: the *e* of *deus*, the *i* of *via*, the *i* of *fī-li-a*. A vowel before another vowel is short (Allen and Greenough §10a). And *h* doesn't count as a consonant: *ni-hil*, short *i*. |
| Chapter 1's *GRĀ-ti-ās*: why does the stress go back to *grā*? | Because ***ti*** stands before a vowel (*ā*), so it's short, and a short second-to-last syllable sends the stress back. You've followed this rule since day one. |
| *Fīō* (Chapter 32), *diēī* (Chapter 23), *ūnīus* (Chapter 22): a long vowel before a vowel? | **Exceptions**, born long anyway. That's what the macron is for: when the book marks a vowel long, **trust the macron**. |
| Chapter 38: why *laudāns* but *laudantis*, and Chapter 65's *recubāns* but *recubantem*? | A vowel before ***ns*** or ***nf*** is always long: *laudāns, cōnsul, īnferō*. A vowel before ***nt*** or ***nd*** is regularly short: *laudantis, amandus* (Allen and Greenough §10d–e). That's why Chapter 61 said the vowel before *-nd-* is short. |

!!! success "The Law of Born Long"
    A syllable with a **long vowel** or a **diphthong** is **long by nature**: *mā-ter, Cae-sar, pa-tu-lae*.  
    A vowel **before another vowel** is short (*de-us, fī-li-a, GRĀ-ti-ās*); *h* doesn't count (*ni-hil*). A few words are long anyway (*fīō, diēī, ūnīus*): trust the macron.  
    A vowel before ***ns, nf*** is long (*laudāns*); before ***nt, nd***, short (*laudantis, amandus*).

## 4. Long by its neighbors

:: Duae cōnsonantēs syllabam longam faciunt. || Two consonants make a syllable long. (teaching line)

Positiōne longa.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Est, ter-ra, sax-um.* The vowels are short. The syllables *est, ter-, sax-*? | **Long.** After the short vowel come **two consonants**: *st*, *rr*, and *x*, which is two sounds in one letter (*ks*); *z* is double too. Grammar name: **long by position**. Plain words: **long by its neighbors**. ("Position" here isn't where the syllable stands in the word. It's about what stands **after its vowel**.) |
| Chapter 1's *Iōannēs*, yō-AN-nēs. Why lean on *an*? | ***Nn***: two consonants, so *an* is long by position, and a long second-to-last syllable takes the stress. The same in *po-TES-tis* (Chapter 18), *me-mi-NIS-se* (Chapter 46), *kwa-drah-GIN-tah* (Chapter 45). |
| Chapter 4: *esse* "to be," *ēsse* "to eat." Is the first syllable long in both? | **Yes, both**: *es-se* by position (*ss*), *ēs-se* by nature. Same **syllable** length, different **vowel**: ES-se with the short *e* of "pet," AYS-se with a long *ē*. "Grandpa is" or "Grandpa eats": the macron still matters. |
| *Aliquis* (someone): *a-li-quis*. Are *q* and *u* two consonants after the *i*? | **No.** *Qu* is **one** sound, *kw* (Allen and Greenough §11, note 3). So *li* is short, and the stress goes back: **A-li-kwis**. Not a-LIK-wis. |
| *Sub tegmine*: the *b* is in one word, the *t* in the next. Does it still count? | **In verse, yes.** Consonants count across the gap between words, so *sub* is long before *tegmine*. The **stress** doesn't care: it works inside one word. |
| And a word ending in a consonant before a word starting with a vowel? Vergil's *meditāris avēnā*. | The consonant **slides over** to the next vowel: *me-di-tā-ri-sa-vē-nā* (Allen and Greenough §11, note 2). So *ris* is **short** there: one short *i*, one consonant after it. (Alone, a last syllable like *-ris* or *-us* counts short too; in a line, the next word decides.) |

!!! success "The Law of Long by Position"
    A short vowel followed by **two consonants** (or by ***x*** or ***z***) makes a syllable **long by position**: *est, ter-ra, sax-um, pu-el-la*. Say the vowel short.  
    ***Qu*** is one consonant (*A-li-quis*); ***h*** is no consonant at all (*ni-hil*).  
    In verse the consonants of the **next word** count too (*sub tegmine*), and a last consonant before a vowel slides over to it (*meditāri-s avēnā*).

## 5. The either-way syllable

:: Et prīmō similis volucrī, mox vēra volucris. || And at first like a bird, soon a true bird. (Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 13.607)

Modo brevis, modo longa.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Patrem*: after the *a* come *t* and *r*, two consonants. Long by position? | **Not in speech.** A **stop** (*p, b, t, d, c, g*) followed by ***l*** or ***r*** stays together and starts the next syllable: *pa-trem*. So *pa* is **short**. But poets may split it, *pat-rem*, and count it long. Grammar name: a **common** syllable (Allen and Greenough §11c). Plain words: **the either-way syllable**. |
| Allen and Greenough give *alacris* (lively) as A-la-kris. And *tenebrae* (darkness)? Where's the stress? | **TE-ne-brai.** In speech *ne* is short (*te-ne-brae*), so the stress goes back to the third from the end, as in *A-la-kris*. |
| *Fenestra* (window, Chapter 8): is *nes* an either-way syllable too? | **No.** *S* is not a stop, so the *s* closes the syllable: *fe-nes-tra*, long by position. Stress: **fe-NES-tra**. Only a stop + *l* or *r* goes either way. |
| Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 13.607, about AD 8. Memnon's ashes rise from his funeral fire and take shape: *et prīmō similis volucrī, mox vēra volucris.* (*Volucris, -is*, f.: bird; *similis* + dative: like; *vērus*: true.) Translate. | "**And at first like a bird, soon a true bird.**" The ash turns into a bird before your eyes. |
| The beat of Ovid's line makes the *lu* of the first *volucrī* short and the *lu* of the last *volucris* long. How can the same word do both? | ***Cr*** is a stop + *r*: an **either-way** syllable. Ovid counts *vo-lu-crī* short and *vo-luc-ris* long, in one line, as if to show off. (Why the beat needs it: Chapter 67.) |

!!! success "The Law of the Either-Way Syllable"
    A short vowel before a **stop + *l* or *r*** (*tr, cr, br, pl, gr…*) makes a **common** syllable: **short** in speech (*pa-trem, TE-ne-brae, A-la-cris*), but a poet may count it **long** (*vo-luc-ris*).  
    Any other pair of consonants makes the syllable long (*fe-NES-tra*).

## 6. Measuring Vergil's line

:: Tītyre, tū patulae recubāns sub tegmine fāgī. || Tityrus, you, lying back under the cover of a spreading beech. (Vergil, *Eclogues* 1.1; Chapter 65)

Syllabam post syllabam…

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Split Grandpa's line into syllables: one consonant goes with the **next** vowel, two consonants split. How many? | ***Tī-ty-re tū pa-tu-lae re-cu-bāns sub teg-mi-ne fā-gī***: **sixteen**. |
| Mark *Tī-ty-re*. | **– ˘ ˘.** *Tī*, born long (long *ī*); *ty* and *re*, short vowels with one consonant after each. |
| Mark *tū pa-tu-lae*. | **– ˘ ˘ –.** *Tū*, long vowel; *pa, tu*, short; *lae*, a diphthong: born long. |
| Mark *re-cu-bāns sub*. | **˘ ˘ – –.** *Bāns*, long *ā* (before *ns*, Section 3); *sub*, long by its neighbors (*b* + *t*). |
| Mark *teg-mi-ne fā-gī*, and then say the whole line in marks. | *Teg-mi-ne fā-gī*: **– ˘ ˘ – –** (*teg*: *g* + *m*). The whole line: **– ˘ ˘ – ˘ ˘ – ˘ ˘ – – – ˘ ˘ – –.** Clap it: slow, quick, quick… It almost gallops. How it groups into beats is Chapter 67; today, just the marks. |
| Line 2: *silvestrem tenuī Mūsam meditāris avēnā*. Mark it. | ***sil-ves-trem te-nu-ī Mū-sam me-di-tā-ri-sa-vē-nā***: **– – – ˘ ˘ – – – ˘ ˘ – ˘ ˘ – –.** *Ves* and *trem* by position (*s* + *t*, *m* + *t*); *nu* is short (before the vowel *ī*); *ris* turns short as its *s* slides onto *avēnā*. |

## 7. Where the stress falls: the whole rule

:: Est autem in omnī vōce utique acūta, sed numquam plūs ūnā nec umquam ultima. || And in every word there is surely one sharp (syllable), but never more than one, and never the last. (Quintilian, *The Orator's Education* 1.5.31)

Ubi accentus?

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 1: what's the stress? | The syllable you **lean on**: louder, a bit longer, often higher. Also called **emphasis** (your high-school teacher's word) or **accent**. |
| Names for the last three syllables? Take *tegmine*. | ***ne*** is the **ultima**, plain "the last." ***mi*** is the **penult** (Chapter 6), "almost last." ***teg*** is the **antepenult**, "before the almost-last": the **third from the end**. |
| A word of two syllables? | Stress the **first**, whatever the lengths: **SAL-way, FAH-gee, NOH-men, BE-ne.** |
| Three or more, and the penult is **long** (by nature or by position)? | Stress the **penult**: *a-MEE-kus, ku-LEE-nah* (by nature); *pu-EL-la, fe-NES-tra* (by position). |
| Three or more, and the penult is **short**? | Stress the **antepenult**: *DO-mi-nus, PA-tu-lai, TEG-mi-ne, A-la-kris*. |
| Why does the stress move in *PU-e-ree · pu-e-ROH-rum* (Chapter 10) and *KI-ke-roh · ki-ke-ROH-nis*? | The stress can **never** go further back than the **third from the end**, so it follows the end of the word. Add *-ōrum*, and the penult is now a long *ō*. |
| Quintilian says the stress is "never the last." Is that always so? | Almost. A few words that lost an ending keep the old stress: *il-LEEK* "there," from older *illīce* (Allen and Greenough §12). Otherwise, never the last. |

!!! success "The Law of Three Syllables"
    Every word has **one** stressed syllable (stress = emphasis = accent), and never the **last**.  
    **Two** syllables: stress the **first** (*SAL-vē*).  
    **More**: look at the **penult**. **Long** (by nature or by position), stress it (*a-MĪ-cus, pu-EL-la*). **Short**, stress the **antepenult** (*DO-mi-nus*). Never further back than three.

## 8. Long isn't stressed

:: Syllaba longa nōn semper accentum habet. || A long syllable doesn't always have the stress. (teaching line)

Longa an acūta?

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *PA-tu-lae*: is the stressed *pa* long? Is the long *lae* stressed? | **No and no.** *Pa* is short but stressed (the antepenult); *lae* is long but unstressed. **Length** and **stress** are two different things. |
| *Recubāns*: which syllable is long, and which is stressed? | Long: ***bāns***. Stressed: ***re*** (the penult *cu* is short, so the stress goes back to the antepenult): **RE-ku-bahns**. |
| Then where does length decide the stress? | **Only at the penult.** The penult's length is the switch: long, stress it; short, step back one. The other syllables keep their lengths and don't move the stress. |
| English shrinks unstressed vowels to "uh" (ba-NA-nuh). Latin? | **Never.** Say an unstressed long vowel full and long: ***a-MEE-kee***, with the last *ī* as long as the second. Say a stressed short vowel short: ***PA-tu-lai***, not PAH-tu-lai. |

## 9. Tags that lean

:: Ītaliam, fātō profugus, Lāvīniaque vēnit / lītora. || To Italy, an exile by fate, and to the Lavinian shores he came. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 1.2–3; Chapter 45)

-que, -ne, -ve…

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 2 named *-ne* an **enclitic** ("leaning on"); Chapter 28 said *-que* pulls the emphasis onto the syllable before it. The exact rule? | With a tag (*-que, -ne, -ve* "or"), stress the syllable **right before the tag**, long or short (Allen and Greenough §12a): ***de-A-kweh*** (*dea* + *-que*), ***ti-BI-ne*** (*tibi* + *-ne*). The usual rule would have said DE-a-kweh. |
| Vergil's *Lāvīniaque* (Chapter 45)? | **lah-wee-ni-A-kweh.** The stress lands on the short *a* right before *-que*. |
| *Itaque* can be two different words. Which is which? | ***i-TA-kweh*** is *ita* + *-que*, "**and so**" (a tag, so the stress leans on *ta*). ***I-ta-kweh*** is one word, "**therefore**," with the normal rule (Allen and Greenough's own pair). |
| *Arma virumque* (Chapter 28), *Estne?* (Chapter 2), *Vidēsne?*: does the tag rule change anything there? | **No.** *Wi-RUM-kweh*: *rum* is long by position anyway (*m* + *qu*). *EST-ne, wi-DAYS-ne*: the same answer either way. The tag rule only shows when the syllable before the tag is **short**. |

!!! success "The Law of the Leaning Tag"
    When ***-que, -ne*** or ***-ve*** is stuck on a word, stress the syllable **right before the tag**, long or short: *de-A-que, ti-BI-ne, Lā-vī-ni-A-que*.  
    *I-ta-que* "therefore" is one word and follows the usual rule; *i-TA-que* "and so" is *ita* + *-que*.

Iterum audiāmus…

## 11. Which is it?

:: Quid audīs? || What do you hear? (teaching line)

Nātūrā? Positiōne? Brevis?

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *imperātor*: the penult, and the stress? | Penult ***rā***, **born long**: **im-pe-RAH-tor**. |
| *magister*: the penult, and the stress? | Penult ***gis***, **long by position** (*s* + *t*): **ma-GIS-ter**. |

## 13. Traps and fix-its

:: Errandō discitur. || By erring, one learns. (Chapter 23)

Ubi errāvit?

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "*Sub* has a short *u*, so *sub tegmine* starts with a short syllable." | **Long.** The vowel is short, but *b* + *t* follow: long by position. Vowel length isn't syllable length. |
| "*Esse* has a long first syllable, so say it *ēsse*." | **No!** Say the vowel short: ES-se, "to be." *Ēsse* (AYS-se) is "to eat." The syllable's length never changes how you say the vowel. |
| *Aliquis* said a-LIK-wis. | **A-li-kwis.** *Qu* is one consonant, so *li* is short. |
| *Tenebrae* said te-NEB-rai in speech. | **TE-ne-brai.** A stop + *r* goes either way, and in speech it counts short. |
| "*Patulae* is stressed on *pa*, so *pa* must be long." | **Short.** The stress landed there because the **penult** (*tu*) is short. Stress doesn't make a syllable long. |
| *Deaque* said DE-a-kweh. | **de-A-kweh.** A tag pulls the stress onto the syllable right before it, even a short one. |

## 14. Lectiō: *Longae et brevēs*

:: Syllaba longa duo tempora habet, brevis ūnum. || A long syllable has two beats, a short one one. (teaching line; Grandpa, after Quintilian)

Avus plaudit…

Diē trīcēsimā quārtā: the morning after Chapter 65. Yesterday Grandpa wrote Vergil's first line on a wax tablet; the second cheese is still locked in Grandma's chest (*arca*), and the dog lies on top of it. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. Clap the long and short syllables as you go. New in passing: *trīcēsimus quārtus*, thirty-fourth; *plaudō*, clap; *semel*, once; *bis*, twice; *lentē*, slowly; *sequor*, follow; *ergō*, so, then; *ambō, ambae*, both; *eaedem*, the same (feminine plural); *retrō*, back; *saliō*, jump; *duplex*, double; *lātrō*, bark; *equus*, horse; *pēs, pedis*, foot; *dīvidō*, divide; *aperiō*, open; *cauda*, tail.

::: dialogue lectio
Iōannēs | Diē trīcēsimā quārtā Avus tabulam cērātam iterum in mēnsā pōnit. || John | On the thirty-fourth day Grandpa puts the wax tablet on the table again.
Avus | Herī prōmīsī: hodiē dīcam cūr hic versus ita sonet. Audīte! || Grandpa | Yesterday I promised: today I'll tell why this line sounds the way it does. Listen!
Iōannēs | Avus manibus plaudit: semel lentē, bis celeriter. "Tī-ty-re." || John | Grandpa claps his hands: once slowly, twice quickly. "Tī-ty-re."
Avus | Syllaba longa duo tempora habet, brevis ūnum. Hoc etiam puerī sciunt, ut ait Quīntiliānus. || Grandpa | A long syllable has two beats, a short one one. Even schoolboys know this, as Quintilian says.
Quīntus | Sed "sub" vōcālem brevem habet! Cūr syllaba longa est? || Quintus | But "sub" has a short vowel! Why is the syllable long?
Cornēlia | Quia duae cōnsonantēs sequuntur: b et t. "Sub-teg…" || Cornelia | Because two consonants follow: b and t. "Sub-teg…"
Avus | Rēctē. Haec syllaba positiōne longa est, nōn nātūrā. || Grandpa | Right. This syllable is long by position, not by nature.
Marcus | Ergō in "est" et "ēst" ambae syllabae longae sunt? || Marcus | So in "est" and "ēst" both syllables are long?
Avia | Ambae longae, sed nōn eaedem. "Avus est" et "Avus ēst." Et Avus semper ēst! || Grandma | Both long, but not the same. "Grandpa is" and "Grandpa eats." And Grandpa is always eating!
Avus | Ēst, quia ēsurit. || Grandpa | He eats because he's hungry.
Quīntus | Nunc ego: "ca-SĒ-us"! Cāseum volō! || Quintus | Now me: "ca-SAY-us"! I want cheese!
Cornēlia | Errāvistī! Sī in scaenā essēs, theātra tōta exclāmārent! || Cornelia | You got it wrong! If you were on stage, whole theaters would shout!
Avus | "E" ante "u" brevis est; ergō accentus retrō salit: "CĀ-se-us." || Grandpa | "E" before "u" is short; so the stress jumps back: "KAH-se-us."
Marcus | Et "Iō-AN-nēs"? Cūr "an"? || Marcus | And "Iō-AN-nēs"? Why "an"?
Iōannēs | "N" enim duplex est: "an" positiōne longa est et accentum habet. || John | For the n is double: "an" is long by position and has the stress.
Iōannēs | Canis in arcā recubāns lātrat: "Bau! Bau!" || John | The dog, lying on the chest, barks: "Bow! Wow!"
Cornēlia | Duae syllabae longae: diphthongī enim sunt! || Cornelia | Two long syllables: for they're diphthongs!
Quīntus | "Aviaque canisque!" Ubi accentus? || Quintus | "Grandma and the dog!" Where's the stress?
Avus | Semper ante "-que": "a-vi-A-que, ca-NIS-que." || Grandpa | Always before "-que": "a-wi-A-kweh, ka-NIS-kweh."
Avia | Itaque, Ave, cāseum hodiē nōn edēs. || Grandma | Therefore, Grandpa, you won't eat the cheese today.
Avus | "I-ta-que": "therefore." Rēctē dīcis, Avia… ēheu. || Grandpa | "I-ta-kweh": "therefore." You say it right, Grandma… alas.
Iōannēs | Tum Avus versum tōtum dīcit et plaudit, et nōs omnēs cum eō: longa, brevis, brevis… || John | Then Grandpa says the whole line and claps it, and all of us with him: long, short, short…
Marcus | Audīte! Versus currit ut equus! || Marcus | Listen! The line runs like a horse!
Avus | Rēctē. Crās "Arma virumque canō" canēmus, et versum in pedēs dīvidēmus. || Grandpa | Right. Tomorrow we'll sing "Arma virumque canō," and divide the line into feet.
Avia | Et sī versum bene canētis, arcam aperiam. || Grandma | And if y'all sing the line well, I'll open the chest.
Iōannēs | Canis caudam movet. Syllabās nōn nōvit, sed "arcam" bene intellegit. || John | The dog wags his tail. He doesn't know syllables, but he understands "chest" very well.
:::

{#g-14}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Line 4: whose words is Grandpa borrowing? | **Quintilian's** (Section 2): *longam esse duōrum temporum, brevem ūnīus etiam puerī sciunt.* |
| Line 12: *Sī in scaenā essēs, theātra tōta exclāmārent!* What kind of "if," and whose words are inside it? | A **not-really if** about now (Chapter 57): "If you **were** on stage, whole theaters **would** shout." The shouting theaters are Cicero's (Section 1). |
| Line 13: why does the stress of *cāseus* "jump back"? | The penult *se* stands before a vowel, so it's **short**, and a short penult sends the stress to the antepenult: **KAH-se-us**. |
| Lines 18–19: *Aviaque canisque*. Which tag stress is a change from the usual rule? | ***a-wi-A-kweh***: the *a* before *-que* is short, and only the tag rule puts the stress there. *Ka-NIS-kweh* would get it anyway (*nis* is long by position). |
| Lines 20–21: why does Grandpa sigh at Grandma's *Itaque*? | Grandma says ***I-ta-que***, one word, "**therefore**": no cheese today. And she said it correctly. |

## 15. Auctor: Quintilian's rule

:: Ideōque in disyllabīs prior. || And so in two-syllable words, the first. (Quintilian, *The Orator's Education* 1.5.31)

Rēgula Rōmāna.

About AD 95. Quintilian, Rome's great teacher of speaking, is explaining what boys must learn about Latin sounds. Here is his rule for the accent, about 1,900 years older than Chapter 1 (the Latin Library text, with macrons added), one phrase per row. Quintilian uses Greek-style words: the stressed syllable is ***acūta***, "sharp" (a raised voice), and an unstressed one has a ***gravis sonus***, a "heavy," low sound. Scholars still argue how much the Roman accent was pitch and how much loudness; this book keeps Chapter 1's word, **stress** (emphasis). It's the same syllable either way. New in passing: *namque*, for; *vōx, vōcis*, here: word; *acūtus*, sharp; *intrā*, within; *contineō*, hold; *sīve … sīve*, whether … or; *sōlus*, only; *proximus*, next to; *extrēmus*, last; *porrō*, further; *flexus*, bent (a rising-falling tone); *gravis*, heavy, low; *acuō*, sharpen; *utique*, surely; *disyllabus*, of two syllables; *prior*, the first (of two).

::: dialogue auctor
Quīntiliānus | Namque in omnī vōce acūta intrā numerum trium syllabārum continētur, || Quintilian | For in every word the sharp (syllable) is held within the count of three syllables,
Quīntiliānus | sīve eae sunt in verbō sōlae sīve ultimae, || Quintilian | whether they are the only ones in the word or the last ones,
Quīntiliānus | et in iīs aut proxima extrēmae aut ab eā tertia. || Quintilian | and among them it is either the one next to the end or the third from it.
Quīntiliānus | Trium porrō dē quibus loquor media longa aut acūta aut flexa erit, || Quintilian | Further, of the three I'm speaking of, the middle one, if long, will be sharp or bent;
Quīntiliānus | eōdem locō brevis utique gravem habēbit sonum || Quintilian | in the same place a short one will surely have a low sound,
Quīntiliānus | ideōque positam ante sē, id est ab ultimā tertiam, acuet. || Quintilian | and so it will sharpen the one placed before it, that is, the third from the last.
Quīntiliānus | Est autem in omnī vōce utique acūta, || Quintilian | And in every word there is surely a sharp one,
Quīntiliānus | sed numquam plūs ūnā nec umquam ultima, || Quintilian | but never more than one, and never the last,
Quīntiliānus | ideōque in disyllabīs prior. || Quintilian | and so in two-syllable words, the first.
:::

{#g-15}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Rows 1–3: how far back can the stress go, and which two places can it take? | **Within the last three syllables**: either the one **next to the end** (the penult) or the **third** from the end (the antepenult). |
| Rows 4–6: when does the penult take it, and when does it pass it back? | **Long** penult: it is "sharp" (stressed). **Short** penult: it has a low sound and "sharpens" the one before it, the **third from the last**. Chapter 1's rule, in a Roman's words. |
| Rows 7–9: how many stressed syllables per word, and where in a two-syllable word? | **Exactly one**, never more and **never the last**; so in a two-syllable word, the **first**. Chapter 1 said the same. |
| *Acūtus* and *gravis*: do you know these as names of marks? | Yes: the **acute** accent (´, as in French *é*) and the **grave** (`) are named from these words. Quintilian's "bent" (*flexa*) tone is the one the **circumflex** (ˆ, from *circumflexus*, "bent around") stands for. You won't need the marks; the stress rule is all of it. |

!!! success "Quantitās: tua est!"
    You can now measure a Latin word. A syllable is **long by nature** (a long vowel or a diphthong) or **long by position** (two consonants after its vowel, *x* and *z* included, *qu* and *h* not), and before a stop + *l* or *r* it can go **either way**. A short vowel stays short even in a long syllable. Then the stress (emphasis) rule, whole: two syllables, the first; more, a long penult takes it, a short one sends it back to the third from the end; never further, never the last; and a tag pulls it to the syllable right before. You can mark Vergil's first line long and short. Next, Chapter 67 *Arma Virumque*: the family groups those longs and shorts into the beat of Vergil's line, and Grandma's chest may open.
