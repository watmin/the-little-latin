# Caput LXV: Ōrdō Verbōrum

*Liber VI, Ad Auctōrēs · Chapter 65*

*In which Grandpa keeps another promise. Last night he said:* Crās vōbīs versum Vergiliī ostendam, cuius verba nōn eō ōrdine stant quō nōs loquimur. *This morning he writes it on a wax tablet: the first line of Vergil's* Eclogues*, where a shepherd lies under a beech tree and the words for "spreading" and "beech" lie four words apart. Chapter 4 told you the endings say who does what, so Latin can put its words anywhere. Today: why it puts them where it does. The first place is a **spotlight**. Some little words never go first. An adjective can wait several words for its noun (**hyperbaton**, the split pair), and two pairs can mirror each other like the letter X (**chiasmus**, the criss-cross). Then the real skill: reading Latin **left to right**, the way a Roman heard it, without hunting for the verb. The real lines: Caesar, Cicero against Catiline, Quintilian on how to build a sentence, Sallust, Ovid, Horace, Cato's old age, and Vergil's two shepherds. It's the thirty-third day. Speaking goal: put the word you care about in the spotlight, and read a line of Vergil in his order.*

## 1. *Quō ōrdine?*

:: Tītyre, tū patulae recubāns sub tegmine fāgī… || Tityrus, you, lying back under the cover of a spreading beech… (Vergil, *Eclogues* 1.1)

Herī prōmīsit… hodiē scrībit.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Last night Grandpa promised: *Crās vōbīs versum Vergiliī ostendam, cuius verba nōn eō ōrdine stant quō nōs loquimur.* Translate. (*Versus*: a line of verse; *ōrdō, ōrdinis*, m.: order, row.) | "Tomorrow I'll show y'all a line of Vergil **whose words don't stand in the order we speak in**." Today he writes it on a wax tablet. |
| The line, the first of Vergil's *Eclogues* (his shepherd poems, about 39 BC): *Tītyre, tū patulae recubāns sub tegmine fāgī.* (*Tītyrus*: a shepherd; *patulus*: spreading; *recubō*: lie back; *tegmen, tegminis*, n.: cover; *fāgus*, f.: beech tree.) Word for word? | "Tityrus, you, of a spreading, lying back, under the cover, of a beech." *Tītyre*: vocative (Chapter 9). *Recubāns*: present participle, "lying back" (Chapter 38). |
| Which word goes with *patulae*? Look at the endings. | ***Fāgī***, four words later. *Patulae* is feminine: "of" or "to" one, or a plural subject. *Fāgus* is a feminine of the second family (trees usually are), so *fāgī* is "of a beech": *patulae* fits as "of" too. "**Under the cover of a spreading beech.**" Same gender, number and case: Chapter 20's agreement. |
| Chapter 4's Law of Endings over Order: *Puer puellam amat · Puellam puer amat.* What did it say? | **The endings say who does what.** Order doesn't change the meaning. It only changes what gets the weight ("It's the **girl** the boy loves"). |
| So if order isn't meaning, what is it? | Weight and shape: what comes first, what is held back, what goes with what. Grammar books call this ***ōrdō verbōrum***, plain **word order**: where the words stand. Today, the rules of the road. |

## 2. The everyday order

:: Ipse in Ītaliam magnīs itineribus contendit. || He himself hurried into Italy by forced marches. (Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.10.3)

Quis? Quid? Agit.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 4: "What's the usual Latin order?" | **Subject, object, verb**: *Marcus mālum edit.* The verb likes to go last. |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.10.3, 58 BC. He hears the Helvetians are on the move, puts his officer Labienus in charge of the wall he has built, and goes for more troops: *ipse in Ītaliam magnīs itineribus contendit.* (*Iter, itineris*, n.: journey, march; *magnīs itineribus*: "by great marches," forced marches; *contendō*: hurry.) Translate. Where's the verb? | "**He himself hurried into Italy by forced marches.**" (*Contendit* is a historic present, Chapter 54.) Who first (*ipse*), then where and how, then the verb, **last**. That's Caesar's everyday order. |
| Chapter 3: where does an adjective like *magnus* usually go? And Chapter 9's *meus*? | **After** its noun: *mālum magnum, fīlius meus.* Put first, it gets weight: *magnum mālum*, *Meum mālum est!* |
| But *magnīs itineribus* has the adjective first. Is Caesar shouting? | No. Words of **size and number** (*magnus, multus, omnis, ūnus, trēs*) and pointing words (*hic, ille*) like to go **first** anyway (Allen and Greenough §598b). *Magnīs itineribus* is just the way Romans said "forced marches." |
| Grandma's plain order: *Avia canī cibum dat.* Name the parts in order. | Who (*Avia*), to whom (*canī*), what (*cibum*), the verb (*dat*). "Grandma gives the dog his food." Nothing in the spotlight. Just news. |

!!! success "The Law of the Everyday Order"
    The plain Latin order is **who → (to whom, where, how) → what → verb**: *Avia canī cibum dat.* The verb likes the **end**.  
    Adjectives usually **follow** their noun (*mālum magnum*), but words of size, number and pointing usually **lead** (*magnīs itineribus, hic cāseus*).  
    The endings still decide who does what (Chapter 4). The order only tells you **what matters most**.

## 3. The spotlight

:: Abiit, excessit, ēvāsit, ērūpit. || He's gone, he's left, he's slipped away, he's broken out. (Cicero, *Against Catiline* 2.1)

Prīmus locus… clārissimus.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The everyday order: *Avus cāseum ēdit*, "Grandpa ate the cheese." Grandma comes in, sees the empty plate, and cries: *Cāseum Avus ēdit!* What changed? | The **cheese** jumped to the front. "It was the **cheese** Grandpa ate!" Same meaning, but now *cāseum* stands in the brightest place. |
| And Quintus, who saw it happen: *Ēdit Avus cāseum!* | Now the **verb** is first: "He **did** eat the cheese!" (or "He **ate** it!"). A word moved out of its usual place gets noticed. |
| The grammar-book word for this "weight by place" is the word your high-school teacher used for the syllable you lean on (Chapter 1). That word stays with syllables in this book. What do we call the weight a word gets from its place? | The **spotlight**. Plain words: **the weight a word gets from where it stands.** Stress (emphasis) is how loud you say a syllable; the spotlight is where a word stands in the sentence. Two different things. |
| Cicero, *Against Catiline* 2.1, 63 BC. The day after Catiline fled Rome: *Abiit, excessit, ēvāsit, ērūpit.* (*Abeō*: go away; *excēdō*: leave; *ēvādō*: slip away; *ērumpō*: break out.) Where are the verbs, and why? | **Only** verbs, and so all of them **first**: "He's gone, he's left, he's slipped away, he's broken out." Four perfects in a row, no subject at all. What matters is that he's **gone**. |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.2.1: *Apud Helvētiōs longē nōbilissimus fuit et dītissimus Orgetorīx.* (*Nōbilis*: noble; *dīves, dītissimus*: rich, richest; *longē* with a superlative: by far.) Translate. Where's the subject? | "**Among the Helvetians, by far the noblest and richest was Orgetorix.**" The subject comes **last**: Caesar builds the man up (where? how noble? how rich?) and saves his name for the end, like a reveal. |
| Is the last place a spotlight too? | Grammars disagree. Gildersleeve and Lodge (§672) count **the beginning and the end** as the two strong places. Allen and Greenough (§597) say the **first** place is the brightest, and a word put last is really an afterthought. Both agree: the **first** word is the one to watch. Read a last word as **saved for the end**. |

!!! success "The Law of the Spotlight"
    The **first** place in a sentence is the brightest **spotlight**: *Cāseum Avus ēdit!* "It was the **cheese**."  
    A word moved **out of its everyday place** gets noticed: a verb at the front (*Abiit, excessit…*), an adjective before its noun (*Meum mālum est!*).  
    The **last** place can hold a word saved for the end (*… Orgetorīx*); grammars disagree on whether that's a second spotlight.  
    The spotlight is about **where a word stands**. Stress (emphasis) is about **how you say a syllable**.

## 4. Words that never go first

:: Dā, quaesō, mihi cāseum. || Give me the cheese, please. (teaching line; Chapter 11)

Secundō locō…

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Some little words have the opposite habit: they **never** go first. Chapter 46's *"Nōlī," inquit, "obsecrō…"* Where does *inquit* stand? | **Slipped in** after the first word or phrase. Chapter 46's Law of the Slipped-In *Inquit*. |
| Chapter 49: *Gaudeāmus igitur.* Chapter 51: *aut enim dō tibi ut dēs…* Chapter 64: *Canēs enim, quī cāseum amant…* Where do *igitur* (so) and *enim* (for) stand? | **Second.** *Enim* is never first; *igitur* usually isn't. English "for" and "so" go first; Latin's go second. The same goes for ***autem*** ("but, however"), ***vērō*** ("indeed, but") and ***quoque*** ("also," right after its word). |
| And the little tags *-que* and *-ne*: *Arma virumque* (Chapter 28), *Estne mālum?* (Chapter 2)? | They're **glued** onto a word: *-ne* onto the first word, *-que* onto the second of the pair. They can't stand alone at all. |
| The grammar-book name for a word that never goes first? | A **postpositive** (*post* + *positus*: placed after). Plain words: **a second-place word.** (Allen and Greenough §599b–c.) So a second-place word is a signpost: the word **before** it is the first word, often the spotlight. |

!!! success "The Law of the Second-Place Words"
    ***enim, autem, vērō, quoque*** never go first; ***igitur*** usually doesn't. They stand **second** (sometimes third).  
    ***inquit*** always slips in after the first word or phrase; ***quaesō*** often does. ***-que*** and ***-ne*** are glued on.  
    Spot one, and you've found the word **before** it: the first word, standing in the spotlight.

## 5. Little sandwiches

:: Ad maiōrem Deī glōriam. || For the greater glory of God. (motto of the Jesuits, 16th century)

Magnā cum cūrā…

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 35's diplomas: *magnā cum laude, summā cum laude*. Where's the *cum*? | **Between** the adjective and its noun: *magnā cum laude*, "with great praise." *Cum magnā laude* is fine Latin too, but the sandwich is the favorite. Chapter 35's *quā dē causā* is another: "for what reason." |
| Chapter 7's *mēcum, tēcum*: "with me," "with you." What's odd? | The preposition comes **after** and is glued on: *mē + cum*. Quintilian (*The Orator's Education* 8.6.65, about AD 95; Chapter 51) lists *mēcum, sēcum* and *quibus dē rēbus* as two words "turned around" (he calls it *anastrophē*, "a turning back"). |
| The Jesuit motto (not ancient, but you may have seen it on a school wall): *Ad maiōrem Deī glōriam*. What's sandwiched? | A **genitive**: *maiōrem … glōriam* with ***Deī*** in the middle. "For the greater glory **of God**." The outside pair holds its "of" inside, like bread holds cheese. |
| Juno in the *Aeneid* 1.47–48 (Chapter 45): *ūnā cum gente tot annōs / bella gerō.* Find the sandwich and the verb. | ***Ūnā cum gente***, "with one nation" (the Trojans), and the verb ***gerō*** last: "with one nation I've been waging war for so many years." |

!!! tip "The sandwich"
    A short preposition (*cum, dē, in, ad, ob*) often stands **between** an adjective and its noun: *magnā cum cūrā, quā dē causā, quem ad fīnem*. A genitive often stands between a noun and its adjective: *maiōrem Deī glōriam*. Read the outer pair as one phrase, with the filling inside. (Allen and Greenough §598g, §599d.)

## 6. The split pair: hyperbaton

:: Quem ad fīnem sēsē effrēnāta iactābit audācia? || To what end will your unbridled boldness hurl itself about? (Cicero, *Against Catiline* 1.1)

Patulae… exspectā… fāgī!

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Back to Grandpa's line: *patulae recubāns sub tegmine fāgī*. A sandwich holds one little word. What's between *patulae* and *fāgī*? | A whole phrase: ***recubāns sub tegmine***. The pair is **split** wide open, and Tityrus lies in the middle, under the tree, just as the words lie inside the pair. |
| The grammar-book name for two words that belong together, pulled apart? | ***Hyperbaton*** (Greek "stepping over"; the other words step over the gap). Plain words: **the split pair.** Old-school also "separation" or "transposition." Quintilian 8.6.62 calls it *verbī trānsgressiō*, "a word stepping across," and counts it a **virtue** of style. |
| Cicero opens his first speech against Catiline (Chapter 43's *Quō usque tandem…*) with three questions. The third: *quem ad fīnem sēsē effrēnāta iactābit audācia?* (*Fīnis*: end, limit; *sēsē* = *sē*; *effrēnātus*: unbridled, *ex* + *frēnum*, bridle; *iactō*: toss, hurl; *audācia*: boldness.) Find the sandwich and the split pair. | Sandwich: ***quem ad fīnem***, "to what end." Split pair: ***effrēnāta … audācia***, with *iactābit* in between. "To what end will **your unbridled boldness** hurl itself about?" The noun waits for the very end, and the boldness bursts out last. |
| Quintilian 8.6.65 quotes the first sentence of Cicero's *For Cluentius*: *animadvertī, iūdicēs, omnem accūsātōris ōrātiōnem in duās dīvīsam esse partīs.* (*Animadvertō*: notice; *iūdex, -icis*: judge; *accūsātor*: prosecutor; *partīs* = *partēs*, Chapter 64's old spelling.) Find the split pair. Then Quintilian's verdict: *Nam "in duās partīs dīvīsam esse" rēctum erat, sed dūrum et incōmptum.* (*Rēctus*: correct; *dūrus*: harsh; *incōmptus*: unkempt, inelegant.) | "I've noticed, judges, that the prosecutor's whole speech was **divided into two parts**." Split: ***duās … partīs***. Quintilian: "For *in duās partīs dīvīsam esse* was correct, but harsh and unpolished." The split pair isn't a mistake; it's **better style**. |
| Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 1.89, AD 8, the first of the four ages of the world: *Aurea prīma sata est aetās.* (*Aureus*: golden; *serō, satum*: sow, plant; *aetās*: age.) Translate. Which word is in the spotlight? | "**Golden** was the first age that was sown," or plainly "The first age to spring up was the Golden Age." ***Aurea … aetās*** is split, and ***aurea*** stands first: gold in the spotlight. |
| Grandpa tries one: *Magnum Avus cāseum ēdit.* Translate. Where's the spotlight? | "Grandpa ate a **big** cheese!" (or "It was a **big** cheese Grandpa ate.") ***Magnum … cāseum*** split, and the size first. |

## 7. Interlocked pairs and word pictures

:: … saevae memorem Iūnōnis ob īram. || … because of cruel Juno's unforgetting anger. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 1.4)

A, B, A, B…

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Line 2 of Vergil's *Eclogue*: *silvestrem tenuī Mūsam meditāris avēnā.* (*Silvestris*: of the woods, woodland; *tenuis*: thin, slender; *Mūsa*: a Muse, here "a song"; *meditor*: practice; *avēna*: oat stalk, a reed pipe.) Pair up the adjectives and nouns. | ***silvestrem … Mūsam*** (accusative: the song) and ***tenuī … avēnā*** (ablative: the pipe). "You're practicing **a woodland song on a slender pipe**." The pairs interlock: adjective A, adjective B, noun A, verb, noun B. |
| *Aeneid* 1.4, about Aeneas, tossed on land and sea *vī superum* (by the gods' force)… *saevae memorem Iūnōnis ob īram.* (*Saevus*: cruel, savage; *memor, memoris*: remembering, unforgetting; *Iūnō, Iūnōnis*: Juno; *ob* + acc.: because of; *īra*: anger.) Pair them up. | ***saevae … Iūnōnis*** (genitive: "of cruel Juno") and ***memorem … īram*** (accusative after *ob*: "the unforgetting anger"). "**Because of cruel Juno's unforgetting anger.**" And *ob* is tucked in before the last word. |
| Why do the poets love this? | It **ties** the line together: you hold *silvestrem* and *tenuī* in your head until the nouns arrive. Allen and Greenough §598h call it the poets' favorite order: **interlocked** (old-school *synchysis*, "mixing"). |
| Horace, *Odes* 1.5.1, 23 BC. A poem to Pyrrha, a flirt: *Quis multā gracilis tē puer in rosā…?* (*Gracilis*: slender; *rosa*: rose; *in rosā*: "among roses," on a bed of them.) Find the two split pairs. What's in the very middle? | ***multā … in rosā*** (on many a rose) holds ***gracilis … puer*** (a slender boy), and in the very middle: ***tē***, Pyrrha. "What slender boy, among many roses, **(courts) you**…?" (The verb, *urget*, waits for line 2.) The boy wraps around her, the roses around them both: a **word picture**. |

!!! success "The Law of the Split Pair"
    An adjective (or a genitive) whose partner isn't next to it is a **split pair** (*hyperbaton*): ***patulae** recubāns sub tegmine **fāgī***.  
    When you meet one, **hold it** and look ahead for a noun with the same **gender, number and case** (Chapter 20). The words in between are the filling.  
    The first half of a split pair is often in the spotlight (***Aurea** prīma sata est **aetās***). Poets also **interlock** two pairs (*silvestrem tenuī Mūsam meditāris avēnā*).

## 8. The criss-cross: chiasmus

:: Ēsse oportet ut vīvās, nōn vīvere ut edās. || You should eat to live, not live to eat. (*Rhētorica ad Herennium* 4.39; Chapter 4)

A B, B A… X!

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 4's very first motto: *Ēsse oportet ut vīvās, nōn vīvere ut edās.* Write the two verbs of each half in order. | **eat, live · live, eat.** *Ēsse … vīvās* then *vīvere … edās*. The second half is the first half **turned around**: A B, B A. (Chapter 51's Quintilian line makes the same X: *nōn ut edam vīvō, sed ut vīvam edō*, eat, live · live, eat.) |
| Write A B over B A, one pair above the other, and draw lines joining the matches. What letter do you get? | An **X**. The Greek letter **chi** looks like an X, and that's the name: ***chiasmus***, "an X-ing." Plain words: **the criss-cross.** |
| The handbook that gave Chapter 4 that motto (the *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, about 85 BC) uses it to teach exactly this: *commūtātiō*, two opposite thoughts said *ex trāiectiōne*, "by switching places" (a criss-cross of the **same** words; Quintilian's Greek name is *antimetabolē*). Its next example: *Eā rē poēmata nōn faciō, quia, cuiusmodī volō, nōn possum, cuiusmodī possum, nōlō.* (*Poēma, -atis*, n.: poem; *cuiusmodī*: of the kind that; *eā rē … quia*: for this reason … because.) Translate. Find the X. | "I don't write poems because **the kind I want to, I can't; the kind I can, I don't want to.**" ***volō … possum · possum … nōlō***: want, can · can, won't. A criss-cross, and a good excuse. |
| Sallust, *Catiline* 5.4, about 41 BC, on Catiline's character: *satis ēloquentiae, sapientiae parum.* (*Satis* + gen.: enough of; *parum* + gen.: too little of; *ēloquentia*: eloquence; *sapientia*: wisdom.) Translate. Find the X. | "**Enough eloquence; of wisdom, too little.**" ***satis ēloquentiae · sapientiae parum***: amount, thing · thing, amount. The two "things" meet in the middle, side by side. |
| Cicero, *On Old Age* 38, 44 BC. Old Cato (Chapter 62's) speaks: *Ut enim adulēscentem, in quō est senīle aliquid, sīc senem, in quō est aliquid adulēscentis, probō.* (*Adulēscēns, -entis*: young man; *senīlis*: of an old man; *aliquid* + gen.: something of; *probō*: approve of.) Translate. Find the X inside. | "For just as I approve of a young man in whom there is **something of the old man**, so I approve of an old man in whom there is **something of the young man**." ***senīle aliquid · aliquid adulēscentis***: old, something · something, young. (Grandpa likes this one.) |
| Cicero, *Laws* 2.13, on the laws of men: *quae suppliciō improbōs afficiunt, dēfendunt ac tuentur bonōs.* (*Supplicium*: punishment; *improbus*: wicked; *afficiō* + abl.: visit with; *tueor*: protect.) Where are the "wicked" and the "good"? | "…which **visit the wicked** with punishment, and **defend and protect the good**." ***improbōs afficiunt · dēfendunt … bonōs***: people, verb · verb, people. The wicked and the good stand at the two ends, as far apart as possible. Allen and Greenough §598f: "Chiasmus is very common in Latin." |

!!! success "The Law of the Criss-Cross"
    Two pairs in **mirror order**, **A B · B A**, make a ***chiasmus***, the criss-cross: *Ēsse … vīvās, … vīvere … edās*.  
    The same order twice, **A B · A B**, is just a **parallel**: *Fuimus Trōes, fuit Īlium* (verb, name · verb, name).  
    A criss-cross puts two words side by side in the middle and two at the far ends: Latin's way of setting things **against** each other.

## 9. Reading left to right

:: Ōrdine legāmus! || Let's read in order! (teaching line)

Verbum post verbum…

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| A Roman heard a sentence one word at a time; he couldn't skip ahead to the verb. So read Cicero's *quem ad fīnem sēsē effrēnāta iactābit audācia?* the same way. *Quem ad fīnem…* | "**To what end…**" A question has begun. Who's doing what? Not yet known. |
| *… sēsē…* | "…himself / herself / itself…": an accusative *sē*. Someone will do something **to himself**. Hold it. |
| *… effrēnāta…* | "…unbridled…": feminine, and nominative singular (or neuter plural). No noun yet. **Hold it**: we're waiting for a feminine noun, probably the subject. |
| *… iactābit…* | "…will hurl…": the verb, "he / she / it will hurl." So: something unbridled will hurl itself… What? |
| *… audācia?* | "…**boldness**?" Feminine nominative: the partner of *effrēnāta*, and the subject. Everything snaps shut on the last word. "To what end will unbridled boldness hurl itself about?" |
| Now you try, with Grandpa's line again: *Tītyre, tū patulae recubāns sub tegmine fāgī.* What do you know after each word? | *Tītyre*: someone is being spoken to. *tū*: it's "you." *patulae*: something spreading, "of" (or "to") a feminine: hold it. *recubāns*: you, lying back. *sub tegmine*: under the cover… *fāgī*: …of a **beech**, and *patulae* snaps onto it. No rebuilding needed. |

!!! success "The Law of Left to Right"
    Read Latin **in its own order**, one word at a time, as a Roman heard it.  
    At each word ask: **what's its ending, and what is it waiting for?** A nominative waits for a verb; an adjective waits for its noun; a preposition waits for its object.  
    **Hold** the open words, and let each one close when its partner comes. Don't hunt for the verb and rebuild the sentence in English order.

## 10. Old friends, new eyes

:: Omnia vincit Amor. || Love conquers all. (Vergil, *Eclogues* 10.69; Chapter 4)

Iterum legāmus…

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 4's motto: *Omnia vincit Amor.* Subject, object, verb: what order is that? | **Object, verb, subject.** Everything upside down from the everyday order: *omnia* in the spotlight at the front, *Amor* saved for the end. "**Everything**, Love conquers." |
| Chapter 28's first line of the *Aeneid*: *Arma virumque canō, Trōiae quī prīmus ab ōrīs…* *Trōiae* goes with which word? | With ***ab ōrīs***: "**from the shores of Troy**." But Vergil put *Trōiae* in front of *quī*, so the relative isn't even first in its clause. Troy gets the spotlight. |
| Chapter 45's next lines: *Ītaliam, fātō profugus, Lāvīniaque vēnit / lītora.* Find the split pair. | ***Lāvīnia … lītora***, "the Lavinian shores," split by *vēnit* and by the **end of the line**: you wait across the line break for the noun. |
| Chapter 15's Caesar: *Gallia est omnis dīvīsa in partēs trēs.* Why is *omnis* so far from *Gallia*? | A split pair: ***Gallia … omnis***, "Gaul as a whole." *Gallia* stands first, the **topic** of the whole book. Allen and Greenough (§597) read it so: "GAUL, in the widest sense, is divided…" |
| Chapter 26's *Fuimus Trōes, fuit Īlium.* Why the verbs first? | They are the news: "We **have been** Trojans, Troy **has been**." The perfect *fuimus* means "we are no more," so the verb gets the spotlight. (Allen and Greenough §598d uses this very line.) |

## 13. Traps and fix-its

:: Errandō discitur. || By erring, one learns. (Chapter 23)

Ubi errāvit?

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Cāseum Avus ēdit* translated as "The cheese ate Grandpa." | "**Grandpa** ate the **cheese**." Order never decides who does what: *cāseum* is accusative, *Avus* nominative (Chapter 4). The front place only puts the cheese in the spotlight. |
| *patulae recubāns sub tegmine fāgī* read as "of a spreading (one), lying under the cover of a beech": two different things. | **One** thing: *patulae … fāgī*, "of a spreading beech." Same gender, number and case: a split pair. Hold the first half until its partner comes. |
| "For the dog is hungry": *Enim canis ēsurit.* | ***Canis enim ēsurit.*** *Enim* is a second-place word: never first. |
| "With great care": *magnā cum cūrā* corrected to *cum magnā cūrā*, "because the preposition goes first." | Both are right. The **sandwich** (*magnā cum cūrā*) is the Romans' favorite (Allen and Greenough §599d); *cum magnā cūrā* is fine too. Don't "fix" the sandwich. |
| *Ēsse oportet ut vīvās, nōn vīvere ut edās* called a split pair. | It's a **criss-cross** (A B · B A). A split pair is **two** words that belong together pulled apart; a criss-cross is **two pairs** in mirror order. |
| Translating *Omnia vincit Amor* as "All things conquers Love," keeping Latin order in English. | "**Love conquers all.**" English has no endings, so it needs its own order. To show the spotlight, use English tools: "**All things** Love conquers," or "It's **everything** that Love conquers." |

## 14. Lectiō: *Ōrdō verbōrum*

In tabulā cērātā…

Diē trīcēsimā tertiā: the morning after Chapter 64. Yesterday Grandpa and John fetched the seller's free second cheese, Grandma locked it in her chest (*arca*), and the dog seemed to say he'd find it. ## 15. Auctor: Tityrus and Meliboeus

:: Deus nōbīs haec ōtia fēcit. || A god made this ease for us. (Vergil, *Eclogues* 1.6)

Meliboeus… Tītyrus…

About 39 BC. After Rome's civil wars, farms in northern Italy were taken from their owners and given to discharged soldiers. In Vergil's first *Eclogue* two shepherds meet. **Meliboeus** has lost his land and is driving his goats into exile. **Tityrus** has kept his and lies in the shade, playing his pipe; a young man at Rome (usually taken to be Octavian, the future Augustus) let him stay. Here are lines 1–6 in Vergil's own words (the Latin Library text, with macrons added), one line of verse per row. Read them **left to right**. (No scansion: Chapter 67.) New in passing: *Meliboeus*, Meliboeus (vocative *Meliboee*); *patria*, homeland; *fīnēs*, borders, lands; *dulcis*, sweet; *linquō*, leave; *arvum*, field; *lentus*, slow, at ease; *umbra*, shade; *fōrmōsus*, lovely; *resonō*, echo; *Amaryllis, -idis* (Greek accusative *Amaryllida*), Amaryllis, a girl's name; *ōtium*, ease, leisure (poetic plural *ōtia*).

::: dialogue auctor
Meliboeus | Tītyre, tū patulae recubāns sub tegmine fāgī || Meliboeus | Tityrus, you, lying back under the cover of a spreading beech,
Meliboeus | silvestrem tenuī Mūsam meditāris avēnā; || Meliboeus | are practicing a woodland song on a slender pipe;
Meliboeus | nōs patriae fīnēs et dulcia linquimus arva. || Meliboeus | we are leaving our homeland's borders and its sweet fields.
Meliboeus | Nōs patriam fugimus; tū, Tītyre, lentus in umbrā || Meliboeus | We are fleeing our homeland; you, Tityrus, at ease in the shade,
Meliboeus | fōrmōsam resonāre docēs Amaryllida silvās. || Meliboeus | are teaching the woods to echo "lovely Amaryllis."
Tītyrus | Ō Meliboee, deus nōbīs haec ōtia fēcit. || Tityrus | O Meliboeus, a god made this ease for us.
:::

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Find every split pair in lines 1–5. | ***patulae … fāgī*** (1) · ***silvestrem … Mūsam*** and ***tenuī … avēnā*** (2, interlocked) · ***dulcia … arva*** (3, split by *linquimus*) · ***fōrmōsam … Amaryllida*** (5, split by *resonāre docēs*). |
| Line 3: *nōs patriae fīnēs et dulcia linquimus arva.* Read it left to right. | *Nōs*: "**we**" (in the spotlight). *patriae*: "of the homeland"… *fīnēs*: "the borders" (accusative: being left). *et dulcia*: "and sweet…" (neuter plural: hold it). *linquimus*: "we are leaving." *arva*: "**fields**," and *dulcia* closes. "We are leaving our homeland's borders and its sweet fields." |
| Look at the pronouns that start the halves of lines 1–4: *Tū … nōs … Nōs … tū.* What shape is that? | A **criss-cross**: ***tū · nōs · nōs · tū***. "**You** lie in the shade; **we** are leaving; **we** are fleeing; **you** are at ease." The exiles' *nōs, nōs* stand together in the middle; the lucky shepherd's *tū* holds both ends. |
| Line 5: *fōrmōsam resonāre docēs Amaryllida silvās.* Who does the echoing, and what do they echo? | ***Silvās***, the **woods**: *doceō* takes the one taught in the accusative and what he's taught to do in the infinitive ("you teach **the woods** **to echo**"). And they echo ***fōrmōsam … Amaryllida***, the name "lovely Amaryllis." Vergil makes you wait to the **last word** to learn who's echoing. |
| Line 6, Tityrus answers: *Ō Meliboee, deus nōbīs haec ōtia fēcit.* What's in the spotlight, and why? | ***Deus***, first after the address: "**A god** made this ease for us." Before saying anything else, Tityrus names who to thank. (*Ōtia*: a poetic plural, one "ease.") |
| Grandpa's parody in the Lectiō (line 16), *Ō Avia, dea nōbīs haec ōtia fēcit*: what did he change? | ***Meliboee*** → ***Avia***, and ***deus*** → ***dea***, a goddess. He kept Vergil's order word for word. |

## 16. Sound check

:: Clārē loquāmur! || Let's speak clearly! (teaching line; Chapter 49)

Audī et dīc.

{#g-16}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *ōrdō verbōrum · hyperbaton · chiasmus · postpositīvus · trīcēsimā tertiā*? | OHR-doh wer-BOH-rum · hü-PER-ba-ton · ki-AS-mus · post-po-si-TEE-wus · tree-KAY-si-mah TER-ti-ah. (*Y* is a Greek sound, like French *u* or German *ü*; *ch* is a *k* with a puff of breath. English says high-PER-ba-ton and kye-AZ-mus.) |
| *Tītyre · patulae · recubāns · tegmine · fāgī · silvestrem · meditāris · avēnā*? | TEE-tü-re · PA-tu-lai · RE-ku-bahns · TEG-mi-ne · FAH-gee · sil-WES-trem · me-di-TAH-ris · a-WAY-nah. |
| *Amaryllida · Meliboee · fōrmōsam · Orgetorīx · effrēnāta · audācia · Iūnōnis · Quīntiliānus*? | a-ma-RÜL-li-da · me-li-BOY-e · fohr-MOH-sam · or-GE-to-reeks · ef-fray-NAH-ta · ow-DAH-ki-a · yoo-NOH-nis · kween-ti-li-AH-nus. A long penult takes the stress (emphasis); a short one sends it back. |

!!! success "Ōrdō verbōrum: tuus est!"
    You can now read a Latin sentence **in its own order**. The endings say who does what; the order says what matters. The first place is the **spotlight**; the end can hold a reveal. *Enim, autem, igitur, quoque* stand second and point at the first word. A little preposition sits in a **sandwich** (*magnā cum cūrā*). An adjective may wait for its noun across half a line (**hyperbaton**, the split pair), and two pairs can mirror each other in an X (**chiasmus**, the criss-cross). And you don't hunt for the verb: you hold each word until its partner comes. Next, Chapter 66 *Quantitās*: which syllables are long and which are short, so Grandpa can tell you why Vergil's line sounds the way it does.
