# Caput LXII: Carthāgō Dēlenda Est

*Liber VI, Ad Auctōrēs · Chapter 62*

*In which the gerund's adjective cousin finally gets its turn. Chapter 61 ended with a warning: some* -nd- *words* agree *with a noun or stand with* est, *and those aren't gerunds. Today they get a name: the **gerundive**, Latin's "must-be" adjective, "to be ___ed."* Carthāgō dēlenda est, *"Carthage must be destroyed," is the most famous one, and you'll find out what old Cato really said (not quite that). It pays off lines you've carried since Book I: Chapter 3's* Dē gustibus … nōn est disputandum, *Chapter 14's* Ōrandum est, *Chapter 20's* mōnstrum horrendum, *Chapter 60's* iūs iūrandum. *It gives you the everyday "I have to" (*Mihi eundum est*, "I have to go"), and it explains the swap Romans make after* ad *(*ad librōs legendōs*). The real lines: Caesar with everything to do at once, Horace's "Now we must drink," Juvenal's prayer, Cicero threatening Catiline, Nepos's Hannibal fighting by cunning, and Pliny's story of Cato's fig. At home it's the thirtieth day: Grandpa reads Nepos as he promised, Grandma guards yesterday's new cheese, and the senate of the family votes. Speaking goal: say what must be done, what you have to do, and what is not to be touched.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Carthāgō dēlenda est · Mihi liber legendus est · Cāseus nōn edendus est! · Mihi eundum est · Nunc est bibendum · ad librōs legendōs*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** gerundive (the "must-be" adjective, "to be ___ed"; old-school also "future passive participle") · passive periphrastic (the "must-be" pattern: gerundive + *sum*; old-school "second periphrastic conjugation") · dative of agent (the "dative doer": *mihi* = "by me" with a gerundive)
    - **Forms:** *amandus, habendus, legendus, capiendus, audiendus* (all of *bonus, -a, -um*); *-ndum est* with no subject
    - **Laws:** the Law of *-ndus* · the Law of Must-Be · the Law of the Dative Doer · the Law of the It-Must · the Law of the Swap · the Law of the Two *-nd-*s
    - **Words:** *dēleō* · *cēnseō* · *cēterum* · *Catō* · *agenda* · *mīrandus* · *gerundive*, *passive periphrastic*, *dative of agent*
    - **Reading:** *Senātus familiae* (day thirty: Nepos, Cato, and a cheese that must not be eaten) and Pliny the Elder on Cato's fig
    - **Speaking:** say what must be done, what you have to do (*Mihi … est*), and what must not be done

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. When you meet an *-nd-* word today, ask one question first: **does it agree with a noun, or stand with *est*?** If yes, it's today's gerundive, "to be ___ed." If it agrees with nothing, it's Chapter 61's gerund.

## 1. *Carthāgō dēlenda est*

:: Carthāgō dēlenda est. || Carthage must be destroyed. (the slogan as it's quoted today; for Cato's real words, see section 2)

Dēlenda… dēlenda est…

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Today's title: *Carthāgō dēlenda est.* (*Carthāgō, Carthāginis*: Carthage, Chapter 45; *dēleō, dēlēre, dēlēvī, dēlētum*: destroy, wipe out, English "delete.") Translate. | "**Carthage must be destroyed.**" (Or "is to be destroyed.") The city of Hannibal (Chapter 60), and Rome's old rival. |
| *Dēlenda* has Chapter 61's *-nd-*. Is it a gerund? Use the Law of the Missing Nominative. | **No.** A gerund is never nominative and never agrees with anything. ***Dēlenda*** **agrees** with *Carthāgō* (feminine, nominative, singular, *-a* like *bona*) and **stands with *est***. It's an adjective. |
| Grammar books call this verb-adjective the **gerundive**. Plain words? | **The "must-be" adjective**: "**to be** ___ed, needing to be ___ed." *Dēlenda* = "to be destroyed." Old-school name: *gerundive* (Latin *gerundīvum*, the gerund's cousin); some books call it the **future passive participle**. |
| Chapters 37–39 gave you three verb-adjectives: *laudātus* (praised, passive, done before), *laudāns* (praising, active, same time), *laudātūrus* (about to praise, active, after). Which corner is empty? | **Passive and looking ahead**: "to be praised." That's ***laudandus***. The gerundive is the fourth verb-adjective. (Chapter 39 promised this passive partner of *laudātūrus sum* for Liber VI. Here it is.) |
| Chapter 39: *Avus cāseum ēsūrus est.* Today: *Cāseus edendus est.* Translate both. Who does the eating? | "Grandpa **is about to eat** the cheese" (active: Grandpa eats). "The cheese **must be eaten**" (passive: the cheese gets eaten). *-ūrus* looks ahead and acts; ***-ndus*** looks ahead and **receives**. |

## 2. What did Cato really say?

:: Carthāginem dēlendam cēnsuit. || He proposed that Carthage be destroyed. (*On Famous Men* 47.8, about Cato)

Catō… cēnseō… dēlendam…

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Cato the Elder (234–149 BC) is an old friend: *Rem tenē, verba sequentur* (Chapter 26), *Sat citō, sī sat bene* (Chapter 34), his cheesecake (Chapter 47). Long after Hannibal's war, the story goes, he ended every speech in the senate, whatever the subject, with: *Cēterum cēnseō Carthāginem esse dēlendam.* (*Cēterum*: for the rest, besides; *cēnseō, cēnsēre*: think, propose, vote, the senator's word.) Translate. | "**Besides, I think Carthage must be destroyed.**" It's Chapter 42's reported speech: ***Carthāginem*** is the accusative subject, ***esse dēlendam*** the infinitive. The gerundive turns accusative to agree with *Carthāginem*. |
| Did Cato say those words? | **Not that we can show.** None of Cato's speeches with it survives, and no ancient writer gives either *Carthāgō dēlenda est* or *Cēterum cēnseō…* word for word. Both are modern forms (the short slogan took shape around 1800). What the Latin writers who report it do give is a gerundive in reported speech, every one of them. |
| Pliny the Elder, *Natural History* 15.74, about AD 77: Cato, *cum clāmāret omnī senātū Carthāginem dēlendam…* (*Clāmō*: shout; *omnī senātū*: at every meeting of the senate.) Translate. What's missing? | "Since he kept shouting at every meeting of the senate that Carthage **must be destroyed**…" The missing word is ***esse***: reported speech often drops it with a gerundive. *Cum clāmāret*: Chapter 55's *cum*. (Section 15 reads the rest.) |
| Florus, *Epitome* 1.31 (2.15 in some editions), about AD 120: *Catō inexpiābilī odiō dēlendam esse Carthāginem, et cum dē aliō cōnsulerētur, prōnūntiābat.* (*Inexpiābilis*: that can't be appeased; *odium*: hatred; *cōnsulō*: ask someone's opinion; *prōnūntiō*: declare.) Translate. | "Cato, with a hatred nothing could appease, kept declaring that **Carthage must be destroyed**, even when he was asked about something else." *Et* here = "even." That's the "every speech" story. |
| *On Famous Men* 47.8 (a short Roman who's-who from the 300s AD), the motto: *Carthāginem dēlendam cēnsuit.* Translate. | "He **proposed** (voted) **that Carthage be destroyed**." ***Cēnseō*** + accusative + gerundive is the senate's formula: "I move that X be done." |
| Plutarch, writing in Greek (*Life of Cato* 27), gives it as "And it seems to me that Carthage should not exist." So how should a careful reader label *Carthāgō dēlenda est*? | **A modern Latin summary of what ancient writers report**, not a quotation. The grammar is genuine: Pliny, Florus and *On Famous Men* all use the gerundive ***dēlendam***. The exact words are not Cato's. |

## 3. Building it: *-ndus, -nda, -ndum*

:: Agenda, legenda, memoranda. || Things to be done, to be read, to be remembered. (teaching line)

Amandus… habendus… legendus…

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 61's gerund of *dēleō* was *dēlendī*. The gerundive is ***dēlendus, dēlenda, dēlendum***. What's the shortcut? | The **same stem and *-nd-*** as the gerund, then the endings of ***bonus, -a, -um***. The gerund had only *-ī, -ō, -um*; the gerundive has every case, all three genders, singular and plural. |
| Make the gerundive (masculine) of *amō, habeō, legō, capiō, audiō*. | ***amandus · habendus · legendus · capiendus · audiendus.*** The vowel before *-nd-* is **short**, and that syllable takes the emphasis (stress): a-MAN-dus, le-GEN-dus. |
| "a book to be read," "books to be read"? Then the same as objects. | ***liber legendus · librī legendī.*** Objects: ***librum legendum · librōs legendōs.*** It agrees like any *bonus* adjective. |
| "a letter to be read" (*epistula*)? And the neuter plural *legenda*, "things to be read"? | ***epistula legenda.*** ***Legenda*** became English "**legend**": a saint's story "to be read" aloud. |
| English kept more of them. Translate: *agenda* (*agō*), *memorandum* (*memorō*: mention, recall), *addendum* (*addō*), *referendum* (*referō*), *dīvidendum* (*dīvidō*), and the names *Amanda* (*amō*) and *Mīranda* (*mīror*: wonder at). | ***agenda***: things to be done · ***memorandum***: a thing to be remembered · ***addendum***: a thing to be added · ***referendum***: a thing to be referred (to the people) · ***dīvidendum*** → "dividend," a thing to be divided · ***Amanda***: she who must be loved · ***Mīranda***: she who must be wondered at. |
| *Mīror* is a deponent (Chapter 43: passive look, active meaning). Yet *Mīranda* means "to be wondered **at**." What does a deponent's gerundive mean? | **Passive**, like every gerundive: *mīrandus*, "to be wondered at"; *sequendus*, "to be followed"; *hortandus*, "to be urged." Deponents have active meanings everywhere else, but their gerundive is passive. |

!!! success "The Law of *-ndus*"
    | Verb | Gerund (Chapter 61) | Gerundive (today) |
    |---|---|---|
    | *amāre* | *amandī* | ***amandus, -a, -um*** |
    | *habēre* | *habendī* | ***habendus, -a, -um*** |
    | *legere* | *legendī* | ***legendus, -a, -um*** |
    | *capere* | *capiendī* | ***capiendus, -a, -um*** |
    | *audīre* | *audiendī* | ***audiendus, -a, -um*** |

    The gerundive is the gerund's stem + ***-nd-*** + the endings of *bonus, -a, -um*. It's an adjective: it **agrees** in gender, number and case. It means "**to be ___ed**": passive and looking ahead, even for deponents.

## 4. "Must be": gerundive + *sum*

:: Cāseus nōn edendus est! || The cheese is not to be eaten! (teaching line; Grandma)

Legendus est… legendus erat… legendus erit…

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Liber legendus est.* Translate. | "**The book must be read.**" (Or "is to be read," "has to be read," "ought to be read.") A gerundive with ***sum*** says what **must** happen to the subject. |
| Grammar books call gerundive + *sum* the **passive periphrastic**. Plain words? How is it different from Chapter 39's *ēsūrus sum*? | **The "must-be" pattern** (old-school "second periphrastic conjugation"). Chapter 39's **active** periphrastic (*-ūrus* + *sum*) says what the subject is **about to do**. The **passive** one (*-ndus* + *sum*) says what **must be done** to the subject. |
| Grandma: *Cāseus nōn edendus est!* Translate. | "The cheese **is not to be eaten**!" ***Nōn*** + gerundive = "**must not** be ___ed." |
| *Sum* carries the time. "Carthage had to be destroyed." "The cheese will have to be bought." | ***Carthāgō dēlenda erat.*** ***Cāseus emendus erit.*** (*Emō, emere*: buy, Chapter 6.) *Erat* = had to be; *erit* = will have to be. |
| "The books must be read." "The letters must be written." | ***Librī legendī sunt.*** ***Epistulae scrībendae sunt.*** The gerundive agrees with its subject, plural for plural. |

!!! success "The Law of Must-Be"
    **Gerundive + *sum*** = "**must be** ___ed" (the passive periphrastic).  
    *Sum* carries the person and the time: ***legendus est*** must be read · ***legendus erat*** had to be read · ***legendus erit*** will have to be read.  
    The gerundive **agrees** with the subject: *Carthāgō dēlenda · librī legendī · cāseus edendus*. ***Nōn*** makes it "must not."  
    Don't mix it up with Chapter 39: ***ēsūrus est*** = he is about to eat · ***edendus est*** = it must be eaten.

## 5. The dative doer

:: Caesarī omnia ūnō tempore erant agenda. || Caesar had everything to do at one time. (Caesar, *Gallic War* 2.20.1)

Mihi… tibi… Caesarī…

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 30's doer in a passive sentence was *ā/ab* + ablative: *Liber ā mē legitur.* But with a gerundive Romans say: ***Mihi*** *liber legendus est.* Translate word for word, then in good English. | Word for word: "The book must be read **by me**." Good English: "**I have to read** the book." With a gerundive, the doer goes in the **dative**, with no preposition. |
| Grammar books call this the **dative of agent**. Plain words? Does it remind you of Chapter 11's *Mihi cāseus nōn est*? | **The "dative doer"**: the person who has to do it. Like Chapter 11's dative of possession ("there's no cheese **for me**" = I have no cheese), *Mihi liber legendus est* is "there's a book **for me** to read": **I have** a book to read, **I have to** read it. |
| "Grandpa has to buy cheese." "We have to read Nepos." | ***Avō cāseus emendus est.*** ***Nōbīs Nepōs legendus est.*** The doer in the dative; the gerundive agrees with the thing (*cāseus, Nepōs*). |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 2.20.1, 57 BC. The Nervii burst out of the woods while his men are still building camp: *Caesarī omnia ūnō tempore erant agenda: vexillum prōpōnendum … signum tubā dandum … aciēs īnstruenda, mīlitēs cohortandī, signum dandum.* (*Vexillum*: flag; *prōpōnō*: put up; *tuba*: trumpet; *aciēs*: battle line; *īnstruō*: draw up; *cohortor*: urge on.) Translate. | "**Caesar had to do everything at once**: the flag had to be raised … the signal given on the trumpet … the battle line drawn up, the soldiers urged on, the signal given." ***Caesarī***: the dative doer. ***Erant***: past. Every gerundive agrees: *vexillum prōpōnendum* (n.), *aciēs īnstruenda* (f.), *mīlitēs cohortandī* (m. pl.; a deponent, so passive: "to be urged"). Caesar leaves out *erat* after the first. |
| "The money must be given to you by me." *Tibi* already means "to you." How do you show the doer? | ***Pecūnia tibi ā mē danda est.*** When the verb already has a "to whom" dative, Romans use ***ā/ab*** for the doer, so the two datives can't be confused. |

!!! success "The Law of the Dative Doer"
    With a gerundive, the person who must do it goes in the **dative**: ***Mihi*** *liber legendus est*, "**I** have to read the book."  
    English turns it round: "the book must be read **by me**" = "**I have to** read the book."  
    If the verb already has a "to whom" dative, use ***ā/ab*** for the doer: *Pecūnia tibi **ā mē** danda est.*

## 6. "One must": *-ndum est*

:: Nunc est bibendum. || Now we must drink. (Horace, *Odes* 1.37.1)

Ōrandum est… eundum est… bibendum est…

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 14 told you what Juvenal really wrote (*Satires* 10.356): *ōrandum est ut sit mēns sāna in corpore sānō.* Now take apart *ōrandum est*. What's its subject? | **It has none.** ***Ōrandum est*** = "there must be praying," "**one must pray**," "you should pray." A neuter singular gerundive with *est* and no subject: "it must be ___ed." *Ut sit*: Chapter 56's tucked-in command, "(pray) that there be a healthy mind in a healthy body." |
| Chapter 32 gave you *Ītur*, "there's a going." *Eō* has no object, so nothing can "be gone." How do you say "We must go" and "I must go"? | ***Eundum est***, "there must be going," with a dative doer: ***Nōbīs eundum est*** "we must go," ***Mihi eundum est*** "**I have to go**." Verbs with no object (*eō, currō, pugnō, dormiō*) can only make this "it-must." |
| Horace, *Odes* 1.37.1–2, 30 BC, when news came that Cleopatra was dead: *Nunc est bibendum, nunc pede līberō / pulsanda tellūs.* (*Bibō*: drink, Chapter 6; *pēs*: foot; *līber*: free; *pulsō*: beat, strike; *tellūs*: earth, f.) Translate. Which gerundive has a subject? | "**Now we must drink**, now the earth **must be beaten** with a free foot" (now we must dance). ***Bibendum*** is the it-must, no subject. ***Pulsanda*** agrees with ***tellūs*** (feminine): a personal must-be, with *est* understood. |
| Chapter 3's motto: *Dē gustibus et colōribus nōn est disputandum.* (*Disputō*: argue.) Word for word? | "About tastes and colors **there must not be arguing**": "There's no arguing about taste." ***Nōn est disputandum***: the it-must with *nōn*. You've been saying a gerundive since Chapter 3. |
| Nepos, *Hannibal* 10.4. Hannibal faces King Eumenes, who has more ships: *dolō erat pugnandum, cum pār nōn esset armīs.* (*Dolus*: trick, cunning; *pār*: equal.) Translate. | "**He had to fight** by cunning, since he was not equal in arms." ***Erat pugnandum***: the it-must in the past. *Cum … esset*: Chapter 55's "since" *cum*. (His cunning: pots of live snakes thrown onto the enemy decks, and the letter you read in Chapter 61.) |
| Nepos opens his *Hannibal* (1.1): *nōn est īnfitiandum Hannibalem tantō praestitisse cēterōs imperātōrēs prūdentiā, quantō populus Rōmānus antecēdat fortitūdine cūnctās nātiōnēs.* (*Īnfitior*: deny; *tantō … quantō*: as much … as; *praestō* + accusative: surpass; *prūdentia*: good sense; *antecēdō*: go ahead of; *fortitūdō*: courage; *cūnctus*: all.) Translate. | "**It must not be denied** that Hannibal surpassed the other generals in good sense as much as the Roman people surpass all nations in courage." ***Nōn est īnfitiandum***: an it-must from a deponent, passive in meaning. *Hannibalem … praestitisse*: Chapter 42's reported speech. (*Antecēdat* is subjunctive because it sits inside reported speech: Chapter 64.) |

!!! success "The Law of the It-Must"
    A neuter ***-ndum est*** with no subject = "**one must** ___, there must be ___ing": *Ōrandum est · Eundum est · Nunc est bibendum*.  
    Add a dative doer for "I must, we must": ***Mihi eundum est.*** ***Nōbīs dormiendum est.***  
    Verbs with no object (*eō, currō, pugnō*) can only make the it-must. *Nōn est ___ndum* = "there's no ___ing, one must not."

## 7. The swap: *ad librōs legendōs*

:: Ad eās rēs cōnficiendās Orgetorīx dēligitur. || Orgetorix is chosen to carry out these things. (Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.3.3)

Ad legendum librōs… ad librōs legendōs…

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 61: a gerund can take an object, *librōs legendō*. "For reading books" would be *ad legendum librōs*. Romans usually said ***ad librōs legendōs***. What happened? | **The swap.** The object ***librōs*** takes the case the gerund had (accusative after *ad*), and the *-nd-* word turns into a **gerundive agreeing** with it: ***legendōs***. Word for word, "toward books to be read"; in English still "**for reading books, to read books**." |
| "The art of reading books": the gerund way is *ars legendī librōs*. Swap it. | ***ars librōrum legendōrum.*** *Librōrum* takes the genitive, and ***legendōrum*** agrees with it. |
| "Grandpa comes to buy cheese," with *causā*. Not *cāseum emendī causā*, but…? | ***Avus cāseī emendī causā venit.*** *Cāseī* goes genitive, ***emendī*** agrees. Caesar 2.20.1, the men who had gone off *aggeris petendī causā* (*agger*: earth for a rampart; *petō*: go and get): "**to fetch** earth for the rampart." |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.3.1. A few years before Caesar arrives, the Swiss Helvetians plan to leave home: *Ad eās rēs cōnficiendās biennium sibi satis esse dūxērunt.* (*Cōnficiō*: carry out, finish; *biennium*: two years; *dūcō*: here "reckon.") Translate. | "They reckoned two years would be enough for them **to carry out these things**." ***Ad eās rēs cōnficiendās***: *rēs* is feminine accusative plural, so ***cōnficiendās*** agrees. The motto (1.3.3) uses it again for Orgetorix. |
| Nepos (*Hannibal* 8.3) says King Antiochus ignored Hannibal's advice *in agendō bellō* (*bellum agere*: wage war). Gerund or gerundive? | **Gerundive.** ***Agendō*** agrees with ***bellō*** (ablative neuter): *in* + the swap, "**in waging the war**." The gerund way would be *in agendō bellum*. |
| Chapter 61's Nepos 11.3 had *ad irrīdendum eum*, gerund + object, no swap. Now 10.3, a page earlier: *Ad hunc interficiundum tālem iniit ratiōnem.* (*Interficiō*: kill; *-undum* is an old spelling of *-endum*; *ineō ratiōnem*: adopt a plan.) Which one swaps? | ***Ad hunc interficiundum*** swaps: *hunc* is accusative after *ad*, and the gerundive agrees. "**To kill him** he adopted this plan." Both are Latin, but the swap is the usual way, especially after a preposition. *Ad irrīdendum eum* is the rarer kind. |

!!! success "The Law of the Swap"
    When a gerund would take an object, Romans usually **swap**: the object takes the gerund's case, and the *-nd-* word becomes a **gerundive** agreeing with it.

    | Gerund + object | Usual Latin | English |
    |---|---|---|
    | *ad legendum librōs* | ***ad librōs legendōs*** | to read books |
    | *legendī librōs* | ***librōrum legendōrum*** | of reading books |
    | *cāseum emendī causā* | ***cāseī emendī causā*** | to buy cheese |
    | *in agendō bellum* | ***in agendō bellō*** | in waging war |

    English never notices: it says "-ing" + object either way.

## 8. The "to-be" adjective

:: Mōnstrum horrendum, īnfōrme, ingēns. || A monster dreadful, shapeless, huge. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 3.658; Chapter 20)

Horrendum… iūrandum… legendus…

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 20 gave you *horrendus* as "horrible." It comes from *horreō*, "shudder at." What does *horrendus* say, word for word? | "**To be shuddered at.**" Vergil's Cyclops is a *mōnstrum horrendum*, "a monster you have to shudder at." A gerundive can be a plain adjective, with no *est*. |
| Chapter 60's oath, *iūs iūrandum, iūris iūrandī*. (*Iūs*: law, a binding formula; *iūrō*: swear.) Why do both words change? | It's noun + gerundive, "a binding formula **to be sworn**," so ***iūrandum*** agrees with *iūs* in every case: *iūris iūrandī*, *iūre iūrandō*. Hannibal's oath was a gerundive all along. |
| As a plain adjective, the gerundive often means "worth ___ing." "A book worth reading," "a man to be praised," "a thing to be wondered at"? | ***liber legendus · vir laudandus · rēs mīranda.*** |

## 9. "I think it must…"

:: Domum meam relinquendam putārem. || I would think I had to leave my house. (Cicero, *Against Catiline* 1.17)

Putō… cēnseō… esse ___ndum…

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 42's *putō* + accusative and infinitive. Grandpa: "I think the cheese must be eaten." | ***Putō cāseum edendum esse.*** The gerundive agrees with *cāseum* (accusative), and *esse* is the infinitive. |
| Grandma, like a senator: "I propose that the cheese be kept." (*Servō*: keep, Chapter 28.) | ***Cēnseō cāseum servandum esse*** (or just *cāseum servandum*). Like Cato's *Carthāginem dēlendam cēnsuit*. |
| Cicero, *Against Catiline* 1.17, 63 BC. Chapter 57 skipped this line because of its gerundive: *Servī mehercule meī sī mē istō pactō metuerent, ut tē metuunt omnēs cīvēs tuī, domum meam relinquendam putārem.* (*Mehercule*: by Hercules!; *istō pactō*: in that way; *metuō*: fear; *relinquō*: leave.) Translate. | "By Hercules, if my slaves feared me the way all your fellow citizens fear you, **I would think I had to leave my house**." *Sī … metuerent … putārem*: Chapter 57's not-really "if," now. ***Domum … relinquendam***: gerundive in reported speech, *esse* dropped. Cicero's point: so you, Catiline, should leave Rome. |
| Florus goes on: Cato kept declaring Carthage must be destroyed, *Scīpiō Nāsīca servandam*. (*Scīpiō Nāsīca*: a senator, Cato's opponent.) Translate. What's understood? | "**Scipio Nasica (kept declaring) that it must be kept.**" Understood: *prōnūntiābat* and *esse Carthāginem*. ## 10. Gerund or gerundive?

:: Tempus est legendī; liber legendus est. || It's time for reading; the book must be read. (teaching line)

Legendī… legendus… quis est?

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Tempus est legendī* and *Librī legendī sunt.* Same *legendī*. Which is which? | ***Tempus … legendī***: gerund, "time **of reading**," agrees with nothing. ***Librī legendī sunt***: gerundive, agrees with *librī* (nominative plural), "the books **must be read**." |
| *Edendō crēscimus* and *dē cāseō edendō*. (*Crēscō*: grow.) | ***Edendō crēscimus***: gerund, "we grow **by eating**." ***Dē cāseō edendō***: gerundive agreeing with *cāseō*, "about **eating the cheese**" (the swap). |
| *Ad edendum parātus* and *Ōrandum est*. Both end in *-ndum*. | ***Ad edendum***: gerund after *ad*, "ready **for eating**." ***Ōrandum est***: the it-must, a gerundive with *est*. A gerund **never** stands with *est*. |
| So what's the test? | **Does it agree with a noun, or stand with a form of *sum*?** Yes → **gerundive** ("to be ___ed," "must"). No → **gerund** ("___ing"). |

!!! success "The Law of the Two *-nd-*s"
    **Gerund** (Chapter 61): a **noun**, agrees with **nobody**, only *-ndī, -ndō, -ndum*: *ars legendī · legendō discimus · ad legendum*.  
    **Gerundive** (today): an **adjective**, **agrees** with a noun or stands with *sum*, all the *bonus* endings: *liber legendus · Carthāgō dēlenda est · Mihi eundum est · ad librōs legendōs*.  
    English "-ing" for the first; "to be ___ed," "must" for the second (except after the swap, where English says "-ing" again).

## 13. Traps and fix-its

:: Errandō discitur. || By erring, one learns. (Chapter 23)

Quis agit? Quis patitur?

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Carthage must be destroyed": *Carthāgō dēlendus est.* | ***Carthāgō dēlenda est.*** *Carthāgō* is **feminine**: the gerundive agrees. |
| "Grandpa is about to eat": *Avus edendus est.* | ***Avus ēsūrus est.*** *Avus edendus est* = "Grandpa **must be eaten**"! The gerundive is **passive**; "about to" is Chapter 39's *-ūrus*. |
| "I have to read the book": *Ā mē liber legendus est.* | ***Mihi liber legendus est.*** With a gerundive the doer goes in the **dative** (unless the verb already has a "to whom"). |
| "The cheese must be eaten": *Cāseus edendum est.* | ***Cāseus edendus est.*** With a subject, the gerundive **agrees** (masculine). *-ndum est* with no subject is the it-must. |
| "For reading books," Roman style: *ad legendum librōs.* | Better ***ad librōs legendōs***: the swap. (*Ad legendum librōs* isn't wrong, just rare.) |
| "To be destroyed": *dēlēndus.* | ***Dēlendus.*** The vowel before *-nd-* is always **short**, even when the verb has a long one (*dēlēre*). |

## 14. Lectiō: *Senātus familiae*

## 15. Auctor: Cato's fig

:: Tam prope ā moerīs habēmus hostem! || So close to our walls we have the enemy! (Cato, in Pliny, *Natural History* 15.75)

Fīcus… Carthāgō… dēlenda…

Pliny the Elder (AD 23–79) wrote a huge *Natural History* in 37 books. In Book 15, on fruit trees, he reaches the African fig and tells how Cato used one. Here is 15.74 from *namque* to the end and 15.75 up to *raptō*, in Pliny's own words (Mayhoff's text, with macrons and quotation marks added; the opening sentence of 15.74 and the last sentence of 15.75 are left out). The subject of the first lines is Cato. *Adtulit* is Pliny's spelling of *attulit*, and *moerīs* an old spelling of *mūrīs*. The last line has Chapter 59's *quamquam* with Chapter 40's untied pair instead of a verb: *quamquam Catōne … raptō*, "though Cato had been snatched away." New in passing: *namque*, for; *perniciālis*, deadly; *flagrō*, burn; *nepōs, nepōtis*, grandson, descendant; *sēcūritās*, safety; *anxius* + genitive, worried for; *afferō, attulī*, bring; *cūria*, the senate house; *praecox, praecocis*, early-ripe; *prōvincia*, province; *fīcus*, f., fig; *dēmō, dēmptum*, take off, pick; *arbor*, tree; *cōnstat*, it is agreed; *atquī*, and yet; *dēcerpō, dēcerptum*, pluck; *mūrus*, wall; *sūmō, sūmptum*, take up; *Pūnicus*, Carthaginian (Chapter 60); *rapiō*, snatch away.

::: dialogue auctor
Plīnius | Namque perniciālī odiō Carthāginis flagrāns nepōtumque sēcūritātis anxius, || Pliny | For, burning with a deadly hatred of Carthage and worried for his descendants' safety,
Plīnius | cum clāmāret omnī senātū Carthāginem dēlendam, || Pliny | since he kept shouting at every meeting of the senate that Carthage must be destroyed,
Plīnius | adtulit quōdam diē in cūriam praecocem ex eā prōvinciā fīcum ostendēnsque patribus: || Pliny | one day he brought into the senate house an early-ripe fig from that province, and showing it to the senators,
Catō | "Interrogō vōs," inquit, "quandō hanc pōmum dēmptam putētis ex arbore." || Cato | "I ask you," he said, "when you think this fruit was picked from the tree."
Plīnius | Cum inter omnēs recentem esse cōnstāret: || Pliny | When all agreed that it was fresh:
Catō | "Atquī tertium," inquit, "ante diem scītōte dēcerptam Carthāgine. Tam prope ā moerīs habēmus hostem!" || Cato | "And yet," he said, "know (y'all) that it was plucked at Carthage three days ago. So close to our walls we have the enemy!"
Plīnius | Statimque sūmptum est Pūnicum tertium bellum, quō Carthāgō dēlēta est, quamquam Catōne annō sequente raptō. || Pliny | And at once the Third Punic War was taken up, in which Carthage was destroyed, though Cato was snatched away (by death) the next year.
:::

{#g-15}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Find the gerundive. What's left out, and what does it agree with? | ***Carthāginem dēlendam***, *esse* left out (section 2). It agrees with *Carthāginem*, accusative feminine. Of all the old reports, this is the closest to the slogan. |
| *hanc pōmum … dēmptam.* *Pōmum* is neuter (Chapter 2). Why feminine *hanc* and *dēmptam*? | Pliny has **the fig** in mind, and ***fīcus*** is feminine. The text goes by the sense, not the word. (The next line's *dēcerptam* is feminine too.) |
| *Scītōte*: which command? Who gets it? | Chapter 47's **"shall" command**, plural: "**know**, y'all" (*sciō*). Cato says it to the whole senate. |
| What did the fig prove, and what happened next? | A fig picked at Carthage was **still fresh in Rome**: the enemy was only days away. The Third Punic War followed, and ***Carthāgō dēlēta est*** (146 BC). Cato, who died in 149, didn't see it. (Plutarch tells it with Libyan figs that fall from his toga.) |

!!! success "Dēlenda, legenda, agenda: tua sunt!"
    You have both of Latin's *-nd-* words now. The **gerund** is the verb-noun, "\_\_\_ing" (*ars legendī*). The **gerundive** is the verb-adjective, "to be \_\_\_ed": with *sum* it says what **must** be done (*Carthāgō dēlenda est*), with a dative it says who **has to** do it (*Mihi eundum est*), and after *ad* or *causā* it does the gerund's job in Roman style (*ad librōs legendōs*). Next, Chapter 63 *Mīrābile Dictū* meets the last verb-noun of all, the supine.
