# Caput LXI: Ars Legendī

*Liber VI, Ad Auctōrēs · Chapter 61*

*In which the verb finds its last missing piece. Since Chapter 3 you've said* Dīcendō discimus, *"We learn by speaking," at the start of every Speak-it-aloud, and Chapter 26 turned it into* Nārrandō discimus. *Today you take that* -ndō *apart. It's the **gerund**, Latin's verb-noun, English "-ing" when "-ing" is a thing: "the art of* reading*," "tired from* running*," "ready for* eating*." It has four cases and no nominative (the infinitive already does that job), and it's built from the participle you met in Chapter 38. Book VI is called* Ad Auctōrēs, *"To the Authors," so the real lines come thick today: Ovid's* Art of Love, *Ecclesiastes' "a time to be born and a time to die," Caesar's men "eager for fighting" and his weather "fit for sailing," Cicero on the pen, Vergil's Aeneas who can't tell of Troy without tears, and Nepos's Hannibal again. At home it's the twenty-ninth day: Grandpa opens a chest of old books, Grandma comes home with a new cheese, and there's a reading game with a prize. Speaking goal: say "the art of ___ing," "time for ___ing," "by ___ing" and "ready for ___ing."*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Ars legendī · Legendō discimus · Tempus est edendī! · Avus edendī cupidus est · Ad legendum parātus sum · Edendī causā venit*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** gerund (the verb-noun: English "-ing" used as a noun)
    - **Forms:** *amandī, habendī, legendī, capiendī, audiendī* (and *eundī*); four cases: *legendī, legendō, legendum, legendō*
    - **Laws:** the Law of *-nd-* · the Law of the Missing Nominative · the Law of the Four Jobs · the Law of the Two *-ing*s · the Law of Half Verb, Half Noun
    - **Words:** *ars legendī* · *causā, grātiā* (+ gerund) · *ad* + gerund · *in* + gerund · *parātus* · *idōneus* · *studiōsus* · *carmen* · *auctor* · *lēctor*
    - **Reading:** *Ars legendī* (day twenty-nine: a chest of books, a new cheese, and a reading game) and Ecclesiastes 3, *Omnia tempus habent*
    - **Speaking:** say "the art of ___ing," "time for ___ing," "by ___ing," "for the sake of ___ing" and "ready for ___ing"

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. When you meet an *-nd-* word today, ask two questions: **which case is it** (*-ī, -ō, -um*), and **what job does that case do** (of, by, for)? English will almost always say "-ing."

## 1. *Dīcendō discimus*

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking. (the Speak-it-aloud motto of Books I and II)

Dīcendō… nārrandō… errandō…

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| From Chapter 3 on, the Speak-it-aloud sections of Books I and II opened with *Dīcendō discimus.* What does it mean? Which word is "we learn"? | "We learn **by speaking**." ***Discimus*** = "we learn" (*discō*, learn). So ***dīcendō*** must carry "by speaking." |
| *Dīcendō* ends in *-ō*, like *dīcō*. Is it "I say"? | **No.** The ***-nd-*** inside marks a **noun made from the verb**. Its *-ō* is a **case ending**, the ablative of a second-family neuter like *bellō* or *vīnō*. Chapter 35's ablative of means: "**by** speaking." |
| Grammar books call this verb-noun a **gerund**. Plain words? | **The verb-noun**: a verb dressed up as a noun. English does it with "-ing": "*reading* is fun," "the art of *reading*," "we learn by *reading*." Old-school name: *gerund*, from Latin *gerundium*, built on *gerō*, "carry on, do" (Chapter 59's *geruntur*): the "doing" word. |
| Chapter 26: *Nārrandō discimus.* Chapter 23: *Errandō discitur.* Translate both. | "We learn **by telling**." "One learns **by making mistakes**" (*discitur*, "it is learned," Chapter 23's impersonal passive). Two more gerunds in the ablative: "**by** ___ing." |
| Today's title: *Ars legendī.* (*Ars, artis*: skill, art, Chapter 15.) Translate. What has changed in *legendī*? | "**The art of reading.**" Same verb-noun, a different case: ***-ī*** is the **genitive**, "of," like *librī*, "of the book." *Legendī* = "of reading." |

## 2. Building it: *-nd-*

:: Amandō, habendō, legendō, audiendō. || By loving, by having, by reading, by hearing. (teaching line)

-and-… -end-… -iend-…

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Amō, amāre* → ***amandī***, "of loving." Take it apart. | *ama-* (the present stem) + ***-nd-*** + *-ī*. The **a** before *-nd-* is **short**: Chapter 38's sound rule (long before *-ns*, short before *-nt*) works for *-nd* too. Say a-MAN-dee. |
| *Habeō, habēre*: "of having"? | ***habendī*** (ha-BEN-dee). Stem *habe-* + *-nd-* + *-ī*. Short *e* before *-nd*. |
| *Legō, legere*: "of reading"? | ***legendī*** (le-GEN-dee, hard *g*). The third family adds ***-e-***: *leg-e-nd-ī*. |
| *Capiō, capere* and *audiō, audīre*: "of taking," "of hearing"? | ***capiendī, audiendī*** (ka-pi-EN-dee, ow-di-EN-dee). The *-iō* verbs and the fourth family use ***-iend-***. |
| Chapter 38 gave you *amantis, habentis, legentis, capientis, audientis* (the participle's "of" forms). Do you see a shortcut? | Swap ***-ntis*** for ***-ndī***: *amantis → amandī · habentis → habendī · legentis → legendī · capientis → capiendī · audientis → audiendī.* The same stem, the same vowel. If you can say "of a reader," you can say "of reading." |
| Try one: *dormiō*, *currō*, *pugnō*, *videō*. | ***dormiendī · currendī · pugnandī · videndī.*** |

!!! success "The Law of *-nd-*"
    | Family | Verb | Participle "of" | Gerund "of" |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | first | *amāre* | *amantis* | ***amandī*** |
    | second | *habēre* | *habentis* | ***habendī*** |
    | third | *legere* | *legentis* | ***legendī*** |
    | third *-iō* | *capere* | *capientis* | ***capiendī*** |
    | fourth | *audīre* | *audientis* | ***audiendī*** |

    The gerund is the present stem + ***-nd-*** + a case ending. The vowel before *-nd-* is always **short**, and it takes the emphasis (stress): a-MAN-dī, le-GEN-dī, au-di-EN-dī.

## 3. Four cases, no nominative

:: Ars longa, vīta brevis. || Art is long, life is short. (a Latin saying from the Greek doctor Hippocrates)

Legendī… legendō… ad legendum…

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The gerund declines like a second-family neuter (*bellum*), **singular only**: gen. ***legendī***, dat. ***legendō***, acc. ***legendum***, abl. ***legendō***. Which case is missing? | The **nominative** (and the vocative). No gerund can be the **subject** of a sentence, and there's no plural. |
| Then how do you say "Reading is pleasant"? (*Iūcundus*: pleasant, Chapter 34.) | With the **infinitive**: ***Legere iūcundum est.*** Chapter 41's *Errāre hūmānum est* works the same way. The infinitive is Latin's verb-noun for the **subject**. |
| And "I love reading"? | Also the infinitive: ***Amō legere*** (Chapter 18's *possum* + infinitive pattern). Not *amō legendum*. When the verb-noun is the plain **object**, Latin uses the infinitive too. |
| So where does the gerund show up? | Everywhere else: after a **noun or adjective** that wants a genitive (*ars legendī*), after a **preposition** (*ad legendum, in legendō*), or alone in the **ablative**, "by ___ing" (*legendō*). |
| What about the dative *legendō*, "for reading"? | It exists but is **rare**, and it looks just like the ablative. You'll hardly meet it. When you see *-ndō*, read "by ___ing" first. |

!!! success "The Law of the Missing Nominative"
    Latin has two verb-nouns, and they share the work:

    | Job | Form | Example |
    |---|---|---|
    | subject ("Reading is…") | **infinitive** | *Legere iūcundum est.* |
    | plain object ("I love reading") | **infinitive** | *Amō legere.* |
    | "of ___ing" | gerund, genitive | *ars **legendī*** |
    | "for ___ing" (after *ad*) | gerund, accusative | *ad **legendum*** |
    | "by ___ing" · "in ___ing" | gerund, ablative | ***legendō** · in **legendō*** |

    No gerund is ever the subject. If you see *-ndum est* or *-nda est*, it isn't a gerund (section 10).

## 4. "Of ___ing": the genitive

:: Tempus tacendī et tempus loquendī. || A time to keep silent and a time to speak. (Ecclesiastes 3:7, Vulgate)

Ars… tempus… cupidus… legendī.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "It's time for eating!" (Latin says "It's the time **of** eating.") | ***Tempus est edendī!*** Grandpa's favorite sentence. English says "time **to** eat"; Latin says "time **of** eating." |
| Chapter 46: *Avus cāseī cupidus est*, "Grandpa is eager for cheese." Now: "Grandpa is eager to eat." | ***Avus edendī cupidus est.*** *Cupidus* takes a genitive, and a gerund can be that genitive: "eager **for eating**." Chapter 46's *perītus* works the same way: *legendī perītus*, "skilled at reading." |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.2, 58 BC. The Swiss Helvetians are hemmed in by the Rhine, the Jura mountains and Lake Geneva, and can't easily raid their neighbors: *quā ex parte hominēs bellandī cupidī magnō dolōre adficiēbantur.* (*Quā ex parte*: on this score; *bellō, bellāre*: make war; *dolor*: pain; *adficiō*: affect.) Translate. | "And on this score, being men **eager for making war**, they were greatly upset." ***Bellandī*** is the genitive gerund after ***cupidī***. That's why they wanted to leave home, and why Caesar's war began. |
| Ovid, *The Art of Love* 1.1–2, about 2 BC, the most famous "art of" in Latin: *Sī quis in hōc artem populō nōn nōvit amandī, / hoc legat et lēctō carmine doctus amet.* (*Populus*: people; *nōvit*: knows, Chapter 46; *carmen, carminis*: poem; *doctus*: taught, expert.) Translate. | "If anyone among this people doesn't know **the art of loving**, let him read this, and, the poem read, let him love as an expert." ***Artem … amandī***: gerund in the genitive. ***Legat, amet***: Chapter 49's "let him." ***Lēctō carmine***: Chapter 40's untied pair. A Roman book that opens by telling you to read it! |
| Cicero, *On the Orator* 1.150, 55 BC. The great speaker Crassus gives his secret: *Stilus optimus et praestantissimus dīcendī effector ac magister.* (*Stilus*: the writing stick, Chapter 2; *praestāns*: outstanding; *effector*: maker.) Translate. Which word is missing? | "The pen is the best and most outstanding maker and teacher **of speaking**." ***Dīcendī*** depends on *effector ac magister*. The missing word is ***est*** (Latin often drops it). Writing makes a speaker. |
| Nepos, *Life of Hannibal* 2.1. Hannibal, in exile, stirs up King Antiochus of Syria against Rome: *Hunc tantā cupiditāte incendit bellandī, ut usque ā rubrō marī arma cōnātus sit īnferre Ītaliae.* (*Hunc*: Antiochus; *incendō*: set on fire; *rubrum mare*: the Red Sea; *īnferō*: carry into.) Translate. Which Book V clause comes after it? | "He fired him with **such a desire for making war** that he tried to carry arms against Italy all the way from the Red Sea." ***Cupiditāte … bellandī***: noun + genitive gerund. ***Tantā … ut … cōnātus sit***: Chapter 54's Free Result (perfect subjunctive). |

## 5. *Edendī causā*: for the sake of

:: Exemplī grātiā. || For the sake of an example (e.g.). (Chapter 35)

Edendī causā… discendī grātiā…

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 35 taught *exemplī grātiā* and *honōris causā*: a genitive, then ***causā*** or ***grātiā***, "for the sake of." "Grandpa comes for the sake of eating." | ***Avus edendī causā venit.*** (or *edendī grātiā*.) Roman order: the gerund **first**, *causā* after. In good English: "Grandpa comes **to eat**." |
| Chapter 51 said it another way: *Avus venit ut edat.* Same meaning? | **Yes**, both are goals. The *ut* clause is a full sentence with its own subject and subjunctive; ***edendī causā*** is a short phrase with no subject. Use the phrase when the doer is the same person. |
| "We read for the sake of learning." | ***Legimus discendī causā*** (or ***grātiā***). |

## 6. *Ad legendum*: for ___ing

:: Edimus ad vīvendum, nōn vīvimus ad edendum. || We eat in order to live; we don't live in order to eat. (teaching line, after an old saying of Socrates)

Ad legendum… ad edendum…

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The accusative ***legendum*** never stands alone: it comes after a **preposition**, nearly always ***ad***. What does *ad legendum* mean? | "**For reading**, (in order) to read." *Ad*, "toward," points at a goal. *Hic liber est ad legendum*: "This book is **for reading**." |
| "Grandpa is ready for eating." (*Parātus, -a, -um*: ready, from Chapter 5's *parō*.) | ***Avus ad edendum parātus est.*** *Parātus ad* + gerund: "ready **to** ___." |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 4.23.1, 55 BC, the start of the crossing that ends with Chapter 59's ships waiting at anchor: *nactus idōneam ad nāvigandum tempestātem tertiā ferē vigiliā solvit.* (*Nactus*: having got, from *nancīscor*; *idōneus*: fit, suitable; *tempestās*: weather; *vigilia*: night watch; *solvō*: cast off.) Translate. | "Having got weather **fit for sailing**, he set sail at about the third watch" (about midnight). ***Ad nāvigandum*** goes with ***idōneam***: "suitable **for sailing**." |
| You now have three ways to say Grandpa came "to eat." Name them. | ***ut ederet*** (Chapter 51) · ***edendī causā*** · ***ad edendum***. All goals. The two gerund ones are short phrases with no subject of their own. |

## 7. *Legendō*: by ___ing

:: Gutta cavat lapidem, nōn vī sed saepe cadendō. || A drop hollows the stone, not by force but by falling often. (Ovid, *Letters from the Black Sea* 4.10.5, with a later ending)

Legendō… cadendō… in legendō…

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Legendō discimus.* Translate. | "We learn **by reading**." The ablative gerund: Chapter 35's **means**, "by ___ing." |
| Seneca, *Letters* 7.8, about AD 64: *hominēs, dum docent, discunt.* A later proverb squeezed it into two words: *Docendō discimus.* Translate both. | "People learn **while** they teach" (Chapter 59's *dum* + present). "We learn **by teaching**." The proverb swaps the time clause for an ablative gerund. |
| The motto. Ovid wrote *Gutta cavat lapidem* (*gutta*: drop; *cavō*: hollow out; *lapis, lapidis*: stone, Chapter 56). Someone later added *nōn vī sed saepe cadendō*. Translate. Which word is the gerund? | "A drop hollows the stone, not by force but **by falling** often." ***Cadendō*** (*cadō*, fall): ablative, "by falling." Grandma's stone from Chapter 60 would agree. |
| ***In*** + ablative gerund = "in ___ing, while ___ing." Nepos, *Hannibal* 7.1. After his defeat at Zama, Hannibal gets ready for more war: *Cum in apparandō ācerrimē esset occupātus, Carthāginiēnsēs bellum cum Rōmānīs composuērunt.* (*Apparō*: prepare; *ācerrimē*: very keenly, hard; *occupātus*: busy; *compōnō*: settle.) Translate. | "While he was very busily occupied **in preparing**, the Carthaginians settled the war with the Romans." ***In apparandō***: *in* + ablative gerund. *Cum … esset occupātus*: Chapter 55's story *cum*. |
| Vergil, *Aeneid* 2.6–8. Aeneas, at Dido's banquet, is asked to tell how Troy fell: *quis tālia fandō … temperet ā lacrimīs?* (*Tālia*: such things; *fandō*, from *for, fārī*: speak, tell; *temperō ā*: hold back from; *lacrima*: tear.) Translate. Which Book V subjunctive is *temperet*? | "Who, **in telling** such things, could hold back from tears?" ***Fandō***: ablative gerund of *for*. ***Temperet***: Chapter 50's "would / could" subjunctive. Chapter 59 read the next lines (*quamquam animus meminisse horret…*). |

!!! success "The Law of the Four Jobs"
    | Case | Job | Example |
    |---|---|---|
    | genitive ***-ndī*** | "of ___ing," after a noun or adjective; "for the sake of" with *causā / grātiā* | *ars legendī · tempus edendī · cupidus bellandī · edendī causā* |
    | accusative ***-ndum*** | only after a preposition, mostly ***ad***: "for ___ing, in order to" | *ad legendum · idōneus ad nāvigandum · parātus ad edendum* |
    | ablative ***-ndō*** | alone: "by ___ing"; with ***in***: "in ___ing" | *Legendō discimus · in apparandō* |
    | dative ***-ndō*** | "for ___ing" (rare) | — |

## 8. Odd verbs and deponents

:: Tempus nāscendī et tempus moriendī. || A time to be born and a time to die. (Ecclesiastes 3:2, Vulgate)

Eundī… loquendī… moriendī…

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Eō, īre* (Chapter 32). Chapter 38 gave its participle *iēns, euntis*. Use the shortcut: "of going"? "It's time to go!" | ***eundī*** (e-UN-dee): *euntis → eundī*. ***Tempus est eundī!*** Then *eundō, ad eundum*. |
| *Ferō* → ? And *sum*, *possum*? | ***ferendī*** (third-family style). ***Sum*** and ***possum*** have **no gerund** in classical Latin. Say it another way. |
| Deponents (Chapter 43: passive look, active meaning). *Loquor* → "of speaking"? Ecclesiastes 3:7: *tempus tacendī et tempus loquendī.* Translate. | ***loquendī***. "A time **to keep silent** and a time **to speak**." The gerund of a deponent looks like any other gerund and means something **active**. |
| Ecclesiastes 3:2: *tempus nāscendī et tempus moriendī.* (*Nāscor*: be born, Chapter 43; *morior*: die, Chapter 39.) Translate. | "A time **to be born** and a time **to die**." ***Nāscendī, moriendī***: two deponents, ordinary gerunds. (*Morior* is an *-iō* verb: *-iend-*.) |
| Vergil's ***fandō*** came from *for, fārī*. Deponent or not? | **Deponent** (*for, fārī, fātus sum*, "speak"). Its gerund is plain *fandī, fandō*. Chapter 43's *fātum*, "what has been spoken," fate, comes from the same verb. |

## 9. The two *-ing*s

:: Lēctor legēns legendō discit. || The reader, reading, learns by reading. (teaching line)

Legēns… legendī…

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| English "-ing" does two jobs: "Grandpa, **reading**, falls asleep" and "**Reading** puts Grandpa to sleep." Which "-ing" describes a person, and which is a thing? | The first **describes Grandpa**: an adjective, Chapter 38's **participle** (*legēns*). The second is a **thing**, a noun: the **gerund** (or the infinitive). |
| *Avus legēns dormit.* Translate. Why *legēns*? | "Grandpa falls asleep **(while) reading**." ***Legēns*** is the participle: it **agrees** with *Avus* (nominative singular). |
| *Avus legendō dormit.* Translate. | "Grandpa falls asleep **from reading**" (by reading: the reading puts him to sleep). ***Legendō***: the gerund, ablative. It agrees with nothing; it's a noun. |
| "the reading girl" and "a girl eager for reading" (*studiōsus, -a, -um* + genitive: keen on). | ***puella legēns*** (participle, agrees with *puella*) · ***puella legendī studiōsa*** (or *cupida*; gerund in the genitive). |
| The motto: *Lēctor legēns legendō discit.* (*Lēctor, lēctōris*: reader.) Find the three "read" words and name each. | ***Lēctor***: "reader," a plain noun. ***Legēns***: participle, "reading," agrees with *lēctor*. ***Legendō***: gerund, "by reading." |

!!! success "The Law of the Two *-ing*s"
    **-ns / -nt-** = the **participle**, an adjective that **agrees** with somebody: *Avus legēns · puellae legentis*.  
    **-nd-** = the **gerund**, a noun that **agrees with nobody**: *ars legendī · legendō discimus*.  
    English says "-ing" for both. Latin never mixes them up.

## 10. Half verb, half noun

:: Faciendī plūrēs librōs nūllus est fīnis. || Of making many books there is no end. (Ecclesiastes 12:12, Vulgate)

Librōs legendō… bene legendī…

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Outside, the gerund is a **noun** (it has cases). Inside, it's still a **verb**: it can take an **object**. "We learn by reading books." | ***Librōs legendō discimus.*** *Librōs* is the accusative object of *legendō*, just as in *librōs legimus*. |
| Ecclesiastes 12:12, the end of the book: *faciendī plūrēs librōs nūllus est fīnis.* (*Plūrēs*: many, more, Chapter 33.) Translate. Find the gerund's object. | "**Of making** many books there is no end." ***Plūrēs librōs*** is the object of ***faciendī***. Written more than 2,000 years ago, and still true. |
| Because it's a verb inside, a gerund takes an **adverb**, not an adjective. "The art of reading well"? | ***Ars bene legendī.*** *Bene* (adverb, Chapter 34) goes with *legendī* like with any verb. Not *ars bonī legendī*. |
| Nepos, *Hannibal* 11.3. Before a sea battle, Hannibal sends King Eumenes a letter; everyone assumes it's about peace (Chapter 60's *nēmō dubitābat quīn*), and the king opens it: *At Eumenēs solūtā epistulā nihil in eā repperit, nisi quae ad irrīdendum eum pertinērent.* (*Solvō*: open; *epistula*: letter; *reperiō, repperī*: find; *irrīdeō*: mock; *pertineō ad*: have to do with.) Translate. | "But Eumenes, **when he opened the letter**, found nothing in it except things meant **for mocking him**." ***Ad irrīdendum eum***: *ad* + gerund with an object (*eum*). ***Solūtā epistulā***: Chapter 40's untied pair. ***Quae … pertinērent***: Chapter 59's kind-of clause. (The letter was a trick: it showed Hannibal which ship was the king's.) |
| Chapter 3's *Dē gustibus et colōribus nōn est disputandum*, Chapter 20's *mōnstrum horrendum*, Chapter 14's *Ōrandum est*, Chapter 60's *iūs iūrandum*: all have *-nd-*. Gerunds? | **Not quite.** Use the Law of the Missing Nominative: a gerund is never a subject, and never agrees. ***Horrendum*** agrees with *mōnstrum*; ***iūrandum*** with *iūs*; ***disputandum est*** and ***ōrandum est*** stand with *est*. These are the gerund's adjective cousin, the **gerundive**: Chapter 62, *Carthāgō dēlenda est*. |

!!! success "The Law of Half Verb, Half Noun"
    Outside, a **noun**: it has cases (*legendī, legendō, legendum*) and does noun jobs.  
    Inside, a **verb**: it takes an **object** (*librōs legendō*) and **adverbs** (*bene legendī*), and deponents keep their active meaning (*loquendī*).  
    *-nd-* that **agrees** with a noun, or stands with *est*, is not a gerund: that's Chapter 62.

## 13. Traps and fix-its

:: Errandō discitur. || By erring, one learns. (Chapter 23)

Quod verbum? Quis cāsus?

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Reading is pleasant": *Legendum iūcundum est.* | ***Legere iūcundum est.*** A subject needs the **infinitive**: the gerund has no nominative. |
| "I love reading": *Amō legendum.* | ***Amō legere.*** A plain object is the **infinitive** too. |
| "The art of reading": *ars legere.* | ***Ars legendī.*** After a noun, "of ___ing" is the **genitive** gerund. |
| "For reading": *ad legendō.* | ***Ad legendum.*** *Ad* takes the **accusative**. |
| "The reading boy": *puer legendī.* | ***Puer legēns.*** A word that describes a person is the **participle** (*-ns*). *Puer legendī* would be "a boy of reading." |
| "Of loving": *amāndī.* | ***Amandī.*** The vowel before *-nd-* is always **short** (like *amantis*). |

## 14. Lectiō: *Ars legendī*

:: Legendō discimus. || We learn by reading. (teaching line, after Chapter 1's *Dīcendō discimus*)

Domī… arca librōrum…

Diē undētrīcēsimā: the day after Chapter 60. The last cheese of Book V is gone (shared nine ways), and Grandpa has a new idea. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. Find every gerund and say its case and job; then find the participles hiding among them. New in passing: *undētrīcēsimus*, twenty-ninth; *arca*, chest; *vetus, veteris*, old; *fīniō, fīnītum*, finish; *versus*, line of poetry (Chapter 16); *frūstum*, bit, scrap (Chapter 58); *fingō*, make up; *ultimus*, last (Chapter 44); *addō, addidī*, add (Chapter 47); *rapiō*, snatch; *hahae*, ha ha (Chapter 54).

::: dialogue lectio
Iōannēs | Diē undētrīcēsimā Avus arcam veterem aperit. In arcā sunt librī: Caesar, Vergilius, Ovidius, Nepōs. || John | On the twenty-ninth day Grandpa opens an old chest. In the chest are books: Caesar, Vergil, Ovid, Nepos.
Avus | Liber Quīntus fīnītus est. Nunc ad auctōrēs! Hodiē artem legendī discēmus. || Grandpa | Book Five is finished. Now to the authors! Today we'll learn the art of reading.
Marcus | Quid est ars legendī, Ave? || Marcus | What is the art of reading, Grandpa?
Avus | Legendō discimus, Marce. Et ars bene legendī est lentē legere. || Grandpa | We learn by reading, Marcus. And the art of reading well is to read slowly.
Iōannēs | Tum Avia cum cāseō novō intrat. Canis statim surgit. || John | Then Grandma comes in with a new cheese. The dog gets up at once.
Quīntus | Ecce! Tempus est edendī! || Quintus | Look! It's time for eating!
Avia | Minimē, Quīnte. Hic cāseus est ad cēnam, nōn ad edendum nunc. || Grandma | Not at all, Quintus. This cheese is for dinner, not for eating now.
Avus | Avia, ego semper edendī cupidus sum… sed hodiē legendī. || Grandpa | Grandma, I'm always eager for eating… but today for reading.
Avia | Bene. Lūdāmus: quī versum bene legit, frūstum cāseī accipit. || Grandma | Good. Let's play a game: whoever reads a line well gets a bit of cheese.
Avus | Optimē! Legendō discimus, et legendō edimus! || Grandpa | Excellent! We learn by reading, and we eat by reading!
Cornēlia | Ego prīma legam. Ovidius: "Sī quis in hōc artem populō nōn nōvit amandī, hoc legat…" || Cornelia | I'll read first. Ovid: "If anyone among this people doesn't know the art of loving, let him read this…"
Avus | Bene lēgistī! Ars amandī, ars legendī… et ars edendī! Dā eī frūstum, Avia. || Grandpa | Well read! The art of loving, the art of reading… and the art of eating! Give her a bit, Grandma.
Iōannēs | Avia Cornēliae frūstum dat. Canis Cornēliam spectat. || John | Grandma gives Cornelia a bit. The dog watches Cornelia.
Marcus | Nunc ego. Caesar: "nactus idōneam ad nāvigandum tempestātem … solvit." || Marcus | Now me. Caesar: "having got weather fit for sailing … he set sail."
Avus | Et nōs hōram idōneam ad edendum nactī sumus! || Grandpa | And we've got an hour fit for eating!
Avia | Ave, tū nōndum lēgistī. Cāseus legentibus datur, nōn dormientibus! || Grandma | Grandpa, you haven't read yet. The cheese is given to people reading, not to people sleeping!
Avus | Tum legam! Ecclēsiastēs: "Tempus flendī et tempus rīdendī… tempus edendī…" || Grandpa | Then I'll read! Ecclesiastes: "A time to weep and a time to laugh… a time to eat…"
Avia | "Tempus edendī"? Hoc in librō nōn est! Est "tempus tacendī." || Grandma | "A time to eat"? That's not in the book! It's "a time to keep silent."
Quīntus | Avus nōn legendō, sed fingendō cāseum quaerit! || Quintus | Grandpa is after the cheese not by reading, but by making things up!
Omnēs | Hahae! || Everyone | Ha ha!
Iōannēs | Ego ultimus legō. Ovidius quoque: "Gutta cavat lapidem." Et aliquis posteā addidit: "nōn vī, sed saepe cadendō." || John | I read last. Ovid too: "A drop hollows the stone." And someone later added: "not by force, but by falling often."
Avia | Lapidem! Meministisne lapidem meum in pannō? || Grandma | A stone! Do you all remember my stone in the cloth?
Avus | Meminī… Et sīc ego quoque: nōn vī, sed saepe petendō, frūstum accipiam. || Grandpa | I remember… And that's how I'll do it too: not by force, but by asking often, I'll get a bit.
Avia | Quia bene lēgit, Iōannēs frūstum accipit. || Grandma | Because he read well, John gets a bit.
Iōannēs | Sed dum Avia mihi frūstum dat, canis id ē manū meā rapit! || John | But while Grandma is giving me my bit, the dog snatches it out of my hand!
Avus | Ecce ars edendī! Canis legere nōn potest, sed exspectandō edit. || Grandpa | There's the art of eating! The dog can't read, but he eats by waiting.
Avia | Ave, tū quoque frūstum habēbis… post cēnam. Nunc est tempus tacendī. || Grandma | Grandpa, you'll have a bit too… after dinner. Now it's a time to keep silent.
Avus | Tacēbō… et legam. Crās Nepōtem legēmus. Legendō enim discimus! || Grandpa | I'll keep quiet… and read. Tomorrow we'll read Nepos. For we learn by reading!

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Find every genitive gerund and the word it hangs on. | ***artem legendī · ars bene legendī · Tempus est edendī · edendī cupidus … legendī · artem … amandī · Ars amandī, ars legendī, ars edendī · Tempus flendī, rīdendī, edendī, tacendī***. Each one hangs on a noun (*ars, tempus*) or an adjective (*cupidus*). |
| Find the *ad* gerunds. What job do they do? | ***ad edendum*** (twice) and Caesar's ***ad nāvigandum***: "**for** ___ing," a goal, after *ad*. *Ad cēnam* is a plain noun ("for dinner"), the same job. |
| Find the "by ___ing" gerunds. | ***Legendō discimus · legendō edimus · nōn legendō, sed fingendō · cadendō · saepe petendō · exspectandō edit.*** All ablative: means. |
| Grandma: *Cāseus legentibus datur, nōn dormientibus!* Gerunds? | **No: participles** (Chapter 38), dative plural, used as nouns: "to **those reading**, not to **those sleeping**." They agree with people. The Law of the Two *-ing*s. |
| Why does Grandma catch Grandpa's Ecclesiastes? | *Tempus edendī* isn't in Ecclesiastes 3. Grandpa slipped it in after *tempus rīdendī*. The real list has *tempus tacendī et tempus loquendī* (section 15), so Grandma hands him the one he deserves. |

## 15. Auctor: *Omnia tempus habent*

:: Omnia tempus habent. || Everything has its time. (Ecclesiastes 3:1, Vulgate)

Tempus… tempus… tempus…

Ecclesiastes (Latin *Ecclēsiastēs*, "the Preacher") is a book of the Old Testament. Jerome put it into Latin around AD 400, and its third chapter is a list of gerunds. Here are verses 1–4 and 7–8 in the Vulgate's own words (the second half of verse 3 and verses 5–6 are left out). New in passing: *spatium*, space, span; *trānseō*, pass by (Chapter 32); *ūniversa*, all things; *plantō*, plant; *ēvellō*, pull up; *occīdō*, kill; *sānō*, heal; *fleō*, weep; *rīdeō*, laugh; *plangō*, mourn; *saltō*, dance; *dīlēctiō*, love; *odium*, hatred; *pāx, pācis*, peace.

::: dialogue auctor
Ecclēsiastēs | Omnia tempus habent, et suīs spatiīs trānseunt ūniversa sub caelō. || Ecclesiastes | Everything has its time, and all things pass under heaven in their own spans.
Ecclēsiastēs | Tempus nāscendī et tempus moriendī; tempus plantandī et tempus ēvellendī quod plantātum est. || Ecclesiastes | A time to be born and a time to die; a time to plant and a time to pull up what has been planted.
Ecclēsiastēs | Tempus occīdendī et tempus sānandī. || Ecclesiastes | A time to kill and a time to heal.
Ecclēsiastēs | Tempus flendī et tempus rīdendī; tempus plangendī et tempus saltandī. || Ecclesiastes | A time to weep and a time to laugh; a time to mourn and a time to dance.
Ecclēsiastēs | Tempus tacendī et tempus loquendī. || Ecclesiastes | A time to keep silent and a time to speak.
Ecclēsiastēs | Tempus dīlēctiōnis et tempus odiī; tempus bellī et tempus pācis. || Ecclesiastes | A time of love and a time of hatred; a time of war and a time of peace.

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Every *tempus* takes a genitive. Which lines use gerunds, and which use plain nouns? | Gerunds: ***nāscendī, moriendī, plantandī, ēvellendī, occīdendī, sānandī, flendī, rīdendī, plangendī, saltandī, tacendī, loquendī***. Plain nouns: ***dīlēctiōnis, odiī, bellī, pācis***. A gerund in the genitive does just what a noun in the genitive does. |

!!! success "Ars legendī: tua est!"
    You have the verb's last everyday piece. Latin's two verb-nouns split the work: the **infinitive** for subject and object (*Legere iūcundum est · Amō legere*), the **gerund** for everything else: ***legendī*** "of reading," ***ad legendum*** "for reading," ***legendō*** "by reading." Next, Chapter 62 *Carthāgō Dēlenda Est* meets the gerund's adjective cousin, the gerundive, which explains *iūs iūrandum*, *mōnstrum horrendum* and *Dē gustibus … disputandum* at last.

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking. (Books I and II)

1. Chant the gerunds: *amandī, habendī, legendī, capiendī, audiendī, eundī*. Then all four cases of one: *legendī, legendō, legendum, legendō*.
2. Three "art of"s and three "time for"s: *ars legendī, ars edendī, ars bene dīcendī; tempus est edendī, dormiendī, eundī*.

6. The two *-ing*s: *Avus legēns dormit* and *Avus legendō dormit*. Say what's different.
