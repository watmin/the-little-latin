# Caput LXIV: Ōrātiō Oblīqua

*Liber VI, Ad Auctōrēs · Chapter 64*

*In which Grandpa keeps his promise. Last night he said:* Crās vōbīs nārrābō quid mercātor mihi dīxerit. *This morning he tells it, first in the seller's own words, then retold. Chapter 42 taught the main part of reported speech: the accusative and infinitive. But the seller said more than one plain statement: there was a cheese **which** he had brought, an **if**, a **because**, a question, an order. What happens to those inside a report? One rule covers nearly all of it: **every hanging clause goes subjunctive**, on the clock of the verb of saying. Then the sneaky cousin: a* quod *with a subjunctive and no verb of saying at all, which means "because, **as he said**." The real lines: Caesar's Helvetian envoys and their old chief Divico, Ariovistus the German king, Socrates, Themistocles who couldn't sleep, an angry mother in Plautus, and two old promises paid (Cicero's* quod … ēmīserim *from Chapter 58 and Nepos's* antecēdat *from Chapter 62). It's the thirty-second day. Speaking goal: retell what someone said, with all its whos, ifs, becauses, questions and orders.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Mercātor dīxit cāseum, quem attulisset, optimum esse · Dīxit sē domum venīre quod aeger esset · Rogāvit cūr tot custōdēs mēcum venīrent · Cavērem canem · Noctū ambulābat, quod somnum capere nōn posset*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** subordinate clause ("the hanging clause"; old-school also "dependent clause") · rhetorical question ("a question that answers itself") · virtual *ōrātiō oblīqua* ("the hidden report": someone else's reason or words, with no verb of saying; old-school also "implied" or "informal indirect discourse")
    - **Forms:** no new endings. Every subjunctive you know, now inside reports: *esset, attulisset, venīrent, cavērem, reminīscerētur, congrederētur*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Reported Clause · the Law of the Saying Verb's Clock · the Law of Who's Who · the Law of the Reported Question · the Law of the Reported Order · the Law of Someone Else's Reason
    - **Words:** *quod, quia* (because) · *aeger* · *grātīs* · *posterō diē* · *queror* · *reminīscor* · *congredior* · *obses* · *maiōrēs*
    - **Reading:** *Quid mercātor dīxit* (day thirty-two: Grandpa reports the seller's words) and Caesar on Divico's speech to Caesar
    - **Speaking:** tell your family what someone said, asked, and ordered, with every "who," "if" and "because" in place

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. When you meet a subjunctive in a long passage with no *ut*, *cum* or question word to explain it, look back for a verb of **saying or thinking**. If there is one, you're inside a report: the main verbs are infinitives and every hanging verb is subjunctive. If there isn't, ask whose reason or words it is: maybe it's someone else's (section 7).

## 1. *Quid mercātor dīxit?*

:: Crās vōbīs nārrābō quid mercātor mihi dīxerit. || Tomorrow I'll tell y'all what the seller said to me. (Grandpa, Chapter 63)

Herī… hodiē… quid dīxit?

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Last night Grandpa promised: *Crās vōbīs nārrābō quid mercātor mihi dīxerit.* Today is "tomorrow." Here is one thing the seller said, his very words: *"Hic cāseus, quem ex Graeciā attulī, optimus est."* (*Afferō, afferre, attulī*: bring; *Graecia*: Greece.) Translate. | "**This cheese, which I brought from Greece, is the best.**" Two clauses: the main one (*hic cāseus … optimus est*) and a *quem* clause hanging on *cāseus* (Chapter 28). |
| Chapter 42: report the main clause only, after *Mercātor dīxit…* | ***Mercātor dīxit cāseum optimum esse.*** Subject to accusative, verb to infinitive: the Law of the Reported Word. *Hic* drops out; Grandpa isn't standing at the stall anymore. |
| Now Grandpa reports the whole thing: *Mercātor dīxit cāseum, quem ex Graeciā attulisset, optimum esse.* What happened to *attulī*? | Two things. **Person**: "I brought" is now about the seller, so third person. **Mood**: ***attulisset*** is a **subjunctive** (pluperfect: past team, earlier, Chapter 54). It isn't a question (Chapter 53) or a purpose (Chapter 51). It's a plain "which" clause. So why the subjunctive? |
| Because it's **inside the report**. Who says the cheese came from Greece: Grandpa, or the seller? | **The seller.** Grandpa is only passing it on. Latin marks every hanging clause in a report as "his words, not mine," and the mark is the subjunctive. |
| Chapter 42 gave the name: *ōrātiō oblīqua*, "slanted speech," reported speech. Chapter 51 called *ut dēs* in *Dō ut dēs* "a clause that hangs on" the main one. The grammar-book name for a hanging clause? | A **subordinate clause** (*sub* + *ōrdō*: ranked under). Plain words: **the hanging clause.** Old-school also "**dependent clause**." The one it hangs on is the **main clause**. Today: what happens to subordinate clauses inside *ōrātiō oblīqua*. |

## 2. Every hanging clause goes subjunctive

:: Dīxit sē domum venīre quod aeger esset. || He said he was coming home because he was sick. (teaching line)

Quod… quia… quī… sī…

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Marcus sends word home from school: *"Domum veniō quod aeger sum."* (*Quod* or *quia*: because. *Aeger, aegra, aegrum*: sick.) Translate. | "**I'm coming home because I'm sick.**" *Quod aeger sum*: a "because" clause, hanging on *domum veniō*. Plain *quod* + indicative: Marcus gives his own reason. |
| Report it after *Marcus dīcit…* | ***Marcus dīcit sē domum venīre quod aeger sit.*** Main clause: ***sē … venīre*** (accusative + infinitive). Hanging clause: ***quod aeger sit***, subjunctive. And "I" became ***sē***, as in Chapter 42. |
| Now after *Marcus dīxit…* | ***Marcus dīxit sē domum venīre quod aeger esset.*** "Marcus said he was coming home because he was sick." The infinitive doesn't change (Chapter 42's Borrowed Clock); the subjunctive moves to the **past team**, *esset*. |
| Grandma, direct: *"Sī canis cāseum edet, īrāta erō."* Report it after *Avia dīcit…* (*Fore* = *futūram esse*, Chapter 41.) | ***Avia dīcit, sī canis cāseum edat, sē īrātam fore.*** The "then" half is the main clause: infinitive (***fore***). The "if" half hangs: subjunctive (***edat***). A future "if" becomes a present subjunctive on the now team. |
| Quintus, direct: *"Canis, quī in culīnā est, ēsurit."* Report it after *Quīntus dīxit…* | ***Quīntus dīxit canem, quī in culīnā esset, ēsurīre.*** "Who," "which," "because," "if," "when," "until": every hanging clause, all subjunctive. A Chapter 55 *cum* was subjunctive already; it simply stays so. |
| Cicero, *On the Orator* 1.63, 55 BC, quoting a saying of Socrates: *Sōcratēs dīcere solēbat, omnīs in eō, quod scīrent, satis esse ēloquentīs.* (*Omnīs, ēloquentīs*: old spellings of *omnēs, ēloquentēs*; *in eō quod*: in what; *ēloquēns*: well-spoken.) Translate. Find the main clause and the hanging one. | "Socrates used to say that **everyone** is eloquent enough **in what he knows**." Main: ***omnīs … esse ēloquentīs***, infinitive. Hanging: ***quod scīrent***, subjunctive, past team after *solēbat*. Cicero calls the saying "more plausible, but still not true." |

!!! success "The Law of the Reported Clause"
    In ***ōrātiō oblīqua*** (reported speech):  
    the **main clause** of a statement → **accusative + infinitive** (Chapter 42);  
    every **subordinate clause** (who, which, because, if, when, until…) → **subjunctive**.  
    The subjunctive says: "these are **his** words, not mine." (Allen and Greenough §580.)

    | His words | Reported: *Dīxit…* |
    |---|---|
    | *Domum veniō quod aeger sum.* | *sē domum venīre quod aeger **esset**.* |
    | *Cāseus quem attulī optimus est.* | *cāseum quem **attulisset** optimum esse.* |
    | *Sī canis cāseum edet, īrāta erō.* | *sī canis cāseum **ederet**, sē īrātam fore.* |

## 3. Whose clock?

:: Dīxit sē Rōmam itūrum ut cōnsulem vidēret. || He said he would go to Rome to see the consul. (grammar-book example, Allen and Greenough §585)

Dīcit… dīxit… sit… esset…

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 54's matching rule: a subjunctive takes its team from the verb it hangs on. Inside a report, which verb sets the team: the infinitive, or the verb of saying? | **The verb of saying.** *Dīcit* → now team (*sit, fuerit*); *dīxit* → past team (*esset, fuisset*). The infinitives in between don't count. |
| *"Domum vēnī quod medicus mē mīsit."* (*Medicus*: doctor.) Report it after *dīcit*, then after *dīxit*. | ***Dīcit sē domum vēnisse quod medicus sē mīserit.*** ***Dīxit sē domum vēnisse quod medicus sē mīsisset.*** The sending came **earlier**: perfect subjunctive (now team), pluperfect (past team). |
| The grammar book's sample: *Dīxit sē Rōmam itūrum ut cōnsulem vidēret.* The going is future (*itūrum*). Why is *vidēret* past team? | Because it hangs, in the end, on ***dīxit***. "He said he would go to Rome to see the consul." The future infinitive in the middle doesn't change the team. |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.17.1. Liscus, a chief of the Aedui, finally talks: *Liscus … prōpōnit: esse nōn nūllōs, quōrum auctōritās apud plēbem plūrimum valeat.* (*Prōpōnō*: lay out; *nōn nūllī*: some, "not none"; *auctōritās*: influence; *plēbs, plēbis*: the common people; *plūrimum valeō*: have the most power.) Translate. Which team, and why? | "Liscus lays it out: **there are some men whose influence carries the most weight with the common people.**" Main: ***esse nōn nūllōs***. Hanging: ***quōrum … valeat***, **now team**, because *prōpōnit* is a historic present. Chapter 54's fine print: a historic present may lead either team. |
| Grandpa: *Mercātor dīxit cāseum, quem attulisset, optimum esse.* Grandpa says it again later: *Mercātor dīcit cāseum, quem attulerit, optimum esse.* What changed, and why? | ***attulisset → attulerit***: ***dīxit*** (past team) became ***dīcit*** (now team). The "earlier" stays earlier. |

!!! success "The Law of the Saying Verb's Clock"
    Every subjunctive in a report follows the **verb of saying** by Chapter 54's matching rule:

    | Verb of saying | Same time | Earlier |
    |---|---|---|
    | **now team**: *dīcit, dīcet* | *quod aeger **sit*** | *quod medicus sē **mīserit*** |
    | **past team**: *dīxit, dīcēbat* | *quod aeger **esset*** | *quod medicus sē **mīsisset*** |

    Fine print: Caesar and Livy sometimes slip back to the now team in a long past report, to make it vivid.

## 4. Who's who

:: Cūr dē suā virtūte aut dē ipsīus dīligentiā dēspērārent? || Why should they despair of their own courage or of his care? (Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.40.4)

Ego → sē… tū → ille…

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The seller to Grandpa: *"Ego tibi crās alterum cāseum dabō."* (*Alter*: a second, another.) John reports it after *Mercātor Avō dīxit…* | ***Mercātor Avō dīxit sē eī posterō diē alterum cāseum datūrum esse.*** "I" (the speaker) → ***sē***. "You" (the listener) → ***eī***. And ***crās*** → ***posterō diē***, "on the next day" (*posterus*: next). |
| Grandpa reports the same words himself: *Mercātor mihi dīxit…* What's "you" now? | ***Mercātor mihi dīxit sē mihi posterō diē alterum cāseum datūrum esse.*** "You" in the seller's mouth is Grandpa, and Grandpa is talking: ***mihi***. Pronouns follow **who is doing the reporting**. |
| Words of "here and now" move too. *hodiē, crās, herī, nunc, hic* in a report told later? | ***eō diē*** (that day), ***posterō diē*** (the next day), ***prīdiē*** (the day before), ***tum*** (then), ***ille*** (that). English does it too: "tomorrow" becomes "the next day." |
| Chapter 42's Law of the Speaker's *Sē*: inside the report *sē* is the speaker. Now inside a hanging clause: *Mercātor dīxit cāseum, quem sibi Graecī dedissent, optimum esse.* Whose *sibi*? | **The seller's**: "the cheese **the Greeks had given him**." The clause is still his words, so *sē/sibi* still points to him. (Allen and Greenough §300 call this the "indirect reflexive.") |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.40.4, to his frightened officers: *Cūr dē suā virtūte aut dē ipsīus dīligentiā dēspērārent?* (*Dīligentia*: care; *dēspērō dē*: give up hope in.) Caesar is the speaker. Whose *suā*, whose *ipsīus*? | "Why should they despair **of their own** courage or **of his** care?" ***Suā*** = the officers', the subject of its own clause (*dēspērārent*). ***Ipsīus*** = Caesar's, the speaker's. Inside a hanging clause *sē/suus* can point either way, so Latin often uses ***ipse*** to mean the speaker and keep it clear. |
| A real question in that line is subjunctive. Which, and why not an infinitive? | ***dēspērārent***. A question inside a report goes subjunctive, like Chapter 53's tucked-in question. Section 5. |

!!! success "The Law of Who's Who"
    Retold words change **person** and **place**:

    | In his words | In the report |
    |---|---|
    | *ego, meus; nōs, noster* (the speaker) | ***sē, suus***, or ***ipse*** to keep it clear |
    | *tū, tuus; vōs, vester* (the listener) | ***is, ille*** (or ***mē, tē*** if that's the reporter or his listener) |
    | *hodiē, crās, herī, nunc, hic* | ***eō diē, posterō diē, prīdiē, tum, ille*** |

    Inside a hanging clause, *sē* may mean the speaker **or** the subject of its own clause; *ipse* points to the speaker.

## 5. Reported questions

:: Quid sibi vellet? Cūr in suās possessiōnēs venīret? || What did he want? Why was he coming into his lands? (Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.44.8)

Quid? Cūr? Num?

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The seller to Grandpa: *"Cūr tot custōdēs tēcum veniunt?"* Grandpa reports it: *Rogāvit…* | ***Rogāvit cūr tot custōdēs mēcum venīrent.*** Chapter 53's tucked-in question, past team. *Tēcum* is Grandpa, so ***mēcum***. |
| In a long report, a question can come **without** *rogāvit*. Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.44.8, 58 BC. Ariovistus, king of the Germans, answers Caesar: *Quid sibi vellet? Cūr in suās possessiōnēs venīret?* (*Possessiō*: property, lands.) Translate. | "**What did he** (Caesar) **want? Why was he coming into his** (Ariovistus's) **lands?**" Subjunctives with no *rogāvit*: the verb of saying (*respondit*, 1.44.1) is several sentences back, but it still rules. |
| Whose *sibi*, whose *suās*? Who's coming? Turn it back to Ariovistus's own words. | ***Sibi*** and ***suās*** point to the **speaker**, Ariovistus. The one coming (***venīret***) is the listener, Caesar. His words: ***Quid tibi vīs? Cūr in meās possessiōnēs venīs?*** |
| Some questions don't want an answer. "Who could forgive that?" means "No one could." The grammar-book name? | A **rhetorical question** (*rhētor*, a public speaker). Plain words: **a question that answers itself**. It's a statement dressed up as a question. |
| Caesar 1.14.3, his reply to the Helvetian envoys: even if he were willing to forget the old insult, *num etiam recentium iniūriārum … memoriam dēpōnere posse?* (*Recēns, -entis*: fresh, recent; *iniūria*: wrong, injury; *dēpōnō*: lay down; *num*: surely not? Chapter 2.) What mood is *posse*, and why? | An **infinitive** (subject *sē*, Caesar, understood): "could **he** lay aside the memory of fresh wrongs as well?" A question that answers itself ("No!") is a statement in disguise, so it takes the statement's form: accusative + infinitive. (Allen and Greenough §586; the line between the two kinds isn't always sharp.) |

!!! success "The Law of the Reported Question"
    In a report, a **real question** (asking for an answer) → **subjunctive**, even with no verb of asking nearby: *Quid sibi vellet?*  
    A **rhetorical question** (a question that answers itself) → usually **accusative + infinitive**, like a statement: *num … memoriam dēpōnere posse?*

## 6. Reported orders

:: Cum vellet, congrederētur. || Whenever he wanted, let him come and fight. (Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.36.6)

Venī! → venīret…

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The seller's last words to Grandpa (section 13): *"Nōlī cāseum tōtum ūnō diē edere!"* Chapter 56 reported orders with *ut / nē*: *Mercātor mē monuit nē cāseum tōtum ūnō diē ederem.* In the middle of a long report, Grandpa can drop *monuit*. How? | ***… nē cāseum tōtum ūnō diē ederem.*** A reported order is a **subjunctive**: no *ut* is needed for a "do," and a "don't" (*nōlī*) becomes ***nē*** + subjunctive. (Allen and Greenough §588.) |
| *"Cavē canem!"* (Chapter 6's Pompeii doorway.) Grandpa reports it: | ***Cavērem canem.*** "I was to beware of the dog." Past team after *dīxit*, first person because Grandpa is telling it about himself. |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.13.4. The Helvetian envoy (section 14) threatens Caesar: *reminīscerētur … veteris incommodī populī Rōmānī.* (*Reminīscor* + genitive: remember; *incommodum*: setback, disaster.) Turn it back into a direct order. | ***Reminīscere veteris incommodī populī Rōmānī!*** "**Remember** the old disaster of the Roman people!" *Reminīscere* is a deponent's command (Chapter 43); in the report it's imperfect subjunctive. |
| Caesar 1.7.5. The Helvetians ask to march through the Roman province. Caesar stalls: *lēgātīs respondit diem sē ad dēlīberandum sūmptūrum: sī quid vellent, ad Īdūs Aprīlēs reverterentur.* (*Lēgātus*: envoy; *dēlīberō*: think over; *sūmō*: take; *Īdūs Aprīlēs*: the Ides of April, April 13, which Caesar writes *Īd. Aprīl.*; *revertor*: come back.) Translate. Sort the verbs. | "He answered the envoys that he would take a day **to think it over**: **if they wanted anything, they should come back on the Ides of April.**" ***Sūmptūrum*** [*esse*]: main clause. ***Vellent***: a hanging "if." ***Reverterentur***: the order. *Ad dēlīberandum*: Chapter 61's gerund. (He was stalling for troops.) |
| Caesar 1.36.6. Ariovistus to Caesar: *Cum vellet, congrederētur: intellēctūrum quid invictī Germānī, exercitātissimī in armīs, quī inter annōs XIIII tēctum nōn subissent, virtūte possent.* (*Congredior*: meet, come and fight; *intellegō, intellēctum*; *invictus*: unbeaten; *exercitātus*: trained; *XIIII*: fourteen; *tēctum*: roof; *subeō*: go under.) Translate. Find all five kinds of verb. | "**Whenever he wanted, let him come and fight**: he would find out what the unbeaten Germans, highly trained in arms, **who in fourteen years had not gone under a roof**, could do by courage." Order: ***congrederētur***. Main: ***intellēctūrum*** [*esse*] (subject Caesar, understood). Question: ***quid … possent***. Hanging "who": ***subissent***. Hanging "when": ***vellet***. |

!!! success "The Law of the Reported Order"
    In a report, every **command** → **subjunctive**, by the matching rule (present after a now-team verb, imperfect after a past-team one). No *ut* is needed.  
    A **don't** (*nōlī…*) → ***nē*** + subjunctive.

    | His words | Reported: *Dīxit…* |
    |---|---|
    | *Cavē canem!* | *cavēret canem* |
    | *Nōlī cāseum gustāre!* | *nē cāseum gustāret* |
    | *Reminīscere!* | *reminīscerētur* |

## 7. The hidden report: someone else's reason

:: Noctū ambulābat Themistoclēs, quod somnum capere nōn posset. || Themistocles used to walk about at night, because (he said) he couldn't sleep. (Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations* 4.44)

Quod… meā sententiā? suā?

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| John tells the story: *Avus īrātus est quod canis cāseum ēdit.* Then Grandpa tells it: *Avus īrātus est quod canis cāseum ēderit.* Same English, "Grandpa is angry because the dog ate the cheese." What's the difference? | ***ēdit***, indicative: the **teller** vouches for the reason. The dog did eat it. ***ēderit***, subjunctive: it's **Grandpa's** reason, "because, **as he says**, the dog ate it." Maybe the dog did; the teller isn't saying. |
| There's no *dīcit* in *quod canis cāseum ēderit*. So where's the report? | **Hidden** in the sentence. Being angry "because…" is Grandpa's own thought; the *quod* clause is his reason, so it's treated as his words. Grammar books call this ***virtual ōrātiō oblīqua*** (old-school also "implied" or "informal indirect discourse"). Plain words: **the hidden report.** |
| Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations* 4.44, 45 BC. Themistocles, the Athenian who beat the Persians at sea: *Noctū ambulābat in pūblicō Themistoclēs, quod somnum capere nōn posset, quaerentibusque respondēbat Miltiadis tropaeīs sē ē somnō suscitārī.* (*Noctū*: at night; *in pūblicō*: out in the streets; *somnus*: sleep; *quaerentibus*: to people who asked; *Miltiadēs*: the general who won at Marathon; *tropaeum*: trophy, victory monument; *suscitō*: wake.) Translate. | "Themistocles used to walk the streets at night, **because (he said) he couldn't sleep**, and to people who asked he answered that **he was kept awake by Miltiades' trophies**." ***Posset***: Themistocles' reason, the hidden report. Then a plain report: ***sē … suscitārī***. Someone else's glory kept him up. |
| Plautus, *The Little Box* (*Cistellāria*) 101–102, about 200 BC. A girl complains: *nunc mea māter īrātast mihi, / quia nōn redierim domum ad sē.* (*Īrātast* = *īrāta est*; *redeō, rediī*: go back.) Translate. Whose *sē*? | "Now my mother is angry with me **because (she says) I didn't come back home to her**." ***Redierim***: the mother's complaint, so subjunctive, and ***sē*** is **the mother**, whose thought it is. A *quia* can do it too. |
| Cicero, *Letters to Atticus* 2.1.12, 60 BC: *Paetus … omnīs librōs quōs frāter suus relīquisset mihi dōnāvit.* (*Paetus*: a friend; *omnīs* = *omnēs*; *relinquō, relīquī*: leave behind; *dōnō*: give as a gift.) Translate. Why *relīquisset*? | "Paetus gave me all the books **which (as he put it) his brother had left**." Not a "because" this time: a *quī* clause, and it's **Paetus's** description, so subjunctive. ***Suus*** is Paetus's too. |
| Caesar 1.41.2. After a pep talk from Caesar, the Tenth Legion *eī grātiās ēgit quod dē sē optimum iūdicium fēcisset.* (*Grātiās agō*: thank; *iūdicium*: opinion, judgment.) Translate. Whose *eī*, whose *sē*? | "It thanked him **because (they said) he had formed the best opinion of them**." ***Eī*** = Caesar; ***sē*** = the legion, whose thought the *quod* clause is. Caesar lets the soldiers' words say it. |

!!! success "The Law of Someone Else's Reason"
    ***Quod / quia*** + **indicative**: the reason the **writer** gives and vouches for.  
    ***Quod / quia*** + **subjunctive**: **someone else's** reason, "because, **as he said / thought**." It's a hidden report (virtual *ōrātiō oblīqua*), and *sē* inside it points to the person whose thought it is.  
    A *quī* clause can do the same: *librōs quōs frāter suus relīquisset*.

## 8. Old promises kept

:: Nōn est īnfitiandum. || It must not be denied. (Nepos, *Hannibal* 1.1; Chapter 62)

Prōmissa… solūta!

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 58, Cicero, *Against Catiline* 2.15: *illud timeō, nē mihi sit invidiōsum aliquandō, quod illum ēmīserim potius quam quod ēiēcerim.* Chapter 58 said: "The *quod* part is 'because, people will say'; just read it." Now explain *ēmīserim*. | A **hidden report**. "That it may someday be held against me **that (as people will say) I let him go** rather than threw him out." The charge is **other people's**, not Cicero's own view, so subjunctive. Perfect *ēmīserim*: now team (after *timeō … sit*), earlier. |
| Chapter 62, Nepos, *Hannibal* 1.1: *nōn est īnfitiandum Hannibalem tantō praestitisse cēterōs imperātōrēs prūdentiā, quantō populus Rōmānus antecēdat fortitūdine cūnctās nātiōnēs.* Chapter 62 said *antecēdat* "is subjunctive because it sits inside reported speech: Chapter 64." Explain it now. | ***Hannibalem … praestitisse*** is the report's main clause (after *nōn est īnfitiandum*, "it mustn't be denied that"). ***Quantō … antecēdat*** hangs inside it: subjunctive. Now team, because *est* is present. |
| Chapter 60, Nepos, *Hannibal* 11.2: *statim ad Eumenem dēductus est, quod nēmō dubitābat…* Why is *dubitābat* indicative? | It's **Nepos's own** reason, and he vouches for it: "because nobody doubted…" No report, so plain *quod* + indicative. |
| Fine print. Cicero, *For Archias* 20, about the general Marius and a writer he favored, Lucius Plotius: *cuius ingeniō putābat ea quae gesserat posse celebrārī.* (*Ingenium*: talent; *gerō*: do, carry out; *celebrō*: make famous.) A *quae* clause inside a report, but indicative. Why? | "By whose talent **he thought** the deeds **he had done** could be made famous." Cicero **vouches** that Marius did those deeds. A writer may leave a hanging clause indicative when it's a fact on his own word. *Quae gessisset* would mean "what Marius **claimed** he'd done." (Allen and Greenough §583.) |
| Caesar 1.7.3. The Helvetian envoys come *quī dīcerent sibi esse in animō … iter per prōvinciam facere, proptereā quod aliud iter habērent nūllum.* (*Sibi est in animō*: they have in mind, intend; *proptereā quod*: because.) Translate. Why *habērent*? | "…to say that **they intended** to march through the province, **because they had no other route**." ***Habērent***: a hanging clause in their report, past team. And their reason, not Caesar's: Caesar doesn't vouch for it. |

## 14. Auctor: Divico speaks to Caesar

:: Reminīscerētur veteris incommodī. || Let him remember the old disaster. (Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.13.4)

Helvētiī… Dīvicō… Caesar…

58 BC. The Helvetians (from what's now Switzerland) are migrating west through Gaul. Caesar has just caught one of their four districts still waiting to cross the river Arar (the Saône) and cut it to pieces; the rest had already crossed. That district, he notes, had killed a Roman consul, Lucius Cassius, and sent his army under the yoke forty-nine years before. Now the Helvetians send envoys, led by **Divico**, the old chief who had led them in that same war. Caesar reports Divico's speech in *ōrātiō oblīqua* from start to finish. Here is *Gallic War* 1.13.3–7 in Caesar's own words (the Latin Library text, with macrons added). New in passing: *Dīvicō, -ōnis*, Divico; *agō cum* + abl., deal with, plead with; *pāx, pācis*, peace; *in eam partem*, to that region; *cōnstituō*, settle, place; *sīn*, but if; *persequor*, pursue; *persevērō*, keep on; *vetus, veteris*, old; *incommodum*, setback, disaster; *prīstinus*, former, long-standing; *virtūs*, courage; *quod* (at the start of a sentence), as for the fact that; *imprōvīsō*, by surprise; *pāgus*, district, canton; *adorior, adortus*, attack; *trānseō*, cross; *magnopere*, greatly; *tribuō*, credit to; *dēspiciō*, look down on; *dolus*, trick; *īnsidiae*, ambush; *nītor* + abl., rely on; *quārē*, so, therefore; *committō ut*, let it happen that; *cōnsistō, cōnstitī*, take a stand; *calamitās*, disaster; *interneciō*, wiping out; *exercitus*, army; *prōdō*, hand down.

::: dialogue auctor
Caesar | Is ita cum Caesare ēgit: || Caesar | He pleaded with Caesar like this:

Caesar | in eam partem itūrōs atque ibi futūrōs Helvētiōs ubi eōs Caesar cōnstituisset atque esse voluisset; || Caesar | the Helvetians would go to that region and stay there, wherever Caesar settled them and wanted them to be;

!!! success "Ōrātiō oblīqua: tua est!"
    You can now retell **anything** anyone said. The main statements go accusative + infinitive (Chapter 42); every who, which, if, because and when goes **subjunctive**; questions go subjunctive (unless they answer themselves); orders go subjunctive, "don't"s with *nē*; the clock is the verb of saying's; *sē* and *ipse* mean the speaker. And a *quod* with a subjunctive is somebody else's reason, even when nobody says "he said." That's how Caesar writes whole speeches, and how Livy and Tacitus do too. Next, Chapter 65 *Ōrdō Verbōrum*: why Latin words stand where they stand, and how to read them left to right, so Grandpa can show you that line of Vergil.

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking. (Books I and II)

1. Report *Domum veniō quod aeger sum* after *Marcus dīcit* and after *Marcus dīxit*.
2. Report the seller's four sentences (section 13) the way Grandpa did, then turn them back.
3. Three hanging clauses in reports, one each with *quī, sī, quod*: *Avia dīxit canem, quī in culīnā esset, ēsurīre…*
4. One reported question and one reported order: *Cūr tot custōdēs mēcum venīrent? Nē cāseum tōtum ūnō diē ederem!*
5. Two pairs with *quod*: your reason (indicative) and somebody else's (subjunctive): *Avus īrātus est quod canis cāseum ēdit / ēderit.*
6. Say *Ego tibi crās cāseum dabō* as reported by the listener, by a third person, and by the speaker himself a week later.
