## L1 (fixed)
1. §6 (g-06) printed the whole Ecl. 1.1 pattern grouped with dots (`– ˘ ˘ · – ˘ ˘ · …`), which is a division into feet: Ch67's job (STATE keep-out: "may be marked long/short syllable by syllable but NOT divided into feet"). → plain sequence `– ˘ ˘ – ˘ ˘ – ˘ ˘ – – – ˘ ˘ – –` and "today, just the marks." Cards f-29/f-30 use the plain sequence too.
2. §16 (g-16) respelled *disyllabīs* di-sül-la-BEES: stress on the last syllable, contradicting the chapter's own Law (penult *la* short → antepenult). → di-SÜL-la-bees.
3. Lectiō line 20 addressed Grandpa as *Avus* (nominative). The vocative is *Ave* (Ch9; the book already writes *Ave* elsewhere, e.g. *Amāris, Ave!*, *Vidēris fessus, Ave*). → *Itaque, Ave, cāseum hodiē nōn edēs.*
4. Lectiō line 8 *Ergō "est" et "ēst" ambae longae sunt?* made quoted words (treated as neuter) agree as feminine. → *Ergō in "est" et "ēst" ambae syllabae longae sunt?* ("So in *est* and *ēst* both syllables are long?").
5. Lectiō line 22 *Avus versum tōtum plaudit*: *plaudō* doesn't take "the line" as an object that way. → *Avus versum tōtum dīcit et plaudit* ("says the whole line and claps it").
6. §15 (g-15) said Quintilian's *flexa* "gives the circumflex," a wrong etymology (the name is from *circumflexus*). → "Quintilian's 'bent' (*flexa*) tone is the one the **circumflex** (ˆ, from *circumflexus*, 'bent around') stands for."

3. §4 (g-04) claimed "*positiō* means what is placed after the vowel" as an etymology (unsourced; the ancient term is a calque on Greek θέσει and its sense is debated). → "'Position' here isn't where the syllable stands in the word. It's about what stands after its vowel."
4. §1 (g-01) quoted Ch1 with an inserted parenthesis inside the quotation marks. → paraphrase the question, quote Ch1's answer verbatim ("No. Just imitate the stresses given in this chapter. The full rule comes back later.").
5. §1 (g-01) "the crowd knows no rules, yet its ears hear length" was looser than *Orator* 173 (*nec vērō multitūdō pedēs nōvit nec ūllōs numerōs tenet … omnium longitūdinum et brevitātum in sonīs … iūdicium ipsa nātūra in auribus nostrīs …*). → "the crowd knows no rules of rhythm, yet nature put the judgment of long and short in its ears."

## L3 (logged)
- 80 frames (top of band), Lectiō 26 lines, Auctor 9, 85 cards.
- The accent is called stress (emphasis) throughout, as Ch1; Quintilian's *acūta/gravis* pitch words are explained in the Auctor intro with a hedge: "Scholars still argue how much the Roman accent was pitch and how much loudness" (no scholar cited on purpose).
- Respellings: *tenebrae* TE-ne-brai and *volucris* WO-lu-kris (speech) follow A&G §11c Note 1 and its own §12 example *a'lacris*; some school books stress te-NE-brae. Not argued further.
