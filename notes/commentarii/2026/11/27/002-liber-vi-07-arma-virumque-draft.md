# Caput LXVII: Arma Virumque

*Liber VI, Ad Auctōrēs · Chapter 67*

*In which Grandma's chest may finally open. Yesterday she promised:* Sī versum bene canētis, arcam aperiam. *Yesterday you also marked Vergil's line long and short, syllable by syllable. Today the family groups those longs and shorts into **feet** and sings the line the way Romans heard it. The tools: two kinds of foot, the **dactyl** (long, short, short) and the **spondee** (long, long); the six-foot line called the **hexameter**; the **beat** your hand claps, which is not the same as a word's stress (emphasis); the **cut** where the voice rests; and **elision**, the vowel that gets swallowed when two words meet. The real lines: Vergil's first seven lines of the* Aeneid*, his* Eclogues *again, the one-eyed giant from Chapter 20 who walks in heavy feet, a cavalry charge that gallops in light ones, and Ovid's bird from Chapter 66. It's the thirty-fifth day. Speaking goal: clap and read a line of Vergil in time.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Arma virumque canō, Trōiae quī prīmus ab ōrīs* in feet · *mult(um) ill(e) et* · – ˘ ˘ / – – · *Bene cecinistis!*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** foot (*pēs*: a small group of longs and shorts that repeats) · dactyl ("the finger": – ˘ ˘) · spondee ("the two-long foot": – –) · hexameter ("the six-foot line") · scansion ("scanning": marking a line's feet) · beat (*ictus*: where the hand comes down, the first syllable of each foot) · caesura ("the cut": a word ending inside a foot, where the voice rests) · elision ("the swallowed ending": a final vowel, or vowel + *m*, dropped before a vowel or *h*)
    - **Forms:** no new endings. Old lines, in feet
    - **Laws:** the Law of Two Feet · the Law of Six Feet · the Law of Seventeen · the Law of Beat and Stress · the Law of the Cut · the Law of the Swallowed Ending
    - **Words:** *pēs* · *versus* · *canō* · *digitus* · *scandō* · *ēlīdō* · *caesūra* · *sonitus* · *ungula* · *campus*
    - **Reading:** *In pedēs* (day thirty-five: Grandma's chest) and the first sentence of the *Aeneid*, 1.1–7
    - **Speaking:** clap the beat of a hexameter while your voice keeps the long and short syllables and the word stress

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Chapter 66's marks are back: **–** over a syllable means long, **˘** means short. New today: **/** between feet, **‖** for the cut, **×** for a syllable that may be long or short, and ( ) around a swallowed ending. When a line is marked for the beat, a **bold** syllable gets the beat; a syllable in CAPITALS gets the word stress (emphasis), as in every sound check.

## 1. *In pedēs*

:: Arma virumque canō, Trōiae quī prīmus ab ōrīs. || Arms and the man I sing, who first from the shores of Troy. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 1.1; Chapter 28)

Herī mēnsūrāvit… hodiē dīvidit.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Yesterday Grandpa said: *Crās "Arma virumque canō" canēmus, et versum in pedēs dīvidēmus.* Grandma added: *Sī versum bene canētis, arcam aperiam.* Translate both. (*Canō*: sing; *pēs, pedis*, m.: foot; *dīvidō*: divide; *arca*: chest; *aperiō*: open.) | "Tomorrow we'll sing *Arma virumque canō*, and we'll **divide the line into feet**." "**If y'all sing the line well, I'll open the chest.**" A fact if about the future (Chapter 57). The second cheese is still locked in that chest. |
| Chapter 66 marked Vergil's *Eclogue* line: **– ˘ ˘ – ˘ ˘ – ˘ ˘ – – – ˘ ˘ – –**. Clap it. What did Marcus say it sounds like? | *Versus currit ut equus*: "the line **runs like a horse**." The longs and shorts come back in little groups, like hoofbeats. |
| Those little groups have a grammar name. What is it? | A **foot** (Latin *pēs*, plural *pedēs*). Plain words: **a small group of long and short syllables that repeats** through a line, like a bar in music. Think of a foot tapping the time. |
| Latin for a line of poetry is *versus*, from *vertō*, "turn." Why "turn"? | At the end of a line you **turn back** and start again in the same pattern. Prose (from *prōrsus*, "straight ahead") just runs on (Allen and Greenough §612, note). |

## 2. Two feet

:: Dactylus currit, spondēus ambulat. || The dactyl runs, the spondee walks. (teaching line)

Ūnus longus, duo brevēs.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Grandpa holds up a finger: *Ecce digitus meus: ūnus articulus longus, duo brevēs.* (*Digitus*: finger; *articulus*: joint.) Translate. Which pattern of marks is that? | "Look, my finger: **one long joint, two short ones**." **– ˘ ˘**: long, short, short. |
| That foot is called a **dactyl**. Why? | Greek *daktylos* means **finger**: one long joint, then two short. Plain words: **the finger foot**, **– ˘ ˘**. *Tī-ty-re* is one. So is Allen and Greenough's *cōn-su-lis*. |
| *Tī-ty-re tū pa-tu-lae…*: is the second foot *tū pa-tu*? | **Yes**: *tū pa-tu*, **– ˘ ˘**, another dactyl. A foot doesn't care where words end. *Tū* finishes a word; *pa-tu* is half of the next one. |
| A foot of **two** longs, **– –**, like *rē-gēs* or *fā-gī*? | A **spondee**. Plain words: **the two-long foot**. It sounds slower and steadier. |
| Chapter 66: a long syllable lasts two of Quintilian's *tempora*, a short one one. Today call those units **counts**, because "beat" gets a new job in Section 6. How many counts in a dactyl? In a spondee? | Dactyl: 2 + 1 + 1 = **4** counts. Spondee: 2 + 2 = **4** counts. The **same** time! Two shorts take the time of one long, so a spondee can stand in for a dactyl and the line keeps time. |

!!! success "The Law of Two Feet"
    A **dactyl** is long, short, short: **– ˘ ˘** (*Tī-ty-re*), "the finger."  
    A **spondee** is long, long: **– –** (*fā-gī*).  
    Both take the same time (two shorts = one long), so a spondee can take a dactyl's place. Feet don't stop at word ends.

## 3. Six feet

:: Sex pedēs, ūnus versus. || Six feet, one line. (teaching line)

Ūnus, duo, trēs, quattuor, quīnque, sex.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Split Grandpa's line into feet: *Tī-ty-re tū pa-tu-lae re-cu-bāns sub teg-mi-ne fā-gī.* How many? | ***Tī-ty-re / tū pa-tu / lae re-cu / bāns sub / teg-mi-ne / fā-gī***: **six**. |
| A line of six feet is a **hexameter**. Why the name? | Greek *hex*, "six," + *metron*, "measure." Plain words: **the six-foot line**. Built from dactyls, it's the **dactylic hexameter**: the line of Homer, of Vergil's *Aeneid* and *Eclogues*, and of Ovid's *Metamorphoses*. |
| Which feet in *Tītyre* are dactyls and which spondees? | **– ˘ ˘ / – ˘ ˘ / – ˘ ˘ / – – / – ˘ ˘ / – –**: dactyl, dactyl, dactyl, **spondee**, dactyl, and the two-syllable last foot. |
| Can any foot be a spondee? | Any of the **first four**. The **fifth** is almost always a **dactyl** (a spondee there is rare: Allen and Greenough §615a–b). That dactyl near the end is what makes a hexameter sound like one. |
| And the sixth foot? | **Two** syllables: a long, then one that may be long **or** short (Allen and Greenough §612c). Mark the free one **×**. Ovid's line from Chapter 66 ends *vo-luc-ris*: its last syllable, *ris*, is short, and that's fine. |

!!! success "The Law of Six Feet"
    A **hexameter** has **six** feet: **– ˘˘ / – ˘˘ / – ˘˘ / – ˘˘ / – ˘ ˘ / – ×**.  
    In feet **one to four**, ˘˘ may become **–** (a spondee).  
    Foot **five** is a dactyl (almost always). Foot **six** is a long and one free syllable (×).

## 4. Scanning Tityrus

:: Tītyre, tū patulae recubāns sub tegmine fāgī. || Tityrus, you, lying back under the cover of a spreading beech. (Vergil, *Eclogues* 1.1; Chapters 65–66)

Tītyre, iterum!

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Marking a line's feet has a name. What? | **Scansion**, or **scanning** (Allen and Greenough §612d: from *scandō*, "climb," "a climbing or advance by steps"). You climb the line foot by foot. |
| Every foot starts the same way. How? | With a **long** syllable. A short never starts a foot: the shorts come **in pairs, after a long**. |
| Line 2 in Chapter 66's marks: **– – – ˘ ˘ – – – ˘ ˘ – ˘ ˘ – –**. Group them into feet from the start. | Long, long: a spondee, done. Then long, short, short… **– – / – ˘ ˘ / – – / – ˘ ˘ / – ˘ ˘ / – –**. |
| Now with the syllables: *sil-ves-trem te-nu-ī Mū-sam me-di-tā-ri-sa-vē-nā*. | ***sil-ves / trem te-nu / ī Mū / sam me-di / tā-ri-sa / vē-nā***: spondee, dactyl, spondee, dactyl, dactyl, last foot. (The *s* of *meditāris* slides onto *avēnā*, as in Chapter 66.) |
| Why can't the first three longs, *sil-ves-trem*, be one foot? | There's **no** three-long foot in a hexameter. Only **– ˘ ˘** and **– –**. Two longs make a spondee; the third long starts the next foot. |

## 5. Count and know

:: Numerā syllabās! || Count the syllables! (teaching line)

Septendecim? Trēdecim?

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| A hexameter of **all** dactyls (plus the two-syllable sixth foot): how many syllables? | **5 × 3 + 2 = 17.** |
| Each spondee has one syllable fewer than a dactyl. *Tītyre* has 16 syllables, line 2 has 15. How many spondees in each? | **16 → one** spondee; **15 → two**. Count the syllables, and you know how many spondees to look for before you mark a thing. |
| The fewest syllables, then? | **13**: spondees in all four free feet (4 × 2 + 3 + 2). (Twelve only in the rare line with a spondee in foot five too.) |
| Chapter 66's homework: *Ar-ma vi-rum-que ca-nō Trō-iae quī prī-mu-sa-bō-rīs*. Count, then scan. | **15** syllables → **two** spondees. Marks **– ˘ ˘ – ˘ ˘ – – – – – ˘ ˘ – –**, so: ***Ar-ma vi / rum-que ca / nō Trō / iae quī / prī-mu-sa / bō-rīs***. Spondees in feet **3** and **4**. (*Trō*: long *ō*; *iae*: a diphthong. Both born long.) |

!!! success "The Law of Seventeen"
    Count the syllables (after elision, Section 8). **Seventeen** means all dactyls; **each syllable fewer is one spondee**.  
    16 → one, 15 → two, 14 → three, 13 → four.

## 6. The beat

:: Manus pulsat, vōx loquitur. || The hand beats, the voice speaks. (teaching line)

Ictus et accentus.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Grandpa claps **once per foot**, on its first syllable. Name? | The **beat**, Latin ***ictus***, "a stroke" (Allen and Greenough §608: "the Ictus (beat), or metrical accent"). Plain words: **where the hand comes down**. It always falls on the long that starts each foot. |
| The beats of *Arma virumque canō*, *Trōiae quī prīmus ab ōrīs*? | **Ar**-ma vi-**rum**-que ca-**nō** Trō-**iae** quī **prī**-mus ab **ō**-rīs: six beats, one per foot. |
| Now Chapter 28's word stress (emphasis): AR-ma wi-RUM-kweh KA-noh TROY-yai kwee PREE-mus ab OH-rees. Where do beat and stress land on the same syllable? | Feet **1, 2, 5, 6**: *ar, rum, prī, ō*. In feet **3** and **4** they pull apart: the beat says ca-**nō**, the stress says KA-noh; the beat says Trō-**iae**, the stress says TROY-yai. |
| *Tītyre*: beats on *Tī, tū, lae, bāns, teg, fā*. Stress: TEE-tü-re, PA-tu-lai, RE-ku-bahns, TEG-mi-ne, FAH-gee. Where do they meet? | Feet **1, 5, 6**: *Tī, teg, fā*. In feet 3 and 4 the beat is on *lae* and *bāns*, the stress on *PA* and *RE*. Again: clash in the middle, **together at the end**. A hexameter usually closes with beat and stress in step. |
| So how do you read it aloud: lean on the beat, or on the stress? | Learn the pattern by **clapping the beat**. Then read with your voice keeping each syllable's **length** and each word's **stress** (emphasis); the lengths carry the rhythm. Allen and Greenough: keep "the measure or time of the syllables," but don't "destroy or confuse the words themselves" (§612d, note). Scholars still argue whether Romans also leaned on the beat with the voice. This book keeps **stress** for words and **beat** for the verse. |

!!! success "The Law of Beat and Stress"
    The **beat** (*ictus*) falls on the **first syllable of every foot**, always a long.  
    The **stress** (emphasis) stays where Chapter 66's rule puts it, inside each word.  
    In the **middle** of a hexameter they often clash; in the **last two feet** they usually meet. Clap the beat; speak the words.

## 7. The cut

:: Versus in duās partēs secātur. || The line is cut into two parts. (teaching line)

Ubi secātur?

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| When the family sings line 1, Cornelia's voice rests after *canō*. Is that the end of a foot? | **No.** Foot 3 is *nō Trō*, and *canō* ends after its first syllable, *nō*: **inside** the foot. A word ending inside a foot is a **caesura** (Latin *caesūra*, from *caedō*, "cut"). Plain words: **the cut**. |
| Where does the main cut usually fall? | In the **third** foot, right **after its first long** (Allen and Greenough §615c), often where the sense pauses too: *Arma virumque canō ‖ Trōiae quī prīmus ab ōrīs*, "Arms and the man I sing ‖ who first from the shores of Troy…" |
| Find the main cut in *Tītyre* and in line 2. | Foot 3 is *lae re-cu*: ***Tītyre, tū patulae ‖ recubāns sub tegmine fāgī***. Foot 3 of line 2 is *ī Mū*: ***silvestrem tenuī ‖ Mūsam meditāris avēnā***. Both right after the first long of the third foot. |
| Is the cut ever somewhere else? | Sometimes in the **fourth** foot, after its first long (§615c). You'll see one in the *Aeneid*'s line 3 (Section 15). |

!!! success "The Law of the Cut"
    A **caesura** is a word ending **inside** a foot, where the voice can rest.  
    The main one usually falls in the **third foot, right after its long**: *Arma virumque canō ‖ Trōiae…* Sometimes it's in the fourth.

## 8. The swallowed ending

:: Lītora, multum ille et terrīs iactātus et altō. || (To the) shores; much tossed was he, on land and on the deep. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 1.3)

Vōcālis perit…

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Aeneid* 1.3: *lītora, multum ille et terrīs iactātus et altō*. (*Lītus, lītoris*, n.: shore; *iactō*: toss; *altum*: the deep sea.) Count the syllables as written. | ***lī-to-ra mul-tum il-le et ter-rīs iac-tā-tu-se-tal-tō***: **16**. |
| Try to scan the start: *lī-to-ra / mul-tum-il…* What goes wrong? | *Lī-to-ra*: – ˘ ˘, fine. Then *mul-tu-mil*: **– ˘ –**. No foot has that shape. The line doesn't fit as written. |
| The fix: when a word **ends in a vowel**, or **a vowel + *m***, and the next word **begins with a vowel or *h***, the ending is squeezed out. Name? | **Elision** (Latin *ēlīsiō*, from *ēlīdō*, "knock out, squeeze out"; Allen and Greenough §612e–f). Plain words: **the swallowed ending**. Write it in parentheses: ***mult(um) ill(e) et*** → *mul-til-let*. |
| Now count and scan line 3. | **14** syllables → **three** spondees: ***lī-to-ra / mul-til / let-ter / rīs-iac / tā-tu-se / tal-tō***: **– ˘ ˘ / – – / – – / – – / – ˘ ˘ / – –**. It fits. |
| *Multum ille*: why does the whole *-um* go, not just the *u*? | A final ***m*** was weak, "a feeble nasal sound" (§612f, note 1), so the vowel **and** the *m* go together. |
| *Iactātus et altō*: another elision? | **No.** *-tus* ends in a **consonant**. The *s* just slides onto *et* (Chapter 66): *tā-tu-se-tal-tō*. Only a vowel or vowel + *m* is swallowed. |
| How do you say a swallowed ending? | Allen and Greenough: "In reading it is usual entirely to suppress elided syllables. Strictly, however, they should be sounded lightly." Either way it takes **no time**: *mul-til-let TER-rees*. |

!!! success "The Law of the Swallowed Ending"
    A word ending in a **vowel** or a **vowel + *m***, before a word beginning with a **vowel** or ***h***, loses that ending: **elision**. *Mult(um) ill(e) et* → *mul-til-let*.  
    It takes no time in the line, so count without it. A consonant ending isn't swallowed; it slides over (*iactātu-s et*).

## 9. Heavy feet, light feet

:: Quadrupedante putrem sonitū quatit ungula campum. || With a galloping sound the hoof shakes the crumbling plain. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 8.596)

Lentē… celeriter!

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 20's one-eyed giant, Polyphemus (*Aeneid* 3.658): *mōnstrum horrendum, īnfōrme, ingēns, cui lūmen adēmptum.* (*Cui lūmen adēmptum*: "from whom the light (his eye) has been taken"; *adimō, adēmptum*: take away.) Find the elisions. | **Three**: ***mōnstr(um) horrend(um) īnfōrm(e) ingēns***. Even the *h* of *horrendum* doesn't stop it: Chapter 66 said *h* isn't a consonant. (Allen and Greenough give this very line as their example.) |
| Count it after elision, and scan. | ***mōn-str(um) hor / rend(um) īn / fōrm(e) in / gēns cui / lū-me-na / dēmp-tum***: **13** syllables → **four** spondees, the most a normal hexameter can hold. (*Cui* is one syllable.) |
| Say it with the slow spondees. What does it sound like? | A **giant lumbering**: long, long, long, long… only the fifth foot hurries. Vergil's sound walks like the monster. |
| *Aeneid* 8.596, cavalry riding out: *quadrupedante putrem sonitū quatit ungula campum.* (*Quadrupedāns*: galloping, "going on four feet"; *putris*: crumbling; *sonitus, -ūs*: sound; *quatiō*: shake; *ungula*: hoof; *campus*: plain.) Translate. | "**With a galloping sound the hoof shakes the crumbling plain.**" (Some editions spell it *quadripedante*.) |
| Count and scan it. | **17** syllables → **no** spondees: ***qua-dru-pe / dan-te-pu / trem-so-ni / tū-qua-ti / tun-gu-la / cam-pum***. Five dactyls: **hoofbeats**. Marcus's horse, really galloping. |
| Chapter 66 said Ovid's *et prīmō similis volucrī, mox vēra volucris* needs the first *volucrī* short and the last *volucris* long, and promised why. Scan it. | ***et prī / mō si-mi / lis vo-lu / crī mox / vē-ra vo / luc-ris***. The first *lu* sits in a dactyl: it **must** be short. The last *luc* starts the sixth foot: it **must** be long. The either-way *cr* lets Ovid do both. |

## 10. Step by step

:: Pedetemptim. || Step by step (literally, "feeling the way with the feet"). (teaching line)

Prīmum, deinde, tum…

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Aeneid* 1.4: *vī superum saevae memorem Iūnōnis ob īram* (Chapter 65). Step 1: any elisions? | **None.** No word ends in a vowel or *-m* before a vowel. (*Ob īram*: the *b* slides over.) |
| Step 2: count. | ***vī-su-pe-rum-sae-vae-me-mo-rem-Iū-nō-ni-so-bī-ram***: **15** → **two** spondees. |
| Step 3: mark the end first: the last five syllables are always **– ˘ ˘ / – ×**. | ***nō-ni-so / bī-ram***: **– ˘ ˘ / – –**. |
| Step 4: the front, from the start, with Chapter 66's laws: *vī-su-pe-rum-sae-vae-me-mo-rem-Iū*. | *Vī* long; *su, pe* short: dactyl. *Rum* (by position), *sae* (diphthong): spondee. *Vae* long; *me, mo* short: dactyl. *Rem* (the *m* and the consonant *I* of *Iūnōnis*), *Iū*: spondee. ***vī su-pe / rum sae / vae me-mo / rem Iū / nō-ni-so / bī-ram***. Two spondees, as counted. |

!!! tip "Scanning in four steps"
    1. **Swallow** the elisions.  
    2. **Count** the syllables: 17 minus the count = the number of spondees.  
    3. **Mark the end**: the last five syllables are – ˘ ˘ / – ×.  
    4. **Fill the front**: each foot starts long; then – ˘ ˘ or – –. Check doubtful syllables with Chapter 66's laws.

## 11. Which foot?

:: Quī pēs? || Which foot? (teaching line)

Dactylus an spondēus?

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Tegmine*: the shape of the whole word? | ***teg-mi-ne***: **– ˘ ˘**, a dactyl all by itself. |
| *Mūsam*? *Rōmae*? | **– –** both: spondee-shaped words. (*Mū* by nature, *sam* by position before *meditāris*; *Rō, mae* by nature.) |
| *Patulae* (˘ ˘ –): can a foot start *pa-*? | **No.** A foot starts **long**. *Pa-tu* end one foot (*tū pa-tu*) and *lae* starts the next. |
| Can a foot be **– ˘** in the middle of the line? | **No.** Only the **sixth** foot can end short (– ×). In feet one to five, one short alone breaks the line. |

## 12. Swallow or slide?

:: Scande versum! || Scan the line! (teaching line)

Pēs post pedem.

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Avia et Avus*: elision? | **Yes**: ***Avi(a) et Avus***, said *A-vi-et-A-vus*: the last *a* of *Avia* goes. *Avia* ends in a vowel, *et* begins with one. |
| *Marcus et Avus*? | **No**: *Marcus* ends in *s*. The *s* slides over: *Mar-cu-set-A-vus*. |
| *Avus cāseum ēst*? | **Yes**: ***cāse(um) ēst***, *cā-se-ēst*: three syllables, not four. Vowel + *m* before a vowel. |
| *Cāseum habet*? | **Yes**: ***cāse(um) habet***. *H* doesn't block it. |
| A hexameter with **14** syllables after elision: how many dactyls in feet one to four? | **14 → three spondees**, so **one** dactyl among the first four (and the fifth foot's dactyl as always). |

## 13. Traps and fix-its

:: Ubi errāvimus? || Where did we go wrong? (teaching line)

Ubi errāvit?

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "A foot ends where a word ends." | **No.** *Tī-ty-re / tū pa-tu / lae re-cu*: feet run across word ends. |
| "*Patulae* starts a foot, because *pa* is stressed." | **No.** A foot starts with a **long**; *pa* is short. Stress isn't length (Chapter 66). |
| "The fifth foot is often a spondee." | **Rarely.** Feet one to four take spondees; the fifth is almost always a dactyl. |
| *Multum ille et* scanned *mul-tu-mil-le-et*. | **Swallow:** *mult(um) ill(e) et*, *mul-til-let*: the vowel **and** the *m* go. |
| *Iactātus et* scanned *iac-tāt(us) et*. | **No elision:** *-us* ends in a consonant. *Iac-tā-tu-set*. |
| In speech, "*ca-NŌ*, because the beat is on *nō*." | **KA-nō.** The beat belongs to the verse; the word keeps its stress (emphasis). |
| "*Mōnstrum horrendum*: no elision, because of the *h*." | **Elision:** *mōnstr(um) horrendum*. *H* doesn't count. |

## 14. Lectiō: *In pedēs*

:: Sī versum bene canētis, arcam aperiam. || If y'all sing the line well, I'll open the chest. (Grandma, Chapter 66)

Avia arcam aperit?

Diē trīcēsimā quīntā: the morning after Chapter 66. The wax tablet is back on the table, the second cheese is still locked in Grandma's chest (*arca*), and the dog is lying on top of it. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. |---|---|
| Line 7: why does Grandpa call the spondee *lentus et gravis*? | Two longs, no quick shorts: it **sounds** slow and heavy. (Grandma's point in line 8 is about Grandpa.) |
| Line 14: *Sī sīc in scaenā canerēs, theātra tōta exclāmārent!* What kind of "if," and whose theaters? | A **not-really if** about now (Chapter 57). The shouting theaters are Cicero's from Chapter 66. Quintus sang two syllables too many. |

## 15. Auctor: the first sentence of the *Aeneid*

:: Atque altae moenia Rōmae. || And the walls of high Rome. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 1.7)

Prīma verba Aenēidis.

About 29–19 BC. Vergil begins his epic about Aeneas, the Trojan who sailed to Italy, with one sentence seven lines long. Chapter 28 gave you its first line, Chapter 45 the next two, Chapter 65 the fourth. Here is all of it (the Latin Library text, with macrons). Read it aloud in time: hand on the beat, voice on the words. New in passing: *profugus*, exile; *iactō*, toss; *altum*, the deep sea; *superum*, of the gods above (old form of *superōrum*); *saevus*, cruel; *memor, memoris*, unforgetting; *Iūnō, Iūnōnis*, Juno; *patior, passus*, suffer (Chapter 43); *dum* + subjunctive, until (Chapter 59); *condō*, found; *īnferō*, bring in; *Latium*, the land of the Latins; *genus*, race; *unde*, from where; *Albānus*, of Alba Longa; *moenia*, walls.

|---|---|
| Line 3: you scanned it in Section 8. Where's the main cut? | In the **fourth** foot: ***lītora, multum ille et terrīs ‖ iactātus et altō*** (Allen and Greenough mark it there). The word *terrīs* ends right after that foot's first long. |
| Line 5: *multa quoque et bellō passus, dum conderet urbem*. Find the elision, count, and scan. | ***quoqu(e) et***. **14** syllables → three spondees: ***mul-ta quo / qu(e) et bel / lō pas / sus dum / con-de-re / tur-bem***. (The *t* of *conderet* slides onto *urbem*.) |
