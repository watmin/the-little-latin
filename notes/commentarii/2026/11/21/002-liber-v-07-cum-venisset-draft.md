# Caput LV: Cum Vēnisset

*Liber V, Modus Subiūnctīvus · Chapter 55*

*In which a little word with two lives finally gets its own chapter. Since Chapter 35 you've known "cum" as "with," as in "magnā cum cūrā" and "cum Avō." But twice it turned up doing something else. Chapter 27's Catullus counted kisses, "then, when we've made many thousands…," and said "that kind of clause comes later." Chapter 47's thief on the cross begged, "remember me when you come into your kingdom." Both times the Latin "when" was "cum." Here it is: the other "cum," the one that starts a clause. With the fact mood it says "when"; with the subjunctive it says "when, as" in a story, "since," or "although." Chapter 54's matching rule picks every one of its tenses. Along the way: Cicero on a silent Senate and on a crooked governor's spring, Caesar's own pages five times (one of them Pompey's boast before his great defeat), Socrates refusing to escape, Saint Matthew's Magi and Saint Luke's lost boy. Speaking goal: say when, since and although, now and in a story.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Cum vēneris, cēnābimus · Cum Avia vēnisset, cāseum invēnit · Cum fessus sīs, dormī! · Cum ēsurīret, tamen nōn ēdit · Quae cum ita sint…*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** *cum* clause (a "when / since / although" clause) · temporal *cum* (the plain "when," fact mood) · circumstantial *cum* (the story "when / as," subjunctive) · causal *cum* ("since") · concessive *cum* ("although")
    - **Forms:** nothing new to build! Every subjunctive today is one you already have, picked by Chapter 54's matching rule.
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Two *Cum*s · the Law of the Plain "When" · the Law of the Story *Cum* · the Law of "Since" and "Although"
    - **Words:** *cum* (when, since, although) · *cum prīmum* (as soon as) · *tūtus* (safe) · *fugō* (chase away)
    - **Reading:** *Custōs cāseī* (day twenty-three: Grandma goes to the forum, Grandpa guards the big cheese, and a mouse tries its luck)
    - **Speaking:** tell when something happens, now and in a story; give a reason with "since"; say "although … still"

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep Chapter 54's two teams close: every subjunctive *cum* today picks its tense by them.

## 1. Two words called *cum*

:: Cum vēnisset. || When he had come. (teaching line; the chapter title)

Cum Avō… cum vēnisset.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 35: *Marcus cum Avō ambulat · Dīc magnā cum cūrā!* What does *cum* mean, and what comes after it? | "**With**": *cum* is a preposition, and a noun in the **ablative** follows, *Avō, cūrā*. |
| Now: *Cum Avus domum venit, canis lātrat.* What follows *cum* this time? | A whole clause with its own verb, ***venit***. "**When** Grandpa comes home, the dog barks." |
| So how do you tell the two apart? | Look right after *cum*. An ablative noun and no verb of its own: "with." A clause with its own verb: "when" (and friends). |
| Are they one word? | **No**: two words that sound alike. Early Latin spelled the "when" word ***quom***. It's a cousin of Chapter 28's *quī, quod*, not of the preposition. |
| You've met the "when" *cum* twice. Chapter 27's Catullus 5: *dein, cum mīlia multa fēcerimus…* Chapter 47's Luke 23:42: *mementō meī, cum vēneris in rēgnum tuum.* Which mood are *fēcerimus* and *vēneris*? | The **fact mood**: both are future perfects (Chapter 27). "Then, **when** we've made many thousands…" · "Remember me **when you come** into your kingdom." Chapter 27 said "that kind of clause comes later." It's later. |
| Grammar books call this a ***cum* clause**. What jobs does it do? | Four: a plain "**when**" (the fact mood); a "**when, as**" in a story; "**since**"; and "**although**." The last three take the **subjunctive**. Their old-school names come one per section. |

## 2. The plain "when"

:: Cum tacent, clāmant. || When they are silent, they shout. (Cicero, *Against Catiline* 1.21)

Cum dormit, stertit.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "When Grandpa sleeps, he snores." (*Stertō*, Chapter 17.) | ***Cum Avus dormit, stertit.*** The fact mood: it just tells **when**. |
| "When you come, we'll have dinner." | ***Cum vēneris, cēnābimus.*** Chapter 27's future perfect: the coming is done before dinner. English says "come"; Latin is stricter. |
| Chapter 43's Catiline again (*Against Catiline* 1.21). Cicero tells him the senators, sitting silent, have already judged him: *Dē tē autem, Catilīna, cum quiēscunt, probant, cum patiuntur, dēcernunt, cum tacent, clāmant.* (*Quiēscō*: keep quiet; *probō*: approve; *patior*: allow, Chapter 43; *dēcernō*: decree, vote.) Translate. | "But about you, Catiline: **when** they keep quiet, they approve; **when** they allow it, they vote for it; **when** they are silent, they shout." Their silence **is** the shout. |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 6.12.1: *Cum Caesar in Galliam vēnit, alterius factiōnis prīncipēs erant Aeduī, alterius Sēquanī.* (*Factiō*: party; *prīnceps*: leader; *alter … alter*: the one … the other; *Aeduī, Sēquanī*: two Gallic peoples.) Translate. | "**When** Caesar came into Gaul, the leaders of one party were the Aedui, of the other the Sequani." |
| *Vēnit* is a past story. Why the fact mood? | This *cum* just **dates** the time: "at the time Caesar came," as you'd say "in 58 BC." A *cum* with the perfect indicative is always about **time**: a plain "when" (or, section 3, a "whenever"). |
| Old-school name? | **Temporal *cum*** (*tempus*, time): the *cum* that answers "**at what time?**" Often a *tum* (then) or *eō tempore* (at that time) points to it from the main clause. |

!!! success "The Law of the Plain “When”"
    ***Cum* + the fact mood** is a plain "**when**": it tells or dates the time. Now: *Cum Avus dormit, stertit.* Later: *Cum vēneris, cēnābimus* (future perfect, or future). In a story: *Cum Caesar in Galliam vēnit…* (perfect).

## 3. "Whenever" and "as soon as"

:: Cum rosam vīderat, tum incipere vēr arbitrābātur. || Whenever he had seen a rose, then he thought spring was beginning. (Cicero, *Against Verres* 2.5.27)

Cum rosam vīderat…

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 15's crooked governor Verres. Cicero mocks how he spent his year in Sicily. Spring's start he marked not by the west wind or by any star, *sed cum rosam vīderat, tum incipere vēr arbitrābātur.* (*Rosa*: rose; *vēr*: spring, Chapter 22; *arbitror*: think, Chapter 53.) Translate. | "But **whenever** he had seen a rose, **then** he thought spring was beginning." Verres's calendar was his flower garden. |
| Which tenses, and why that pair? | ***Vīderat***, pluperfect, and ***arbitrābātur***, imperfect. The seeing came first, the thinking followed, and it happened **again and again**, every year. Pluperfect + imperfect in the fact mood: "**whenever**" in the past. |
| And "whenever" for now: "Whenever Grandpa sees a cheese, he smiles." | ***Cum Avus cāseum vīdit, rīdet.*** Latin likes the perfect here: "whenever he **has seen**." (*Cum … videt* is fine too.) |
| *Cum prīmum*: "as soon as" (*prīmum*: first, Chapter 22). "As soon as Grandpa saw the cheese, he smiled." | ***Cum prīmum Avus cāseum vīdit, rīsit.*** The fact mood, perfect: one moment, straight after the other. |
| So what do "when," "whenever" and "as soon as" have in common? | All three just tell the **time**, so all three use the **fact mood**. |

## 4. The story *cum*

:: Cum Avia domum vēnisset, cāseum invēnit. || When Grandma had come home, she found the cheese. (teaching line)

Cum esset… cum vēnisset.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "While Grandma was in the forum, Grandpa was gazing at the cheese." (*Spectō*: look at.) Use *esset*. | ***Cum Avia in forō esset, Avus cāseum spectābat.*** Imperfect subjunctive: **same time**. |
| "When Grandma had come home, she found the cheese." | ***Cum Avia domum vēnisset, cāseum invēnit.*** Pluperfect subjunctive: **earlier**. That's the chapter title. |
| These things really happened. Why the subjunctive? | Not for doubt. This *cum* doesn't date the time; it **paints the scene**, the circumstances around the main event. Classical Latin made this its normal way to set a scene in a story. |
| Its names? | **Circumstantial *cum*** (*circum* around + *stō* stand: "what stands around" the event). Also called the **narrative** or **historical *cum***. Plain words: **the story *cum***. |
| Which team? | The **past team** (Chapter 54), always: imperfect for "**while, as, when**," pluperfect for "**when, after**." |
| Chapter 39: Latin has no participle for "having eaten." How would you say "Grandpa, having eaten the cheese, slept"? | ***Cum Avus cāseum ēdisset, dormīvit.*** "**When** Grandpa **had eaten** the cheese, he slept." The story *cum* fills that gap, and Latin writers use it all the time. |
| So in a story, should you ever say *Cum Avia domum vēnit…*? | You may (Caesar's *Cum Caesar in Galliam vēnit* dates the time). But in a story the subjunctive is the everyday choice. Rule of thumb: **when you tell a story, use the subjunctive *cum***; when you read, a perfect indicative after *cum* is the plain "when." |

!!! success "The Law of the Story *Cum*"
    In a past story, ***cum* + the imperfect subjunctive** sets the scene at the **same time** ("while, as, when"); ***cum* + the pluperfect subjunctive** sets it **earlier** ("when, after," "having …"). It's the **past team**, always: *Cum Avia in forō esset… · Cum Avia domum vēnisset…*

## 5. Magi and a lost boy

:: Cum autem nātus esset Iēsus in Bethlehem… || Now when Jesus had been born in Bethlehem… (Matthew 2:1, Nova Vulgata)

Cum nātus esset… cum redīrent…

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Matthew 2:1 (Nova Vulgata): *Cum autem nātus esset Iēsus in Bethlehem Iūdaeae in diēbus Hērōdis rēgis, ecce Magī ab oriente vēnērunt Hierosolymam.* (*Iūdaea*: Judea; *Hērōdēs*: Herod; *Magī*: the wise men; *oriēns*: the east, where the sun rises; *Hierosolyma*: Jerusalem.) Translate. | "Now **when** Jesus **had been born** in Bethlehem of Judea in the days of King Herod, behold, wise men came from the east to Jerusalem." |
| What's ***nātus esset***? | The pluperfect subjunctive of *nāscor*, be born (Chapter 43's deponents). A story *cum*, **earlier**: the birth came before the Magi's journey. |
| Luke 2:42–43 (Nova Vulgata). Every year Mary and Joseph go up to Jerusalem for the Passover. *Et cum factus esset annōrum duodecim, ascendentibus illīs secundum cōnsuētūdinem diēī fēstī, cōnsummātīsque diēbus, cum redīrent, remānsit puer Iēsus in Ierūsalēm, et nōn cognōvērunt parentēs eius.* (*Annōrum duodecim*: twelve years old; *ascendō*: go up; *secundum cōnsuētūdinem*: by the custom; *diēs fēstus*: feast day; *cōnsummō*: complete; *redeō*: go back, Chapter 32; *remaneō*: stay behind.) Translate. | "And **when** he **had become** twelve years old, as they went up by the custom of the feast, and **when** the days were completed, **as** they **were going back**, the boy Jesus stayed behind in Jerusalem, and his parents did not know." |
| Luke sets the scene four times. Find the two *cum* clauses and the two ablative absolutes (Chapter 40). | *Cum* clauses: ***cum factus esset*** (pluperfect: earlier) and ***cum redīrent*** (imperfect: same time). Ablative absolutes: ***ascendentibus illīs*** (present participle: while) and ***cōnsummātīs diēbus*** (perfect passive participle: after). |
| Turn *cōnsummātīs diēbus* into a *cum* clause. | ***Cum diēs cōnsummātī essent***, "when the days had been completed." Chapter 53's passive pluperfect subjunctive. Two tools, one job. |

## 6. Caesar's present, the past team

:: Quō cum vēnisset, cognōscit… || When he had come there, he learns… (Caesar, *Civil War* 1.34)

Mātūrat… cum nūntiātum esset.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 54: after Caesar's historic present, a subjunctive may take either team. Now *Gallic War* 1.7.1, the Helvetians on the move again: *Caesarī cum id nūntiātum esset, eōs per prōvinciam nostram iter facere cōnārī, mātūrat ab urbe proficīscī.* (*Nūntiō*: report, Chapter 42; *cōnor*: try, a deponent (Chapter 26 named it); *mātūrō*: hurry; *proficīscor*: set out; *urbs*: here Rome.) Translate. | "**When** it **had been reported** to Caesar that they were trying to march through our province, he **hurries** to set out from the city (Rome)." |
| *Mātūrat* is present. Which team does *nūntiātum esset* take? | The **past team**, pluperfect. And notice that *cum* sits after *Caesarī*: it doesn't have to come first. |
| 49 BC, the civil war. Caesar reaches Further Gaul (*Civil War* 1.34): *Quō cum vēnisset, cognōscit missum ā Pompeiō Vībullium Rūfum.* (*Quō*: to where, here "there," Chapter 8; *Pompeius*: Pompey, Caesar's great rival; *Vībullius Rūfus*: a Pompeian officer Caesar had captured and let go a few days before.) Translate. | "**When he had come** there, he **learns** that Vibullius Rufus had been sent by Pompey." *Missum (esse)*: Chapter 42's report, accusative and infinitive. |
| Can a story *cum* after a historic present ever take the now team? | **No.** Chapter 54's fine print, paid: a story *cum* always takes the **past team**, even after Caesar's present. (A goal or a tucked-in question may go either way; a story *cum* never does.) |
| *Quō cum, Caesarī cum*: what's the habit? | Latin often puts a linking word in front of *cum*. When you read, look for *cum* anywhere near the start of the clause. |

## 7. "Since"

:: Quae cum ita sint, Catilīna, perge quō coepistī. || Since this is so, Catiline, carry on where you began. (Cicero, *Against Catiline* 1.10)

Cum fessus sīs…

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Since you're tired, sleep!" Use *sīs*. | ***Cum fessus sīs, dormī!*** A *cum* that gives the **reason** takes the subjunctive. |
| Its names? | **Causal *cum*** (*causa*, reason; English *cause*). Plain words: ***cum* = "since."** |
| Cicero to Catiline again, *Against Catiline* 1.10: *Quae cum ita sint, Catilīna, perge quō coepistī: ēgredere aliquandō ex urbe; patent portae; proficīscere.* (*Quae*: "these things," a *quī*-word pointing back; *pergō*: carry on; *coepī*: I began, Chapter 46; *ēgredior*: go out, Chapter 45; *aliquandō*: at last; *pateō*: stand open.) Translate. | "**Since** this is so, Catiline, carry on where you began: go out of the city at last; the gates are open; set out!" |
| *Sint*: which team, and why? | The **now team**, present: the reason is true **now**, and the main verbs are commands. A "since" *cum* takes any tense the matching rule gives it. *Quae cum ita sint* became a stock phrase: "**since this is so**, therefore…" |
| 48 BC, the days before Pharsalus. Pompey has told his council that his cavalry will turn Caesar's flank (*Civil War* 3.86): *Id autem difficile nōn est, cum tantum equitātū valeāmus.* (*Equitātus*: cavalry, Chapter 54; *valeō*: be strong, Chapter 5's *Bene valēmus*; *tantum*: so much.) Translate. | "And that isn't hard, **since** we are so strong in cavalry." At Pharsalus, Caesar beat him. |
| "Since Grandpa had eaten the cheese, Grandma was angry." | ***Cum Avus cāseum ēdisset, Avia īrāta erat.*** The same form as a story *cum*: "when" or "since"? The **sense** decides, and often both are true, just like Chapter 38's participles ("while / because / although"). |

## 8. "Although"

:: Cum facile posset ēdūcī ē custōdiā, nōluit. || Although he could easily have been led out of prison, he refused. (Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations* 1.71, of Socrates)

Cum ēsuriat, tamen…

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Although Grandpa is hungry, he still isn't eating the cheese." (*Ēsuriō*, Chapter 39; *tamen*, Chapter 38.) | ***Cum Avus ēsuriat, tamen cāseum nōn edit.*** A *cum* that **clashes** with the main clause: "although." |
| Its names? | **Concessive *cum*** (*concēdō*, grant: "granted that…"). Plain words: ***cum* = "although."** The ***tamen*** ("still") in the main clause is the signpost, as with Chapter 38's participles. |
| 52 BC, a battle on the Seine near Paris. On the left wing (Caesar, *Gallic War* 7.62.4): *cum prīmī ōrdinēs hostium trānsfīxī tēlīs concidissent, tamen ācerrimē reliquī resistēbant.* (*Ōrdō*: row, rank, Chapter 54; *trānsfīgō*: pierce; *tēlum*: spear; *concidō*: fall; *ācerrimē*: most fiercely; *reliquī*: the rest; *resistō*: resist.) Translate. | "**Although** the enemy's front ranks had fallen, pierced by spears, the rest **still** resisted most fiercely." |
| Athens, 399 BC. Condemned to death, Socrates could have been smuggled out of prison by his friends. Cicero (*Tusculan Disputations* 1.71): *paucīs ante diēbus, cum facile posset ēdūcī ē custōdiā, nōluit.* (*Paucīs ante diēbus*: a few days before; *ēdūcō*: lead out, Chapter 54; *custōdia*: prison; *nōlō*: not want, refuse, Chapter 18.) No *tamen*. How do you know it's "although"? | The **sense**: he could escape easily, and he refused. The two clash. "A few days before, **although** he could easily have been led out of prison, he refused." *Posset*: Chapter 52's imperfect subjunctive of *possum*. |
| Do "since" and "although" ever take the fact mood after *cum*? | **Not in classical prose.** (Early writers like Plautus did.) For you: *cum* + fact mood is about **time**; "since" and "although" take the subjunctive. |

!!! success "The Law of “Since” and “Although”"
    ***Cum* + the subjunctive** may give a **reason** ("since," causal) or a **clash** ("although," concessive), with any tense the matching rule picks: *Cum fessus sīs, dormī! · Quae cum ita sint… · Cum ēsuriat, tamen nōn edit.* A ***tamen*** in the main clause means "although." Otherwise, the **sense** decides.

## 9. Sorting *cum*

:: Prīmum quaere modum! || First look for the mood! (teaching line; after Chapter 54)

Cum… cum… cum…

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Avus cum Aviā ad forum it.* | The preposition: "Grandpa goes to the forum **with** Grandma." Ablative *Aviā*, no verb of its own. |
| *Cum Avus domum vēnit, canis lātrāvit.* | Fact mood, perfect: the plain "**when**." "When Grandpa came home, the dog barked." |
| *Cum Avus domum vēnisset, canis lātrāvit.* | Subjunctive in a story: the story *cum*. "**When** Grandpa **had come** home, the dog barked." English hardly tells the two apart; Latin stories prefer this one. |
| *Cum Avus ēsuriat, cāseum edit.* | Subjunctive, a reason: "**Since** Grandpa is hungry, he's eating the cheese." |
| *Cum Avus ēsuriat, tamen cāseum nōn edit.* | Subjunctive with *tamen*: "**Although** Grandpa is hungry, he still isn't eating the cheese." |
| *Cum Avus cāseum vīderat, rīdēbat.* | Fact mood, pluperfect + imperfect: "**Whenever** Grandpa had seen a cheese, he smiled." |

!!! success "The Law of the Two *Cum*s"
    First look **right after *cum***, then at the **mood**.

    | After *cum* | Mood | Meaning | Old-school name |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | an ablative noun | (no verb) | **with** | preposition |
    | a clause | fact mood | **when, whenever, as soon as** (*cum prīmum*) | temporal |
    | a clause in a past story | subjunctive (imperfect, pluperfect) | **when, while, as, after** | circumstantial |
    | a clause | subjunctive | **since** | causal |
    | a clause | subjunctive (often + *tamen*) | **although** | concessive |

## 10. Three ways to set a scene

:: Cum hoc cognōvisset, rīsit. || When he had learned this, he laughed. (teaching line)

Edēns… cāseō inventō… cum invēnisset…

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 38: *Avus, cāseum edēns, cantāvit.* Say it with *cum*. | ***Cum Avus cāseum ederet, cantāvit.*** "While Grandpa was eating the cheese, he sang." Imperfect: same time, like the present participle. |
| Chapter 40: *Cāseō inventō, Avus rīsit.* Say it with *cum*. | ***Cum cāseus inventus esset, Avus rīsit.*** "When the cheese had been found, Grandpa laughed." Pluperfect passive: earlier, like Chapter 37's perfect passive participle. |
| "When Grandpa had found the cheese, he laughed": now Grandpa does the finding. | ***Cum Avus cāseum invēnisset, rīsit.*** Only a *cum* clause can say this one actively: there's no "having found" participle (Chapter 39). |
| So which should you use when you speak? | Any of the three. The *cum* clause is the easiest to build, and it can say anything: same time or earlier, active or passive. |

## 11. Build them

:: Cum vēneris, cēnābimus. || When you come, we'll have dinner. (teaching line)

Nunc et ōlim.

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "As soon as the dog saw Grandpa, he barked." | ***Cum prīmum canis Avum vīdit, lātrāvit.*** |
| "As we were going back home, it began to rain." (*Pluit*, Chapter 50; *coepī*, Chapter 46.) | ***Cum domum redīrēmus, pluere coepit.*** |
| "Since you are my friend, I'll give you some cheese." | ***Cum amīcus meus sīs, tibi cāseum dabō.*** |
| "Although Marcus had eaten, he was still hungry." | ***Cum Marcus ēdisset, tamen ēsuriēbat.*** |
| "When Grandma had heard this, she laughed." Two ways. | ***Cum Avia hoc audīvisset, rīsit***, or with Chapter 40: ***Hōc audītō, Avia rīsit.*** |

## 12. Traps and fix-its

:: Errāre licet; corrigere melius. || Making a mistake is allowed; correcting it is better. (teaching line; Chapter 50)

Quī modus?

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "When you come, we'll have dinner": *Cum venīs, cēnābimus.* | ***Cum vēneris, cēnābimus*** (or *Cum veniēs, cēnābimus*). Latin marks the future: future perfect or future. |
| "Since you're tired, sleep!": *Cum fessus es, dormī!* | ***Cum fessus sīs, dormī!*** "Since" takes the subjunctive. (*Cum fessus es* would mean "when(ever) you're tired.") |
| "Although he was hungry, he didn't eat": *Cum ēsuriēbat, tamen nōn ēdit.* | ***Cum ēsurīret, tamen nōn ēdit.*** "Although" takes the subjunctive. |
| A story in Caesar's present: *Quō cum veniat, cognōscit.* | ***Quō cum vēnisset, cognōscit.*** A story *cum* always takes the past team. |
| "Grandma came home with Grandpa": *Avia cum Avus domum vēnit.* | ***Avia cum Avō domum vēnit.*** The preposition *cum* needs the ablative. |

## 13. Lectiō: *Custōs cāseī*

:: Quis custōdiet ipsōs custōdēs? || Who will guard the guards themselves? (Juvenal, *Satires* 6.347–348; Chapter 29)

Cāseus in mēnsā… Avus prope mēnsam…

Diē vīcēsimā tertiā: the day after Chapter 54. The new big cheese is still on the table, and Grandma's *Nē tetigerīs!* still rings in Grandpa's ears. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. Sort every *cum*. New in passing: *vīcēsimus tertius*, twenty-third; *adhūc*, still; *interim*, meanwhile; *tūtus*, safe; *apertus*, open; *subitō*, suddenly; *fugō*, chase away; *tantum*, only; *mīrābile!*, amazing! Old friends: *custōs*, guard (Chapter 13); *mūs, mūris*, mouse (Chapter 15); *digitus*, finger (Chapter 23).

::: dialogue lectio
Iōannēs | Diē vīcēsimā tertiā cāseus magnus adhūc in mēnsā est. Nēmō eum tetigit. || John | On the twenty-third day the big cheese is still on the table. Nobody has touched it.
Avia | Ad forum eō. Cum redierō, cēnābimus. Interim nēmō cāseum tangat! || Grandma | I'm going to the forum. When I'm back, we'll have dinner. Meanwhile, let nobody touch the cheese!
Avus | Cum ego hīc sim, cāseus tūtus est. Ego eum custōdiam! || Grandpa | Since I'm here, the cheese is safe. I'll guard it!
Avia | Tū? Cum tū cāseum custōdiās, quis tē custōdiet? || Grandma | You? Since you're guarding the cheese, who's going to guard you?
Iōannēs | Ego, Avia! Cum Avus cāseum spectat, ego Avum spectō. || John | Me, Grandma! Whenever Grandpa looks at the cheese, I look at Grandpa.
Iōannēs | Cum Avia abiisset, Avus ad mēnsam sēdit. || John | When Grandma had gone, Grandpa sat down at the table.
Avus | Ō cāsee! Quam pulcher es! || Grandpa | O cheese! How beautiful you are!
Iōannēs | Deinde Avus tacēbat. Sed cum tacēret, tamen oculī eius clāmābant. || John | Then Grandpa went quiet. But although he was silent, his eyes were still shouting.
Iōannēs | Ut Cicerō dīxit: cum tacent, clāmant! || John | As Cicero said: when they're silent, they shout!
Avus | Iōannēs, cum tam fessus sim, paulum dormiam. Tū cāseum custōdī. || Grandpa | John, since I'm so tired, I'll sleep a little. You guard the cheese.
Iōannēs | Avus oculōs clausit… sed ūnum oculum apertum tenēbat. || John | Grandpa closed his eyes… but kept one eye open.
Iōannēs | Cum hōra praeteriisset, mūs parvus in mēnsam ascendit. || John | When an hour had gone by, a little mouse climbed onto the table.
Iōannēs | Cum mūs ad cāseum curreret, Avus subitō surrēxit! || John | As the mouse was running to the cheese, Grandpa suddenly jumped up!
Avus | Abī, mūs! Hic cāseus nōn tuus est! || Grandpa | Go away, mouse! This cheese isn't yours!
Iōannēs | Cum prīmum Avus clāmāvit, canis ē hortō cucurrit et mūrem fugāvit. || John | As soon as Grandpa shouted, the dog ran in from the garden and chased the mouse away.
Avus | Bene, canis! Cum mūrem fugāverīs, tibi grātiās agō. || Grandpa | Good dog! Since you've chased the mouse away, I thank you.
Iōannēs | Avus, cum valdē ēsurīret, tamen cāseum nōn ēdit. || John | Although Grandpa was very hungry, he still didn't eat the cheese.
Avus | Iōannēs, nōnne bonus custōs sum? || Grandpa | John, aren't I a good guard?
Iōannēs | Optimus! Sed cūr digitus tuus in cāseō est? || John | The best! But why is your finger on the cheese?
Avus | Hem… digitum tantum pōnēbam, ut scīrem num cāseus adhūc ibi esset. || Grandpa | Hmm… I was only putting my finger there, to know whether the cheese was still there.
Iōannēs | Cum Avia domum vēnisset, cāseum tōtum invēnit. || John | When Grandma had come home, she found the cheese whole.
Avia | Mīrābile! Cāseus adhūc tōtus est! Quid factum est? || Grandma | Amazing! The cheese is still whole! What happened?
Iōannēs | Cum tū abessēs, mūs vēnit. Sed Avus et canis eum fugāvērunt. || John | While you were away, a mouse came. But Grandpa and the dog chased it away.
Avus | Et cum valdē ēsurīrem, tamen cāseum nōn tetigī! || Grandpa | And although I was very hungry, I didn't touch the cheese!
Avia | Nōn tetigistī? Quid est igitur hoc in digitō tuō? || Grandma | You didn't touch it? Then what's this on your finger?
Avus | Hem… mūs id fēcit? || Grandpa | Hmm… the mouse did it?
Iōannēs | Omnēs rīdent, etiam Avia. || John | Everyone laughs, even Grandma.
Avia | Cum tam bonus custōs fuerīs, prīmam partem tibi dabō. || Grandma | Since you've been such a good guard, I'll give you the first piece.
Avus | Quae cum ita sint, cēnēmus! || Grandpa | Since that's how things are, let's have dinner!
Avia | Ō Ave! Cum cāseum vīdistī, cēnam incipere arbitrāris! || Grandma | Oh, Grandpa! Whenever you've seen a cheese, you think dinner is starting!
Iōannēs | Cum cēnāvissēmus, parva pars cāseī in mēnsā manēbat. Avus eam spectābat… || John | When we had had dinner, a small piece of the cheese stayed on the table. Grandpa was looking at it…

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Find the fact-mood *cum*s: the plain "when," the "whenever"s and the "as soon as." | ***Cum redierō*** (Grandma, future perfect), ***Cum Avus cāseum spectat*** (John, "whenever"), ***Cum prīmum Avus clāmāvit*** ("as soon as"). And Grandma's last joke, ***cum cāseum vīdistī***, "whenever." |
| Find the story *cum*s. Which are same time, which earlier? | Same time (imperfect): ***cum mūs ad cāseum curreret · cum tū abessēs.*** Earlier (pluperfect): ***cum Avia abiisset · cum hōra praeteriisset · cum Avia domum vēnisset · cum cēnāvissēmus.*** |
| Find the "since"s. Which team does each take? | ***Cum ego hīc sim · cum tū cāseum custōdiās · cum tam fessus sim*** (present) · ***cum mūrem fugāverīs · cum tam bonus custōs fuerīs*** (perfect, "have done"): all **now team**, because the main verbs are now. And Grandpa's Cicero, ***Quae cum ita sint***. |
| Find the "although"s, and their signpost. | ***Cum tacēret · cum valdē ēsurīret · cum valdē ēsurīrem***, each with ***tamen***. |
| Grandma's last line echoes which Roman, and how is Grandpa like him? | **Verres** (*cum rosam vīderat, tum incipere vēr arbitrābātur*): Verres's spring started with a rose; Grandpa's dinner starts with a cheese. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Loquāmur Latīnē! || Let's speak Latin! (teaching line)

1. In your own words: how do you tell the preposition *cum* from the clause *cum*?
2. Three plain "when"s: one now, one later (*Cum vēneris…*), one "whenever."
3. Tell a three-line story with two story *cum*s, one same time (*cum … esset*), one earlier (*cum … vēnisset*).
4. Give two reasons with "since," one now (*Cum fessus sīs…*), one in a story.
5. Say two "although … still" sentences with *tamen*.
6. Say one scene three ways: a participle, an ablative absolute, a *cum* clause.
7. The four Laws in your own words: the Two *Cum*s, the Plain "When," the Story *Cum*, "Since" and "Although."
