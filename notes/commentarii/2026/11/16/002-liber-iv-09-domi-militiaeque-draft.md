# Caput XLV: Domī Mīlitiaeque

*Liber IV, Verba Agentia · Chapter 45*

*In which the map and the clock get tidied up. Back in Chapter 8 you went* Rōmam *and stayed* domī *with no prepositions at all, and in Chapter 22 you learned to say* hōrā tertiā. *Today all of it comes together: where, where to, and where from; Athens and Carthage, which don't look like Rome; the countryside, the ground, and the evening; and the Roman pair that gives this chapter its name,* domī mīlitiaeque, *"at home and in the field." Then the clock: when, within how long, for how long, how long ago, how many years old, and how many miles. Along the way: a Roman historian on the good old days, Aeneas coming to Italy, an apostle going to Corinth, a temple built in forty-six years, a goddess who has been at war for years, a slave who tells his master he can never be happy where he is, and Caesar building a wall nineteen miles long. Speaking goal: say where you are, where you're going, and where you're from; say when, how long, and how long ago; and answer* Quot annōs nātus es?

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Domī sum · Rōmam eō · Athēnīs veniō · Rūrī habitō · Humī sedeō · Tribus diēbus veniam · Trēs hōrās dormīvī · Abhinc trēs annōs · Quot annōs nātus es? · Decem mīlia passuum*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** place where, place to which, place from which (the *Ubi? Quō? Unde?* places) · locative (recalled: the "at" case) · ablative of time within which (the "within" ablative) · accusative of extent (the "how long, how far" accusative)
    - **Forms:** *Rōmae, Corinthī, Athēnīs, Carthāgine* · *domī, rūrī, humī, mīlitiae, vesperī* · *Athēnās, Athēnīs* · *hōrā tertiā · tribus diēbus · trēs hōrās · abhinc trēs annōs · post trēs diēs · tribus diēbus post*
    - **Laws:** the Law of Home and Rome, full edition · the Law of the Point and the Stretch · the Law of Ago and Later
    - **Words:** *rūs, humus, mīlitia, Athēnae, Carthāgō, Corinthus, passus, abhinc, ante, post, per, vīgintī, quīnquāgintā, septuāgintā, ūndecimus, duodecimus, tertius decimus, castra, iter, legiō*
    - **Reading:** *Domī Mīlitiaeque* (Grandpa tells war stories for three hours, the dog falls asleep, and the cheese goes missing)
    - **Speaking:** say where you live, go, and come from; tell your age; say when, for how long, and how long ago; ask *Quō? Ubi? Unde? Quandō? Quam diū?*

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep Chapter 8 close (*Quō īs? domum, domī, domō; Rōmam, Rōmae, Rōmā*) and Chapter 22 (*hōrā tertiā* and the numbers).

## 1. *Quō? Ubi? Unde?*, recalled

:: Omnēs viae Rōmam dūcunt. || All roads lead to Rome. (medieval proverb; Chapter 8)

In culīnā. In culīnam. Ē culīnā.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 8's three questions: *Quō? Ubi? Unde?* What does each ask? | ***Quō?*** "Where to?" · ***Ubi?*** "Where?" · ***Unde?*** "Where from?" |
| An ordinary place: the kitchen. "I'm in the kitchen. I'm running into the kitchen. I'm coming out of the kitchen." | ***In culīnā sum. In culīnam currō. Ē culīnā veniō.*** *In* + ablative for where; *in* or *ad* + accusative for where to; *ē/ex*, *ā/ab*, or *dē* + ablative for where from. |
| Grammar books name the three: **place where**, **place to which**, **place from which**. Plain words? | The ***Ubi?*** place, the ***Quō?*** place (the goal), and the ***Unde?*** place (the starting point). Very old books say "place whither" and "place whence." |
| Chapter 8's Law of Home and Rome. "I'm going home. I'm at home. I'm coming from home." | ***Domum eō. Domī sum. Domō veniō.*** No preposition at all. |
| And Rome? | ***Rōmam eō. Rōmae sum. Rōmā veniō.*** Town names work like *domus*: bare accusative, the "at" form, bare ablative. |

## 2. Home and Rome, grown up

:: Nōn cuivīs hominī contingit adīre Corinthum. || Not every man gets to go to Corinth. (Horace, *Epistles* 1.17.36; Chapter 44)

Rōmae, Corinthī, Athēnīs, Carthāgine.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 44's Horace: *Nōn cuivīs hominī contingit adīre Corinthum.* Why no *ad* before *Corinthum*? | *Corinthus* is a **town**, so "to Corinth" is the bare accusative, just like *Rōmam*. (*Adīre* has *ad* glued on front, but the noun gets no preposition of its own.) |
| Chapter 8 called *Rōmae* and *domī* the **locative**, "the 'at' case." *Corinthus* is a second-family word like Chapter 8's *Tarentum*. "At Corinth"? | ***Corinthī.*** For singular first- and second-family towns, the locative looks like the genitive: *Rōmae* like *puellae*, *Corinthī* like *hortī*. |
| Chapter 8 promised that towns with plural names, like ***Athēnae*** (Athens; feminine plural), "work differently." "At Athens"? | ***Athēnīs.*** Plural towns use the **ablative** plural for "at." "I live at Athens": *Athēnīs habitō.* |
| "To Athens" and "from Athens"? | ***Athēnās eō*** (accusative plural) · ***Athēnīs veniō*** (ablative plural). Yes: "at Athens" and "from Athens" are the same word. The verb tells you which. |
| ***Carthāgō, Carthāginis*** (f.): Carthage, a third-family town. "At Carthage"? | ***Carthāgine*** (or *Carthāginī*). Third-family towns use the ablative (or an old *-ī*) for "at." To Carthage: *Carthāginem*. From Carthage: *Carthāgine*. |
| So what does a locative look like? | It **borrows** a look: singular first- and second-family towns look like the genitive (*Rōmae, Corinthī*); every other town looks like the ablative (*Athēnīs, Carthāgine*). Let the meaning ("at") guide you, as Chapter 8 said. |

## 3. The country, the ground, the evening

:: Rōmae rūs optās. || At Rome you long for the country. (Horace, *Satires* 2.7.28)

Rūrī, humī, vesperī.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| ***Rūs, rūris*** (n.): the country, the countryside (English *rural*). Like *domus*, it takes no preposition. "I'm going to the country. I live in the country. I'm coming from the country." | ***Rūs eō. Rūrī habitō. Rūre veniō.*** Locative *rūrī* (sometimes *rūre*). |
| Horace, *Satires* 2.7.28–29. At a holiday when slaves may speak freely, the slave Davus tells his master Horace: *Rōmae rūs optās; absentem rūsticus urbem / tollis ad astra levis.* (*Optō*: wish for, long for; *absēns*: absent; *rūsticus*: in the country, a countryman; *tollō … ad astra*: raise to the stars, Chapter 8's *ad astra*; *levis*: fickle.) | "**At Rome** you long for **the country**; **in the country**, fickle, you praise the absent City to the stars." *Rōmae*: locative. *Rūs*: what he wants, an ordinary accusative object. Never happy where he is. |
| ***Humus, humī*** (f.): ground, earth (English *humble*, "low to the ground"). *Humī* is a locative too. *Canis humī sedet.* | "The dog sits **on the ground**" (on the floor). *Humī*, "at the ground," with no *in*. |
| Chapter 16's ***vesperī***, "in the evening." Now look at the ending. | It's the **locative** of *vesper* (Chapter 40): "at evening." You've been using a locative since Chapter 16. |
| So which non-town words take a locative? | Just a handful: ***domī*** (at home), ***rūrī*** (in the country), ***humī*** (on the ground), ***vesperī*** (in the evening), and two more in the next section. Everything else uses *in* + ablative. |

## 4. *Domī mīlitiaeque*

:: Domī mīlitiaeque. || At home and in the field. (Roman saying)

Domī et mīlitiae.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| ***Mīlitia, mīlitiae*** (f.): military service, the army (Chapter 38's *mīlitiae caelestis*, "of the heavenly army"). Its locative, ***mīlitiae***, means "on military service, in the field." *Domī mīlitiaeque*? | "**At home and in the field**": in peace and in war, in everyday life and on campaign. Two locatives joined by *-que*. |
| Sallust, *The War with Catiline* 9.1, on the good old days of Rome: *Igitur domī mīlitiaeque bonī mōrēs colēbantur.* (*Igitur*: so, therefore; *mōs, mōris*: custom, character, Chapter 14's *Ō mōrēs*; *colō*: cultivate, honor, Chapter 41.) | "So **at home and in the field** good character was cultivated." *Colēbantur*: Chapter 30's imperfect passive. Sallust uses the pair again and again in that book. |
| Chapter 38's *mīlitiae caelestis* was a genitive. Today's *mīlitiae* is a locative. How do you tell? | **By the job.** "Of the heavenly army" belongs to a noun (*multitūdō*); "in the field" stands next to *domī* and tells **where**. First-family words have one form for genitive, dative, and locative singular: the sentence decides. |
| The Romans also paired *domī* with ***bellī***, the locative of *bellum* (Chapter 18). *Domī bellīque*? | "**At home and at war**": the same idea. Those are the last two locatives: *mīlitiae* and *bellī*, and they almost always come paired with *domī*. |

!!! success "The Law of Home and Rome, full edition"
    **Towns**, **small islands**, and five little words (*domus, rūs, humus, mīlitia, bellum*) use the bare case with **no preposition**:

    | | to (*Quō?*) | at (*Ubi?*) | from (*Unde?*) |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Rome | *Rōmam* | *Rōmae* | *Rōmā* |
    | Corinth | *Corinthum* | *Corinthī* | *Corinthō* |
    | Athens | *Athēnās* | *Athēnīs* | *Athēnīs* |
    | Carthage | *Carthāginem* | *Carthāgine* | *Carthāgine* |
    | home | *domum* | *domī* | *domō* |
    | the country | *rūs* | *rūrī* | *rūre* |

    The middle column is the **locative**: genitive-looking for singular first- and second-family towns, ablative-looking for the rest. Also *humī* (on the ground), *vesperī* (in the evening), and the pairs *domī mīlitiaeque*, *domī bellīque*. Everything else (rooms, gardens, countries, big islands) takes a preposition: *in Ītaliam, in Ītaliā, ex Ītaliā*.

## 5. Poets and apostles

:: Ītaliam … Lāvīniaque vēnit / lītora. || He came to Italy and the Lavinian shores. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 1.2–3)

Ītaliam vēnit.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| ***Ītalia*** (Italy) is a country, not a town. "I'm going to Italy. I'm in Italy." | ***In Ītaliam eō. In Ītaliā sum.*** Countries take prepositions, like rooms and gardens. |
| Chapter 28 gave you the *Aeneid*'s first line, *Arma virumque canō, Trōiae quī prīmus ab ōrīs*. Lines 2–3 go on: *Ītaliam, fātō profugus, Lāvīniaque vēnit / lītora.* (*Fātum*: fate; *profugus*: an exile, a refugee; *Lāvīnius*: of Lavinium, Aeneas's first town in Italy; *lītus, lītoris*: shore, Chapter 16.) | "…who first from the shores of Troy came, an exile by fate, **to Italy** and the Lavinian **shores**." Two goals in the bare accusative: *Ītaliam* and *lītora*. |
| A country with no *in*? Is Vergil breaking the Law? | **Poets may.** Poetry often drops the preposition with any goal. Prose would say *in Ītaliam … ad lītora Lāvīnia*. Read the bare accusative after a verb of motion as "to." |
| Acts 18:1, Saint Paul leaving Athens: *Post haec ēgressus ab Athēnīs, vēnit Corinthum.* (*Post haec*: after these things; *ēgredior, ēgressus sum*: go out, a Chapter 43 deponent.) | "After this he left **Athens** and came **to Corinth**." *Corinthum*: bare accusative, like Horace's. *Ab Athēnīs*: the Bible's Latin adds *ab*; Cicero would usually write the bare *Athēnīs*. Both mean "from Athens." |

## 6. When: a point in time

:: Tertiā diē resurrēxit. || On the third day he rose again. (the Apostles' Creed)

Hōrā tertiā. Diē tertiā. Nocte.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 22's Law of When: "at the third hour." Chapter 23: "on the third day." | ***Hōrā tertiā. Diē tertiā*** (or *diē tertiō*). The ablative, no preposition: Chapter 35's old "in / at" job. |
| "At night. In summer. In the year of the Lord." | ***Nocte. Aestāte. Annō Dominī.*** All bare ablatives of time when. |
| Luke 24:7, the angels at the empty tomb, quoting Jesus: *Oportet Fīlium hominis trādī … et crucifīgī, et diē tertiā resurgere.* (*Trādō*: hand over; *crucifīgō*: crucify; *resurgō*: rise again.) | "The Son of Man **must** be handed over … and be crucified, and **on the third day** rise again." Chapter 44's *oportet* + accusative + infinitive, Chapter 30's passive infinitives, and an ablative of time. The Creed says it too: ***tertiā diē resurrēxit ā mortuīs***, "on the third day he rose from the dead." |
| Chapter 40 named a year by its consuls: *M. Messallā, M. Pīsōne cōnsulibus*. Which case marks the time there? | The **ablative** again: an ablative absolute, "when Messalla and Piso were consuls" (61 BC). A point in time, in Latin, is almost always an ablative. |
| Past ten: ***ūndecimus*** (eleventh), ***duodecimus*** (twelfth), ***tertius decimus*** (thirteenth: "third-tenth"). Both halves of *tertius decimus* decline. "On the thirteenth day"? | ***Diē tertiā decimā.*** The story's day count (*diē duodecimā* last chapter) uses exactly this. |

## 7. Within which: a window in time

:: Quadrāgintā et sex annīs aedificātum est templum hoc. || This temple was built in forty-six years. (John 2:20)

Tribus diēbus veniam.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Tribus diēbus veniam.* Is that "on the three days"? | No: "**I'll come within three days**," in three days' time. The ablative can mark a **window** as well as a point: somewhere inside those three days. |
| Grammar books call this the **ablative of time within which**. Plain words? | "**The 'within' ablative**": the stretch of time inside which something happens. Same case as Chapter 22's time when; English needs "within" or "in." |
| John 2:20. Jesus has said "Destroy this temple, and I'll raise it in three days." The crowd answers: *Quadrāgintā et sex annīs aedificātum est templum hoc.* (*Quadrāgintā*: forty; *sex*: six; *aedificō*: build, Chapter 40.) | "This temple **was built in forty-six years**": it took forty-six years. *Annīs*: within which. *Aedificātum est*: Chapter 31's perfect passive. |
| *Paucī, paucae, pauca*: few. "Grandma makes a cheese in a few days." | ***Avia paucīs diēbus cāseum facit.*** |

## 8. How long: a stretch of time

:: Tot annōs bella gerō. || For so many years I have been waging wars. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 1.47–48)

Trēs hōrās dormīvī.

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 22 left a note: duration "often uses the **accusative**," *multōs annōs*, "fuller treatment later (Book IV)." Here it is. *Multōs annōs Rōmae habitābat.* | "He lived at Rome **for many years**." How long? The **accusative**, no preposition. |
| "Grandpa slept for three hours." "The dog barked all night." | ***Avus trēs hōrās dormīvit. Canis tōtam noctem lātrāvit.*** The accusative covers the whole stretch. |
| Grammar books call this the **accusative of extent**. Plain words? | "**The 'how long, how far' accusative**": it measures a stretch of time (or, in section 10, of space). Older books say "accusative of duration of time." |
| Vergil, *Aeneid* 1.47–48. Juno, queen of the gods, complains: *ūnā cum gente tot annōs / bella gerō.* (*Ūnā cum gente*: with one nation, Chapter 34's *gēns*; *tot*: so many; *bellum gerō*: wage war, Chapter 37's *gerō*.) | "With one nation, **for so many years**, I wage wars." English says "**I have been** waging wars." A Latin present + a stretch of time = English "have been ___ing." |
| *Per* (Chapter 8: through) can lean on the stretch: *per trēs annōs*. | "**Throughout** three years," all through them. Plain *trēs annōs* says the same with less push. |
| *Hōrā tertiā* vs *trēs hōrās*? | ***Hōrā tertiā***: **at** the third hour (a point; ablative). ***Trēs hōrās***: **for** three hours (a stretch; accusative). |

## 9. *Quot annōs nātus es?*

:: Quot annōs nātus es? || How old are you? (teaching line)

Vīgintī annōs nātus sum.

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 43's *nātus*, "born." *Quot annōs nātus es?* Word by word? | "**Born how many years** are you?" = "**How old are you?**" The years are a stretch of time: accusative. To a woman or girl: *Quot annōs nāta es?* |
| "I'm twenty years old." (***Vīgintī***: twenty.) | ***Vīgintī annōs nātus sum*** (a woman: *nāta sum*). |
| "Marcus is ten." "Cornelia is eight." | ***Marcus decem annōs nātus est. Cornēlia octō annōs nāta est.*** |
| "John, how old are you?" Ask it, then answer it for yourself (the tens are in the box below). | ***Iōannēs, quot annōs nātus es?*** · *___ annōs nātus sum.* Add units with *et*: *quadrāgintā et sex*, like John 2:20, or put them first: *sex et quadrāgintā*. |

!!! info "The tens"
    | | | | |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | 10 *decem* | 20 *vīgintī* | 30 *trīgintā* | 40 *quadrāgintā* |
    | 50 *quīnquāgintā* | 60 *sexāgintā* | 70 *septuāgintā* | 80 *octōgintā* |
    | 90 *nōnāgintā* | 100 *centum* | | |

    Like *quattuor* to *decem*, they never change their endings. Twenty-one: *ūnus et vīgintī* or *vīgintī ūnus*.

## 10. How far: a stretch of space

:: Mīlia passuum XVIIII mūrum … fossamque perdūcit. || He runs a wall and a ditch for nineteen miles. (Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.8.1)

Decem mīlia passuum.

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| ***Passus, passūs*** (m., Chapter 23's fourth family): a pace, a Roman double step, about five feet. *Mīlle passūs*? | "**A thousand paces**" = a Roman **mile** (about 0.9 of an English mile). English *mile* comes from *mīlle*. |
| Chapter 22: thousands are *mīlia* + genitive. "Ten miles"? | ***Decem mīlia passuum***: "ten thousands of paces." *Passuum*: genitive plural, fourth family. |
| "We walked ten miles." Which case is *mīlia*? | ***Decem mīlia passuum ambulāvimus.*** The **accusative of extent** again: how far. |
| "The wall is ten feet high." (*Pēs, pedis*: foot, Chapter 14; *altus*: high.) | ***Mūrus decem pedēs altus est.*** How high, how long, how wide: the accusative. |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.8.1. To stop the Helvetii, Caesar builds a wall from Lake Geneva to the Jura mountains: *mīlia passuum XVIIII mūrum … fossamque perdūcit.* (*Mūrus*: wall; *fossa*: ditch; *perdūcō*: run, extend; *XVIIII*: nineteen, the way Caesar's manuscripts write it.) | "He runs a wall and a ditch **for nineteen miles**." *Mīlia*: accusative of extent. |

!!! success "The Law of the Point and the Stretch"
    A **point** or a **window** takes the **ablative**; a **stretch** takes the **accusative**.

    | question | case | example | English |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | *Quandō?* when? | ablative | *hōrā tertiā, diē tertiā, nocte* | at, on, in |
    | *Quot diēbus?* within how long? | ablative | *tribus diēbus* | within, in |
    | *Quam diū?* how long? | accusative | *trēs hōrās, multōs annōs* | for |
    | how far? how high? | accusative | *decem mīlia passuum, decem pedēs* | for, (no word) |

    No preposition in any of them. *Per* + accusative may push "all through."

## 11. Ago, before, after

:: Abhinc trēs annōs. || Three years ago. (teaching line)

Ante cēnam. Post cēnam.

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| ***Abhinc*** (*ab* + *hinc*, "from here"): ago. *Abhinc trēs annōs Rōmae erāmus.* | "**Three years ago** we were at Rome." *Abhinc* + the accusative of extent: counting back from now. |
| Chapter 43: *Avus post cēnam dormīre solet.* Now meet ***post*** and ***ante*** as prepositions with the **accusative**: after, before. "Before dinner"? | ***Ante cēnam.*** "After three days": ***post trēs diēs.*** |
| Chapter 35's *paulō post*, "a little later" (later by a little). Now: *tribus diēbus post*. | "**Three days later**": later by three days. Chapter 35's ablative of degree of difference, with *post* as a how-word. Also *paucīs post diēbus*, "a few days later." |
| Acts 18:1's *Post haec*? | "**After these things**," after this. *Haec*: neuter accusative plural, Chapter 19. |
| *Post trēs diēs* vs *tribus diēbus post* vs *abhinc trēs diēs*? | "**After** three days" (preposition) · "three days **later**" (how-word + by how much) · "three days **ago**" (counting back from today). |

!!! success "The Law of Ago and Later"
    | Latin | case | English |
    |---|---|---|
    | *abhinc trēs annōs* | accusative | three years ago |
    | *post trēs diēs* / *ante trēs diēs* | accusative (preposition) | after / before three days |
    | *tribus diēbus post* / *ante* | ablative (by how much) | three days later / earlier |

    *Ante* and *post* with an accusative are prepositions; on their own they are how-words, and Chapter 35's "by how much" ablative tells you how much later or earlier.

## 12. Ask the right question

:: Quō vādis? || Where are you going? (John 13:36)

Quō? Ubi? Unde? Quandō? Quam diū?

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Rōmā veniō.* Ask the question it answers. | ***Unde venīs?*** Where are you coming from? |
| *Athēnīs habitō.* | ***Ubi habitās?*** Where do you live? (Same *Athēnīs* could answer *Unde?* with a verb of coming.) |
| *Hōrā sextā cēnāmus.* | ***Quandō cēnātis?*** When do y'all have dinner? |
| *Tōtam noctem vigilāvī.* (*Vigilō*: stay awake, Chapter 39.) | ***Quam diū vigilāvistī?*** How long did you stay awake? (*Quam diū*: Chapter 19.) |
| *Quīnque mīlia passuum ambulāvimus.* | ***Quot mīlia passuum ambulāvistis?*** How many miles did y'all walk? |

## 13. Look-alikes and traps

:: Nōn "in Rōmā," sed "Rōmae." || Not "in Rōmā," but "Rōmae." (teaching line)

Rōmae? Rōmā? Rōmam?

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Rōmae · Rōmā · Rōmam.* | "**At** Rome" (locative) · "**from** Rome" (ablative) · "**to** Rome" (accusative). |
| *Athēnīs sum · Athēnīs veniō.* | "I'm **at** Athens" · "I'm coming **from** Athens." Same form: the verb decides. |
| *Hōrā tertiā · trēs hōrās · tribus hōrīs.* | "**At** the third hour" (point) · "**for** three hours" (stretch) · "**within** three hours" (window). |
| *Domī · domum.* | "**At** home" · "(to) home." *Domī maneō* (I stay home) · *Domum eō* (I go home). English says "home" for both. |
| *Mīlitiae* in *domī mīlitiaeque* vs *Lūcās dē mīlitiae caelestis …*? Pick the job. | With *domī*: the **locative**, "in the field." After a noun, "of the army": the **genitive**. |

## 14. Fix it

:: Errāre hūmānum est. || To err is human. (later saying; Chapter 41)

In Rōmā? Rōmae!

{#g-14}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "I live in Rome": *In Rōmā habitō.* | ***Rōmae habitō.*** A town: locative, no *in*. |
| "We're going to Athens": *Ad Athēnās īmus.* | ***Athēnās īmus.*** (*Ad Athēnās* would mean "toward Athens," to its outskirts.) |

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| John: *omnēs domī sumus. … canis humī sedet.* Find two locatives. | ***Domī***, at home, and ***humī***, on the ground. Neither has *in*. And *vesperī*, in the evening, makes three. |
| Grandpa: *Abhinc quīnquāgintā annōs mīles eram. Decem annōs mīlitiae fuī.* Sort the time words. | ***Abhinc quīnquāgintā annōs***: fifty years **ago**. ***Decem annōs***: **for** ten years (a stretch, accusative). *Mīlitiae*: locative, "in the field." |
| *Rōmae, Corinthī, Athēnīs, Carthāgine.* Why four different endings for one idea? | Each is "**at** ___": *Rōmae* and *Corinthī* look like genitives (singular first and second family); *Athēnīs* (plural) and *Carthāgine* (third family) look like ablatives. |
| *Rōmā Brundisium ambulāvimus … Brundisiō Athēnās nāvigāvimus.* No prepositions at all. Why? | All towns: from Rome (ablative), to Brundisium (accusative), from Brundisium (ablative), to Athens (accusative plural). |
| Cornelia: *Quot diēbus?* Grandpa: *Decem diēbus! Cotīdiē trīgintā mīlia passuum ambulābāmus.* Check his sums. | ***Decem diēbus***: within ten days (ablative). ***Trīgintā mīlia passuum***: how far, accusative. Ten days × thirty miles = three hundred miles. For once Grandpa's arithmetic is right. |
| Grandma: *domī mīlitiaeque cāseum edis!* Grandpa: *Rōmae rūs optābam, rūrī Rōmam.* Whose line is Grandpa borrowing? | **Horace's slave Davus** (section 3): *Rōmae rūs optās.* Grandpa turns it into a confession: never happy where he is, except with cheese. |
