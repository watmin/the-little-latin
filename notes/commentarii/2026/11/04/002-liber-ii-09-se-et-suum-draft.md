# Caput XXI: Sē et Suum

*Liber II, Rēs Cotīdiānae · Chapter 21*

*In which "I / me / to me / with me" finally gets its full set, the third person looks back at itself, and "his" splits into two Latin words. Chapter 11 lined up* ego, mē, mihi, mēcum *and promised the rest; Chapter 19's* liber eius *was "someone else's," and today you meet* liber suus*, "his own." Grandpa washes himself (not the dog), and somebody eats the wrong cheese.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Ego mē lavō.* · *Avus sē lavat.* · *Marcus librum suum legit.* · *Liber eius.* · *Nōsce tē.* · *Ipse dīxit.* · *Suum cuique.*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** personal pronoun (I, you, we, y'all); reflexive pronoun (points back to the subject: himself, herself, themselves); intensive (adds "himself / the very" for stress/emphasis)
    - **Forms:** *ego, meī, mihi, mē*; *tū, tuī, tibi, tē*; *nōs, nostrum/nostrī, nōbīs*; *vōs, vestrum/vestrī, vōbīs*; *mēcum, tēcum, nōbīscum, vōbīscum, sēcum*; *sē, suī, sibi*; *suus, sua, suum* vs *eius*; *ipse, ipsa, ipsum* (*ipsīus, ipsī*)
    - **Laws:** the Law of Looking Back
    - **Words:** *lavō, nōscō, sē, suus, ipse, sibi, suī, sēcum, nōbīscum, vōbīscum, nostrum, vestrum, meī, tuī*
    - **Reading:** *Cāseus suus?* (a bath, a book, and the wrong cheese)
    - **Speaking:** wash yourself, say whose book/cheese it is, point with *ipse*

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep Chapter 5's *ego, tū, nōs, vōs*, Chapter 7's *mēcum, tēcum*, Chapter 11's sort *ego, mē, mihi, mēcum*, and Chapter 19's *eius* ("someone else's") within reach. Today those threads meet.

## 1. *Ego, meī, mihi, mē*

:: Nōsce tē. || Know yourself. (Cicero's Latin for Delphi's *gnōthi seauton*; *Tusculan Disputations* 1.52. Later writers often add *ipsum*.)

Ego sum. Mē vidēs. Mihi cāseum dās. Venī mēcum!

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 11 sorted *ego, mē, mihi, mēcum* and said the full set waits here. Read the blurb. Name each job. | *Ego*: I (subject). *Mē*: me (object). *Mihi*: to/for me. *Mēcum*: with me (Chapter 7 glued *cum* on the end). |
| What's missing from that sort? Every noun you've declined has an "of" form. | The genitive: ***meī***, "of me." *Amīcus meī*: a friend of me (rarer than *amīcus meus*; see §2). |
| So chant *ego* across: subject, of, to/for, object, by/with/from. | *Ego, meī, mihi, mē, mē.* Subject *ego*; of *meī*; to/for *mihi*; object and with/from both ***mē***. No separate "with" form: *cum* glues on → *mēcum*. |
| Now *tū*. Guess from what you already know. | *Tū, tuī, tibi, tē, tē.* And *tēcum*. "You (one person), of you, to/for you, you (object), by/with/from you." |
| "You love me." "I love you." (*Amō, amāre*.) | *Mē amās.* · *Tē amō.* Objects: *mē, tē*. |
| "Give me the cheese!" "I give you the cheese." | *Dā mihi cāseum!* · *Tibi cāseum dō.* Datives: *mihi, tibi* (Chapter 11). |
| The motto, *Nōsce tē.* (*Nōscō, nōscere*: get to know, learn.) Word for word? | "Know **yourself**" (literally "know you"). *Tē* is the object, pointing back to the same person as the command. Cicero reports Apollo's precept as *nōsce tē*; he explains it as *nōsce animum tuum*, "know your mind." |
| Later Latin often writes *Nōsce tē ipsum.* What did *ipsum* add? (You'll meet *ipse* properly in §6.) | Stress (emphasis): "know **you yourself**," "know your very self." Classical Latin was content with *nōsce tē*. |
| "Of me" in a sentence: *pars meī* ("a part of me") is possible, but for "my book"? | *Liber meus.* Everyday ownership uses the possessive adjective (*meus*), not the pronoun genitive (*meī*). |
| Ablative without *cum*: *ā mē*, "from me"; *prō mē*, "for me / on my behalf." | Same *mē* as the object form. *Cum* is the one that glues: only *mēcum*, never *cum mē* in ordinary prose. |

!!! tip "Personal pronouns"
    A **personal pronoun** is a short word that stands in for a person: *I, you, we, y'all*. Latin: *ego, tū, nōs, vōs*. They change form by job (case), just as nouns do, but they don't have a gender of their own the way *bonus* does.

## 2. *Nōs, vōs* — and *nostrum*

:: Nōn nōbīs, Domine. || Not unto us, O Lord. (Psalm 115:1 / Vulgate 113:9, opening)

Nōs sumus. Nōs vidēs. Nōbīs cāseum dās. Venīte nōbīscum!

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Read the blurb. Match each form to a job, from Chapter 5 and Chapter 11. | *Nōs*: we (subject) **and** us (object) — same shape, two jobs. *Nōbīs*: to/for us. *Nōbīscum*: with us. |
| Chapter 7 gave you *mēcum, tēcum*. Extend the glue to "us" and "y'all." | ***Nōbīscum*** · ***vōbīscum***. Always *cum* on the end: never *cum nōbīs* in ordinary prose. |
| Chant *nōs* across. | *Nōs, nostrum (or nostrī), nōbīs, nōs, nōbīs.* Subject and object both *nōs*; to/for and with/from both *nōbīs*. |
| Two "of us" forms: ***nostrum*** and ***nostrī***. Different jobs. | ***Nostrum*** after a number or "many/few" (*duo nostrum*, "two of us"; *multi nostrum*). ***Nostrī*** elsewhere ("of us," "our people"). Same split for *vestrum / vestrī* ("of y'all"). |
| Don't confuse *nostrī* (of us) with *noster, nostra, nostrum* (our). | *Noster* is the possessive adjective (Chapter 9), like *meus*: *liber noster*, "our book." *Nostrī* is the pronoun's genitive. *Liber nostrī* would mean "a book of us," which Latin almost never wants — it wants *liber noster*. |
| Same trap for *meī / meus* and *tuī / tuus*. | *Meus, mea, meum* = my (agrees with the thing owned). *Meī* = of me (the pronoun). *Amīcus meus* is the everyday "my friend"; *amīcus meī* is rare. |
| "Y'all are here." "I see y'all." "I give cheese to y'all." "Come with us!" | *Vōs hīc estis. Vōs videō. Vōbīs cāseum dō. Venīte nōbīscum!* |
| Full table so far (singular left, plural right): | See the success box below. |
| The motto, *Nōn nōbīs, Domine.* | "Not **to us**, Lord" (the glory). *Nōbīs*: dative plural. Chapter 11 already used this line; now you see *nōbīs* in the full set. |
| "Two of us are here." Which "of us"? | *Duo nostrum hīc sunt.* Partitive → ***nostrum***. (*Duo* is a number; fuller number-work waits for Chapter 22.) |
| Object *nōs* vs dative *nōbīs*: "He sees us." "He gives us cheese." | *Nōs videt. Nōbīs cāseum dat.* |

!!! success "*Ego* and *tū*; *nōs* and *vōs*"
    | | I | you (one) | we | you all |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | **subject** | *ego* | *tū* | *nōs* | *vōs* |
    | **of** | *meī* | *tuī* | *nostrum / nostrī* | *vestrum / vestrī* |
    | **to/for** | *mihi* | *tibi* | *nōbīs* | *vōbīs* |
    | **object** | *mē* | *tē* | *nōs* | *vōs* |
    | **by/with/from** | *mē* | *tē* | *nōbīs* | *vōbīs* |
    | **with…** | *mēcum* | *tēcum* | *nōbīscum* | *vōbīscum* |

    No vocative column: when you call someone, you use a name or *tū* / *vōs*. Chapter 9's *Marce!* still does that job.

## 3. *Sē* — looking back

:: Manus manum lavat. || One hand washes the other. (Petronius, *Satyricon* 45; Chapter 4)

Avus sē lavat. Avus eum lavat. Ego mē lavō. Tū tē lavās.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Read the blurb. *Lavō, lavāre*: wash (Chapter 4's *lavat*). Who is Grandpa washing in *Avus sē lavat*? | Himself. *Sē* points **back to the subject**. |
| And in *Avus eum lavat*? | Someone else: "him" (Chapter 19's *is, ea, id* → *eum*). Grandpa washes another person (or the dog). |
| *Ego mē lavō. Tū tē lavās.* Why not *sē*? | For "I" and "you," Latin reuses *mē* and *tē*. ***Sē* is only for a third-person subject** (he / she / it / they) looking back at itself. |
| "Marcus washes himself." "The girls wash themselves." | *Marcus sē lavat. Puellae sē lavant.* Same *sē* for one or many. |
| Does *sē* have a nominative — a subject form? | **No.** It always points back to a subject that is already there. You never say "*Sē lavat*" alone to mean "He washes"; you need *Avus* or *is* (or just *lavat*, with the ending carrying "he"). |
| The other forms of *sē*, matching *mē*: of, to/for, object, with/from, with. | ***Suī, sibi, sē, sē, sēcum.*** Of himself *suī*; to/for himself *sibi*; object and with/from *sē*; with himself *sēcum*. |
| "He gives himself a cheese." "She talks with herself." (*Dīcō* for "talk/say," roughly.) | *Sibi cāseum dat. Sēcum dīcit.* |
| A made twin of Chapter 20's Bias line: *Omnia sua sēcum portat.* | "He carries all **his own** things **with him**." (*Sua* belongs with §4; *sēcum* is today's glue.) Bias himself said *omnia mēcum portō mea* (first person). This third-person twin is a teaching example, not a second ancient quote. |
| Grammar name? | ***Sē* is a reflexive pronoun**: it *reflects* (from *reflectere*, bend back) to the subject. Old-school: "the reflexive." Plain English: "himself / herself / itself / themselves" when the same person is subject and object. |
| Chapter 4's motto again: *Manus manum lavat.* Is anyone washing **themselves**? | No: one hand washes **another** hand. Object *manum*, not *sē*. Today's *Avus sē lavat* is the new move. |
| "We wash ourselves." "Y'all wash yourselves." | *Nōs nōs lavāmus. Vōs vōs lavātis.* First/second person reuse *nōs/vōs*; no *sē*. |

!!! tip "Reflexive"
    A **reflexive** word points back to the subject of its own clause. English: *he washes **himself***, *she sees **herself***. Latin's third-person reflexive is *sē* (and *suus*, next). First and second person just reuse *mē / tē / nōs / vōs*.

## 4. *Suus* or *eius*?

:: Suum cuique. || To each his own. (Cicero, *On Duties* 1.15, *tribuendoque suum cuique*; *cuique* "to each" is a chunk for now → Chapter 29)

Marcus librum suum legit. Marcus librum eius legit.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 19: *Liber eius* = "his/her book," someone else's. Read the blurb. What's the new partner? | ***Suus, sua, suum***: "his own / her own / their own." It agrees like *meus* (Chapter 9): *liber suus, mēnsa sua, corpus suum*. |
| *Marcus librum suum legit.* Whose book? | Marcus's **own** book. *Suus* looks back to the subject, Marcus. |
| *Marcus librum eius legit.* Whose book? | Someone else's. *Eius* points to a different person (Chapter 19's *is*). |
| "Grandpa eats his own cheese." "Grandpa eats her cheese." (Avia's) | *Avus cāseum suum edit. Avus cāseum eius edit.* |
| "The girls love their (own) dog." "The girls love his dog." (Marcus's) | *Puellae canem suum amant. Puellae canem eius amant.* *Suus* still agrees with *canem* (masculine object), not with "girls." |
| *Suus* has a full adjective set. Give "his/her own" for a feminine subject form and a neuter object plural. | *Sua* (e.g. *fīlia sua*). *Sua* also covers neuter plural objects: *omnia sua*, "all his/her own things" (the Bias twin above). |
| Genitive of the reflexive pronoun *sē* was *suī*. Don't mix it with *suus*. | *Suī* = "of himself" (pronoun). *Suus* = "his own" (adjective, agrees with the thing owned). *Liber suī* is odd; *liber suus* is what you want. Same contrast as *meī / meus*. |
| The motto, *Suum cuique.* | "His own to each" → "to each his own." *Suum*: neuter, "one's own (thing)." *Cuique*: "to each," dative of *quisque* (Chapter 29). Learn the two-word chunk. |

## 5. The Law of Looking Back

:: Marcus librum suum legit, nōn eius. || Marcus reads his own book, not someone else's.

Avus sē lavat et canem suum. Avus eum lavat et canem eius.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Read the blurb. Two sentences, four "him/his." Sort each. | First sentence: *sē* = Grandpa himself; *suum* = Grandpa's own dog. Second: *eum* = another male; *eius* = that other person's dog. |
| Rule in plain words? | When the owner **is the subject**, Latin uses *sē / suus*. When the owner is **somebody else**, Latin uses *eum / eam / id / eius*. |
| "Cornēlia washes herself and calls her (own) brother." | *Cornēlia sē lavat et frātrem suum vocat.* |
| "Cornēlia washes him and calls his brother." (Quīntus, talking about Marcus) | *Cornēlia eum lavat et frātrem eius vocat.* |
| Does *suus* care about the subject's gender? | No. *Avus… suum*, *Avia… suum*, *puellae… suum*: *suus* agrees with **the thing owned**, not with the owner. The "looking back" is in the choice of *suus* vs *eius*, not in the ending. |
| First and second person? | No special reflexive adjective: you already have *meus, tuus, noster, vester*. *Ego librum meum legō. Tū librum tuum legis.* |
| One sentence, both tools: "Grandpa washes himself and his (own) dog." | *Avus sē lavat et canem suum.* Contrast: *Avus eum lavat et canem eius.* |

!!! success "The Law of Looking Back"
    **If it points back to the subject, use *sē / suus*. If it points to somebody else, use *is / eius* (or a name).**

    - *Marcus sē lavat* — Marcus washes himself
    - *Marcus eum lavat* — Marcus washes him (another)
    - *Marcus librum suum legit* — his own book
    - *Marcus librum eius legit* — someone else's book

    *Sē* has no nominative. *Suus* agrees with the thing owned, like *meus*.

## 6. *Ipse* — the very one

:: Ipse dīxit. || He himself said it. (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* 1.10, on the Pythagoreans; Greek *autos epha*)

Ipse Avus sē lavat. Ego ipse mē lavō. Nōsce tē ipsum.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Read the blurb. *Ipse* sits next to *Avus*. What does it add? | Stress (emphasis): "**Grandpa himself** washes himself," "Grandpa in person." Not a different person — the same one, underlined. |
| Grammar name? | An **intensive** (also "emphatic pronoun," "intensive adjective"). Plain English: it adds *himself / herself / itself / the very* for stress. Old grammars say "*ipse* intensifies." |
| Forms: like *ille*, including the Chapter 19 surprises. | *Ipse, ipsa, ipsum*; genitive ***ipsīus*** (all genders); dative ***ipsī***; object *ipsum, ipsam, ipsum*; ablative *ipsō, ipsā, ipsō*. Plurals *ipsī, ipsae, ipsa*… |
| *Ego ipse mē lavō. Tū ipsa tē lavās.* (A girl speaking in the second.) | "I myself wash myself." "You yourself wash yourself." *Ipse* agrees with the person: masculine *ipse*, feminine *ipsa*. |
| *Nōsce tē ipsum* — the later form of §1's motto. What is *ipsum* doing? | Agreeing with *tē* (object, masculine): "know **you yourself**." Intensive, not reflexive — the reflexive work is already done by *tē*. |
| Trap: *ipse* vs *sē*. | *Sē* = the object that is the same person as the subject (*Avus sē lavat*). *Ipse* = stress on whoever it sits with (*ipse Avus*, *Avus ipse*, *mē ipsum*). You can even have both: *Avus ipse sē lavat* — "Grandpa himself washes himself." |
| "The cheese itself is sharp." "I see the woman herself." | *Cāseus ipse ācer est. Fēminam ipsam videō.* |
| *Ipse dīxit* again. Who is *ipse*? | Pythagoras, in Cicero's story: "the master himself said it." |
| *Ipsa Avia cāseum suum edit.* Count the looking-back words. | *Ipsa*: intensive on Avia ("Grandma herself"). *Suum*: her own cheese (reflexive possessive). Two tools, one sentence. |
| Ablative *ipsō / ipsā*: "with the king himself," "by the woman herself." | *Cum rēge ipsō. Ā fēminā ipsā.* *Ipse* agrees in case, number, and gender with the noun it stresses. |

## 7. Mix and catch

:: Omnia sua sēcum portat. || He carries all his own things with him. (teaching twin of Bias; §3)

Ego mē lavō. Avus sē lavat. Marcus librum suum legit, nōn eius.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Translate: *Tēcum veniō. Nōbīscum venīte. Sēcum dīcit.* | "I'm coming with you. Come (y'all) with us. He talks with himself." |
| *Puella sē videt.* · *Puella eam videt.* | "The girl sees herself." · "The girl sees her" (another girl). |
| *Canis suus* · *canis eius* — after *Avia canem ____ amat*. | *Avia canem suum amat* (her own). *Avia canem eius amat* (someone else's). |
| Fix: *Avus sē est.* · *Marcus librum suum eius legit.* · *Ego sē lavō.* · *Cum nōbīs venī!* · *Liber meī in mēnsā est.* | *Avus **ipse** est* or just *Avus est* (*sē* has no nominative). · Pick one: *librum suum* **or** *librum eius*. · *Ego **mē** lavō.* · ***Nōbīscum** venī!* · *Liber **meus** in mēnsā est.* |
| *Omnia mea mēcum portō* (Bias). Make the third-person twin. | *Omnia sua sēcum portat.* |
| *Ipse, ipsīus, ipsī* — which Chapter 19 law is this? | The Law of the Pointing Words: genitive singular *-īus*, dative singular *-ī*, all genders. *Ipse* joins *ille, iste, is, īdem*. |
| Say in Latin: "I wash myself. You wash yourself. He washes himself. We wash ourselves." | *Ego mē lavō. Tū tē lavās. (Is / Avus) sē lavat. Nōs nōs lavāmus.* |
| *Suum cuique.* Gloss and name the untaught word. | "To each his own." *Cuique* = "to each" (dative of *quisque*); chunk until Chapter 29. |

## 8. Lectiō: *Cāseus suus?*

:: Cāseus suus an eius? || His own cheese — or someone else's?

*The morning after the race. The dog is dusty, Grandpa wants a wash, and a cheese on the table has two claimants.*

::: dialogue lectio
Iōannēs | Māne est. Heri Cāseus omnēs vincēbat… et cāseum ācrem edēbat! || John | It's morning. Yesterday Cāseus was beating everyone… and eating the sharp cheese!
Avia | Canis sordidus est. Lavāte eum! || Grandma | The dog is dirty. Wash him!
Marcus | Ego eum lavō! || Marcus | I'll wash him!
Cornēlia | Et ego! || Cornēlia | And I!
Avus | Ego mē lavō. Tum canem lavābō. || Grandpa | I'm washing myself. Then I'll wash the dog.
Avia | Tū ipse, Ave? Fortis es! || Grandma | You yourself, Grandpa? You're strong!
Avus | Ipse mē lavō… Ēheu! Aqua frīgida est! || Grandpa | I myself am washing… Oh, no! The water is cold!
Quīntus | Avus sē lavat! Et nōs canem lavāmus. || Quīntus | Grandpa's washing himself! And we're washing the dog.
Cāseus | Bau! Bau! || Cāseus | Woof! Woof!
Marcus | Tēcum, Cāseus! In aquam! || Marcus | With you, Cāseus! Into the water!
Cornēlia | Canis sē nōn lavat. Nōs eum lavāmus! || Cornēlia | The dog doesn't wash himself. We're washing him!
Pater | Bene. Ego mēcum sāpōnem portō. || Dad | Good. I'm bringing soap with me.
Māter | Et ego mēcum mappam! || Mom | And I'm bringing a towel with me!
Avus | Iam mē lavō! Ubi est cāseus? || Grandpa | I'm washing myself now! Where's the cheese?
Avia | Cāseus ācer heri in stomachō Cāseī erat. Sed ecce: hic cāseus in mēnsā est. || Grandma | The sharp cheese was in Cāseus's stomach yesterday. But look: this cheese is on the table.
Avus | Cāseum meum edō! || Grandpa | I'm eating my cheese!
Quīntus | Meum? Is cāseus meus est! || Quīntus | Your cheese? That cheese is mine!
Avus | Tuus? Minimē! Meus est! || Grandpa | Yours? Not at all! It's mine!
Avia | Avus cāseum suum edit… an cāseum eius? || Grandma | Is Grandpa eating his own cheese… or his (Quīntus's)?
Iōannēs | Cāseum eius! Cāseus Quīntī est! || John | His (someone else's)! It's Quīntus's cheese!
Marcus | Ipse Quīntus dīcit: "Meus est!" || Marcus | Quīntus himself says: "It's mine!"
Quīntus | Ipse dīcō! Meus est! || Quīntus | I myself say it! It's mine!
Avus | Ēheu… Suum cuique. Sed ego… || Grandpa | Oh, no… To each his own. But I…
Avia | Tū cāseum eius edis, Ave. Nōn tuum! || Grandma | You're eating his cheese, Grandpa. Not yours!
Avus | Sed omnia mea mēcum portō… || Grandpa | But I carry all my things with me…
Iōannēs | Hodiē cāseus Quīntī est in stomachō… || John | Today Quīntus's cheese is in the stomach of…
Omnēs | …Avī! || Everyone | …Grandpa!
Avus | Omnia sua sēcum portat… sed nōn cāseum aliēnum! || Grandpa | A man carries all his own things with him… but not somebody else's cheese!
Avia | Bene dīcis, Ave. Ecce cāseus tuus — cāseus tuus ipse! || Grandma | Well said, Grandpa. Here's your cheese — your cheese itself!
Avus | Tandem! Nunc cāseum meum edō. Suum cuique… et mihi meum! || Grandpa | At last! Now I'm eating my cheese. To each his own… and mine to me!
:::

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Ego mē lavō* · *Avus sē lavat* · *Nōs eum lavāmus.* Sort reflexive vs not. | *Mē* and *sē* look back to the subject (I; Grandpa). *Eum* is the dog: not the subject. |
| *Tū ipse, Ave?* · *Ipse mē lavō.* What is *ipse* doing? | Intensive: "you yourself," "I myself." Stress, not a new object. |
| *Canis sē nōn lavat. Nōs eum lavāmus!* | The dog doesn't wash himself; the kids wash him. *Sē* vs *eum* in one breath. |
| *Avus cāseum suum edit… an cāseum eius?* | The whole chapter in one line: his own, or someone else's? |
| *Ipse Quīntus dīcit* · *Ipse dīcō!* | Intensive on Quīntus; then Quīntus echoes Cicero's chunk in the first person: "I myself say (it)." |
| *Omnia sua sēcum portat… sed nōn cāseum aliēnum!* (*Aliēnus*: belonging to another — gloss.) | Grandpa twists the teaching twin: you carry your own things, not another's cheese. |
| *Cāseus tuus ipse!* | "Your cheese itself" / "your very cheese": *ipse* intensive with *cāseus*. |
| End: *in stomachō… Avī!* Why *Avī*, not *Avus*? | Genitive of *avus* (Chapter 10 / 14 pattern): "in Grandpa's stomach." Same gag family as always. |

## 9. Sound check

:: Linguam ipsam nōsce. || Know the tongue itself.

Audī et dīc.

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *ego · meī · mihi · mē · mēcum*? | EH-go · MEH-ee · MI-hee · may · MAY-kum. Short *e* in *ego*; *mihi* often sounds almost MEE-hee in poetry, but learn MI-hee. |
| *tū · tuī · tibi · tē · tēcum*? | too · TOO-ee · TI-bi · tay · TAY-kum. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking.

1. Chant *ego* and *tū*: *ego, meī, mihi, mē, mē · mēcum* · *tū, tuī, tibi, tē, tē · tēcum*.
2. Chant *nōs* and *vōs*, including the glue: *nōbīscum, vōbīscum*.
3. Chant *sē*: *suī, sibi, sē, sē · sēcum*. Then *suus, sua, suum* like *meus*.
4. Minimal pairs, out loud: *Avus sē lavat / Avus eum lavat* · *librum suum / librum eius* · *cāseum suum / cāseum eius*.
5. Intensives: *ego ipse · tū ipsa · Avus ipse · cāseus ipse · Nōsce tē · Nōsce tē ipsum · Ipse dīxit*.
6. Old lines, cracked open: *Omnia mea mēcum portō · Omnia sua sēcum portat · Nōn nōbīs, Domine · Suum cuique · Manus manum lavat*.
7. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part. Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
