# Caput XX: Fortis et Celer

*Liber II, Rēs Cotīdiānae · Chapter 20*

*In which you finally get to say "sweet." Back in Chapter 3 you asked for* dulcis *and were told "not yet": it belongs to another family of adjectives. Today that family arrives: words like* fortis *(brave),* celer *(fast), and* fēlīx *(lucky). They come from the third family, so they don't rhyme with the nouns they describe, and that's fine: an adjective matches its noun's gender, number, and case, not its letters. Then the family describes itself, and somebody runs a race for a cheese.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Quālis est?* · *Fortis est!* · *Mel dulce est.* · *Crūra mea brevia sunt!* · *Fortēs fortūna adiuvat.*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** two-ending, three-ending, and one-ending adjectives (how many naming forms an adjective has)
    - **Forms:** *dulcis, dulce*; *fortis, forte*; *ācer, ācris, ācre*; *celer, celeris, celere*; *fēlīx, fēlīcis*; the i-stem endings *-ī, -ia, -ium*; mixed pairs like *rēx bonus* and *fēmina fortis*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Two Families, the Law of the Three Shapes
    - **Words:** *ācer, celer, fortis, dulcis, omnis, brevis, trīstis, gravis, facilis, quālis, fēlīx, ingēns, pār, sapiēns, fēmina, fortūna, ars, adiuvō, vincō*
    - **Reading:** *Quis vincet?* (a race around the house, with a cheese for the prize)
    - **Speaking:** describe people: what they're like, and what you're like

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep two old friends handy: Chapter 3's *bonus, bona, bonum* and Chapter 15's *mare, marī, maria*. Today's adjectives are built from the second one, and they sit next to nouns of every family.

---

## 1. *Dulcis, dulce*

:: Dulce et decōrum est prō patriā morī. || It is sweet and fitting to die for your country. (Horace, *Odes* 3.2.13)

Mel dulce est. Ūva dulcis est. Cāseus nōn dulcis est!

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 3: "'Sweet' is *dulcis*. Can we use it yet?" The answer was "not yet." Read the blurb. Which words are "sweet"? | *Dulce* and *dulcis*: "Honey is sweet. The grape is sweet. Cheese isn't sweet!" Today it's "yes." |
| Genders: *mel*, *ūva*, *cāseus*? | *Mel* is neuter (Chapter 14), *ūva* feminine, *cāseus* masculine. |
| So how many naming forms does "sweet" have? | Two. ***Dulcis*** for masculine **and** feminine (*cāseus dulcis, ūva dulcis*); ***dulce*** for neuter (*mel dulce*). *Bonus, bona, bonum* had three. |
| Where have you seen *-is* and a neuter in *-e* before? | In the third family: *ovis* (Chapter 15) and *mare*. *Dulcis* is a **third-family adjective**. |
| What should we call an adjective like *dulcis, dulce*? | A **two-ending adjective**: two naming forms, one for masculine and feminine together, one for neuter. Old grammars say "adjective of two terminations" (*termination* = ending). The dictionary writes it *dulcis, dulce*. |
| *Fortis, forte*: brave, strong. "A brave boy. A brave girl. A strong body." | *Puer fortis. Puella fortis. Corpus forte.* *Corpus* is neuter (Chapter 14's Disguise), so ***forte***. |
| "Sweet wine. Sweet water. A sweet apple." | *Vīnum dulce. Aqua dulcis. Mālum dulce.* |
| Many: *Ūvae dulcēs sunt. Māla dulcia sunt.* Which old endings are these? | ***-ēs*** like *rēgēs* (masculine and feminine), and ***-ia*** like *maria* (neuter). "The grapes are sweet. The apples are sweet." |
| The motto, *Dulce et decōrum est prō patriā morī.* (*Decōrus*: fitting; *prō patriā*: for your country, Chapter 13; *morī*: to die, an odd infinitive for later.) Why is *dulce* neuter? | Its subject is "to die," an infinitive: a thing, not a person, so neuter. *Decōrum* is neuter too, from the *bonus* family. Two families, one neuter. Horace meant it; Wilfred Owen, a soldier-poet of the First World War, called it "the old Lie." |

## 2. *Rēx bonus, fēmina fortis*

:: Mēns sāna in corpore sānō. || A healthy mind in a healthy body. (Juvenal, *Satires* 10.356)

Rēx bonus. Māter bona. Nōmen bonum.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 13 made a choice: on third-family nouns, only *meus* and friends for now (*Patrī meō*). Read the blurb. Do the endings rhyme? | No: *rēx bon****us***, *māter bon****a***, *nōmen bon****um***. The adjective follows the noun's **gender** (masculine, feminine, neuter), not its letters. Chapter 3's Law of the Family (not the Rhyme). |
| "Of the good king." (*Rēx, rēgis*.) | *Rēgis bonī.* Not "*bonis*": *bonus* keeps its own family's "of" ending, ***-ī***. |
| "To the good king. I see the good king. With the good king." | *Rēgī bonō. Rēgem bonum videō. Cum rēge bonō.* The noun says it the third-family way, the adjective the *bonus* way. Same case each time. |
| Chapter 14's motto, *Mēns sāna in corpore sānō.* *Corpore* ends in *-e*, *sānō* in *-ō*. Do they agree? | Yes. Both are ablative (after *in*), singular, neuter. Chapter 14 promised this: same case, not same letters. *Mēns sāna* agrees too: feminine, subject. |
| Now the mirror: a *bonus*-family noun, a third-family adjective. *Fēmina, fēminae* (f.): woman (Chapter 13's *Dux fēmina factī*). "A brave woman." | *Fēmina fortis.* |
| "I see a brave woman." "The brave woman's book." | *Fēminam fortem videō. Liber fēminae fortis.* The noun uses the first family's *-am, -ae*; the adjective uses the third's *-em, -is*. |
| "Brave women." "Good kings." | *Fēminae fortēs. Rēgēs bonī.* |
| Now both from the third family: "a brave king," "of a brave king." Do they rhyme now? | *Rēx fortis, rēgis fortis*: this time, yes. But don't trust it. In the next section their "with" forms part ways: *cum rēge fortī*. |
| Two adjectives, two families, one noun: "Grandpa is good and brave." "Grandma is good and brave." | *Avus bonus et fortis est. Avia bona et fortis est.* *Bonus* changes with the gender; *fortis* doesn't need to. |

!!! success "The Law of the Two Families"
    **A noun and its adjective share gender, number, and case, not letters.** Each keeps its own family's endings:

    - *rēx bonus* · *rēgis bonī* · *rēgem bonum*
    - *fēmina fortis* · *fēminae fortis* · *fēminam fortem*
    - *in corpore sānō*

    Ask "what gender, number, and case is the noun?" Then give the adjective **its own** ending for that.

## 3. *Fortī, fortia, fortium*

:: Omnia mea mēcum portō. || I carry all my things with me. (Bias of Priene; Cicero, *Paradoxes of the Stoics* 1.8, has *omnia mēcum portō mea*)

Cum puerō fortī. Cum puellā fortī. Cum rēge fortī.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Read the blurb. Three nouns, three different "with" endings (*-ō, -ā, -e*). What stays the same? | ***Fortī***, every time: "with a brave boy, a brave girl, a brave king." The adjective's "with/from" form ends in long ***-ī***. Even next to *rēge*. |
| Why *-ī*, not *-e* like *rēge*? Think of Chapter 15's *in marī*. | These adjectives are **i-stems** all the way, like *mare* (the Law of the Sea): with/from one ***-ī***, many (neuter) ***-ia***, of many ***-ium***. |
| "Strong bodies." | *Corpora fortia.* The noun's neuter plural *-a*, the adjective's *-ia*. Same case, same number, same gender. |
| "Of the brave soldiers." (*Mīles, mīlitis*.) | *Mīlitum fortium.* *Mīlitum* is a plain consonant stem (*-um*); *fortium* is an i-stem (*-ium*). |
| The rest is just *rēx*: *fortis, fortī, fortem*; *fortēs, fortibus*. "I give cheese to a brave soldier." | *Mīlitī fortī cāseum dō.* Here the two *-ī* endings match, because both are datives. |
| Chapter 8's *Omnēs viae Rōmam dūcunt*: "*omnēs* belongs to a family you meet in Book II." Which family, and which form? | This one: ***omnis, omne***, "every" (one), "all" (many). *Omnēs* is feminine plural with *viae*: "**All** roads lead to Rome." |
| Chapter 4's *Omnia vincit Amor.* What is *omnia* now? | The neuter plural of *omnis*, with the i-stem ***-ia***, standing alone: "all things." "Love conquers **all**." (Vergil, *Eclogues* 10.69) |
| Chapter 15's *Gallia est omnis dīvīsa in partēs trēs*, and Chapter 18's *Nōn omnia possumus omnēs*. Match each *omnis* to its partner. | *Omnis* is feminine singular with *Gallia*: "Gaul, **as a whole**, is divided into three parts." *Omnia*: neuter plural object, "all things"; *omnēs*: masculine plural subject, "all of us." |
| The motto, *Omnia mea mēcum portō.* Two adjectives on one invisible noun. | *Omnia* and *mea*: both neuter plural objects, "all my things." Two families again: *omn-ia*, *me-a*. When his city fell, everyone else fled with armfuls of belongings; Bias took nothing. What he carried was in his head. |

!!! success "*Fortis, forte*: the third-family adjective"
    | | m. / f. | n. | | m. / f. | n. |
    |---|---|---|---|---|---|
    | **subject** | *fortis* | *forte* | | *fortēs* | ***fortia*** |
    | **of** | *fortis* | *fortis* | | ***fortium*** | ***fortium*** |
    | **to/for** | *fortī* | *fortī* | | *fortibus* | *fortibus* |
    | **object** | *fortem* | *forte* | | *fortēs* | ***fortia*** |
    | **by/with/from** | ***fortī*** | ***fortī*** | | *fortibus* | *fortibus* |

    Singular on the left, plural on the right. The bold forms are the i-stem ones; compare *mare, marī, maria, marium*. (The object plural *fortēs* can also be spelled *fortīs*, Chapter 15's old *-īs*.)

## 4. *Ācer, ācris, ācre*

:: Cāseus ācer, vīnum ācre, mēns ācris. || A sharp cheese, a sharp wine, a sharp mind.

Cāseus ācer. Mēns ācris. Vīnum ācre.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Read the blurb. How many naming forms now? | Three: ***ācer*** (masculine, with *cāseus*), ***ācris*** (feminine, with *mēns*), ***ācre*** (neuter, with *vīnum*). *Ācer*: sharp, keen, fierce. |
| Which shape looks new? | Only the masculine, ***ācer***. *Ācris, ācre* are just *fortis, forte*. |
| Grammar name? | A **three-ending adjective** ("of three terminations"). The dictionary writes all three: *ācer, ācris, ācre*. |
| Chapter 3's *niger, nigra, nigrum* drops the *e* after the masculine. Does *ācer*? | Yes: *ācer*, but *ācr-is, ācr-e, ācr-ī*. Everything after the masculine is *fortis*. |
| "With a sharp cheese." "Sharp cheeses." "Sharp wines." | *Cum cāseō ācrī. Cāseī ācrēs. Vīna ācria.* The noun is second family, the adjective third. |
| *Celer, celeris, celere*: fast. Does this one drop its *e*? | No. It keeps it, like *puer, puerī*: ***celer, celeris, celere***, *celerī, celerem*… |
| "A fast boy. A fast girl. A fast ship." (*Nāvis*, f.: Chapter 15.) And "a fast animal"? | *Puer celer. Puella celeris. Nāvis celeris. Animal celere.* |
| "I see a fast dog." "With fast dogs." | *Canem celerem videō. Cum canibus celeribus.* |
| Ever wonder why December is the twelfth month but *decem* means ten? The Roman *mēnsis* (month) *December* is an adjective like *ācer*: *December, Decembris*. | The old Roman year began in March, so *September, Octōber, November, December* were the seventh to tenth months. All four are three-ending adjectives. (Numbers: Chapter 22.) |

## 5. *Fēlīx, ingēns, pār*

:: Ō fēlīx culpa! || O happy fault! (the *Exsultet*, sung at the Easter Vigil)

Puer fēlīx. Puella fēlīx. Animal fēlīx.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Read the blurb. How many naming forms does *fēlīx* (lucky, happy) have? | One: ***fēlīx*** for masculine, feminine, and neuter. A **one-ending adjective**. |
| With only one naming form, how do you find the stem? | The way you did for *rēx, rēgis*: the dictionary gives the "of" form. ***Fēlīx, fēlīcis***, stem *fēlīc-*. |
| "I see a lucky boy." "I see a lucky animal." | *Puerum fēlīcem videō. Animal fēlīx videō.* Neuter object = neuter subject (the Neuter Twins). |
| "With a lucky girl." "Lucky animals." "Of lucky children." (*Līberī*: Chapter 9.) | *Cum puellā fēlīcī. Animālia fēlīcia. Līberōrum fēlīcium.* Still an i-stem: *-ī, -ia, -ium*. |
| The motto, *Ō fēlīx culpa!* (*Culpa*, f.: fault, as in Chapter 9's *Meā culpā*.) | "O happy fault!" One form, so *fēlīx* fits feminine *culpa*. The Easter hymn calls Adam's sin "happy," because it brought such a redeemer. |
| *Ingēns, ingentis*: huge. "A huge cheese!" "Huge cheeses!" "A huge body." | *Cāseus ingēns! Cāseī ingentēs! Corpus ingēns.* (*Corpora ingentia*, many.) |
| Vergil's Cyclops, Polyphemus (*Aeneid* 3.658): *mōnstrum horrendum, īnfōrme, ingēns.* (*Mōnstrum*, n.: monster; *horrendus*: horrible; *īnfōrmis*: shapeless.) Three adjectives. Three shapes? | All three! ***Horrendum*** (*bonus* family), ***īnfōrme*** (two-ending), ***ingēns*** (one-ending). Three different endings, all neuter, singular, and in the same case as *mōnstrum*: "a monster, horrible, shapeless, huge." (In Vergil they're objects: the Trojans *see* him. Neuter subject and object look alike anyway.) |
| Chapter 19's Catullus: *Ille mī pār esse deō vidētur.* Now *pār*: the dictionary says *pār, paris*. | "Equal": one naming form, ***pār*** (long ā), stem ***par-*** (short a: *paris, parī, parem*). *Pār deō*: "equal **to** a god," with the dative (Chapter 11). |
| *Pars, partis* (Chapter 15) and *pār, paris*. "Equal parts." | *Partēs parēs.* A part and an equal: look-alikes, different words. |

!!! success "The Law of the Three Shapes"
    **The dictionary shows how many naming forms an adjective has:**

    1. **three endings:** *ācer, ācris, ācre* · *celer, celeris, celere*
    2. **two endings:** *fortis, forte* · *dulcis, dulce* · *omnis, omne*
    3. **one ending, plus the "of" form for the stem:** *fēlīx, fēlīcis* · *ingēns, ingentis* · *pār, paris*

    **After the naming form, all of them go like *fortis*, and all are i-stems:** with/from one ***-ī***, neuter many ***-ia***, of many ***-ium***. (The noun tests of Chapter 15 don't apply: the adjective family is i-stem almost always. A few rebels come later.)

## 6. *Quālis est?*

:: Fortēs fortūna adiuvat. || Fortune helps the brave. (proverb; Terence, *Phormio* 203)

Quālis est Avus? Avus fortis est. Quālis est Avia? Avia sapiēns est.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 16's Horace: *Nōn sum quālis eram.* What is *quālis*? | "What sort?", "such as": "I'm not **the sort of man** I used to be." It's a two-ending adjective too: ***quālis, quāle***. *Quālis est Avus?* "What's Grandpa like?" |
| *Sapiēns, sapientis*: wise. "A wise woman." "Wise women." | *Fēmina sapiēns. Fēminae sapientēs.* One-ending, like *ingēns*. |
| *Trīstis, trīste*: sad. "Marcus is sad. Cornēlia is sad. The boys are sad." | *Marcus trīstis est. Cornēlia trīstis est. Puerī trīstēs sunt.* |
| *Gravis*: heavy, serious. *Brevis*: short. "Father is serious." "Quīntus's legs are short." (*Crūs, crūris*, n.: Chapter 14.) | *Pater gravis est. Crūra Quīntī brevia sunt.* Neuter plural: ***-ia***. |
| *Facilis*: easy. "Latin is easy!" (*Lingua Latīna*.) | *Lingua Latīna facilis est!* (Not always.) |
| An adjective alone can mean people: *fortēs*, "the brave"; *omnēs*, "everyone"; *sapiēns*, "a wise man." Read the motto. (*Fortūna*: luck, fortune; *adiuvō, adiuvāre*: help, *ad* + the *iuvō* of Chapter 16's *Forsan et haec… iuvābit*.) | *Fortēs fortūna adiuvat*: "Fortune helps **the brave**." *Fortūna* is the subject; *fortēs*, the brave people, the object. |
| Terence wrote it *fortīs fortūna adiuvat*. What's *fortīs*? | The old object plural in ***-īs*** (Chapter 15): the same as *fortēs*. Pliny the Elder said it too (*Fortēs fortūna iuvat*), sailing toward erupting Vesuvius to rescue his friends, as his nephew tells it (*Letters* 6.16). |
| *Ars longa, vīta brevis.* (*Ars, artis*, f.: skill, art; two consonants, so *artium*.) Which adjective is the third family's? | ***Brevis***, feminine with *vīta*: "Skill takes long; life is short." *Longa* is the *bonus* family. It's the doctor Hippocrates in Latin dress; Seneca quotes him as *vītam brevem esse, longam artem* (*On the Shortness of Life* 1.2). |
| *Iōannēs, quālis es?* And to the whole family, *Quālēs estis?* ("What are you all like?") | *Fortis sum et fēlīx!* (or *trīstis, sapiēns*…). *Fortēs sumus!* "We're brave!" *Quālēs*: the plural, for y'all. |

## 7. Two families, one noun

:: Omnia praeclāra rāra. || All splendid things are rare. (Cicero, *On Friendship* 79)

Rēx bonus et fortis. Fēmina bona et fortis. Corpus bonum et forte.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Quick: "a sweet girl, a sweet boy, a sweet apple." | *Puella dulcis, puer dulcis, mālum dulce.* |
| *Rēx bonis est.* ("The king is good.") What's wrong? | *Rēx* **bonus** *est.* *Bonus* never borrows the third family's *-is*. |
| *Fēmina forta est.* What's wrong? | *Fēmina* **fortis** *est.* *Fortis* has no *-a*: masculine and feminine share *fortis*. |
| *Cum patre forte.* ("With a brave father.") What's wrong? | *Cum patre* **fortī**. The noun says *-e*; the adjective's "with" form is always *-ī*. (*Forte* is the neuter.) |
| *Māla dulca sunt.* What's wrong? | *Māla* **dulcia** *sunt.* The neuter plural of an i-stem adjective is *-ia*. |
| *Puella fēlīcis est.* ("The girl is lucky.") What's wrong? | *Puella* **fēlīx** *est.* *Fēlīcis* is the "of" form; the subject is *fēlīx*. |
| *Nōmen tuum dulcis est.* What's wrong? | *Nōmen tuum* **dulce** *est.* *Nōmen* is neuter (*tuum* knew it). |
| "Of all the girls." "Of all the boys." | *Omnium puellārum. Omnium puerōrum.* Two "of many" endings: *-ium* for the adjective, *-ārum/-ōrum* for the noun. |
| The motto, *Omnia praeclāra rāra.* (*Praeclārus*: splendid; *rārus*: rare, Chapter 15's *Rāra avis*; *sunt* is understood.) | "All splendid things are rare." Three neuter plurals: *omnia* (third family), *praeclāra* and *rāra* (*bonus* family). Cicero is talking about true friends. |

## 8. Lectiō: *Quis vincet?*

:: Fortūna caeca est. || Fortune is blind. (Cicero, *On Friendship* 54)

Quālis est familia? Quis celer est? Quis vincet?

The morning after the market, in the garden. John describes the family, Grandma produces a cheese from somewhere, and everyone races around the house for it. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. New words: *vincō, vincere*, "win, beat" (Chapter 17's *vincēs*), so *Quis vincet?* is "Who will win?"; *circum vīllam*, "around the house," is Chapter 8; and *sī vincet*, "if she wins," is literally "if she will win" (Latin is strict about the clock).

::: dialogue lectio
Iōannēs | Quālis est familia nostra? Ecce! || John | What's our family like? Look!
Iōannēs | Pater gravis est, sed vōx eius nōn dulcis est. || John | Dad is serious, but his voice isn't sweet.
Pater | Vōx mea dulcis est! Cantābō! || Dad | My voice is sweet! I'll sing!
Omnēs | Nōlī, Pater! || Everyone | Don't, Dad!
Iōannēs | Māter sapiēns est. Avia ācris est. || John | Mom is wise. Grandma is fierce.
Avia | Ācris? Minimē! Sapiēns sum! || Grandma | Fierce? Not at all! I'm wise!
Iōannēs | Avus fortis est… sed hodiē fessus. || John | Grandpa is strong… but tired today.
Iōannēs | Marcus celer est. Cornēlia celeris est. Et Quīntus… || John | Marcus is fast. Cornēlia is fast. And Quīntus…
Quīntus | Ego quoque celer sum! || Quīntus | I'm fast too!
Marcus | Tū? Crūra tua brevia sunt! || Marcus | You? Your legs are short!
Quīntus | Brevia, sed fortia! || Quīntus | Short, but strong!
Cornēlia | Bene! Circum vīllam currēmus. Quis vincet? || Cornēlia | Fine! We'll run around the house. Who will win?
Avia | Ecce cāseus! Parvus, sed ācer. Cāseum dabō… celerī! || Grandma | Here's a cheese! Small, but sharp. I'll give the cheese… to the fast one!
Avus | Cāseum? Ubi erat ille cāseus? || Grandpa | A cheese? Where was that cheese?
Avia | In culīnā erat. Sapiēns sum! || Grandma | It was in the kitchen. I'm wise!
Cornēlia | Puellae celerī cāseum dabis, Avia? || Cornēlia | Will you give the cheese to a fast girl, Grandma?
Avia | Ita, sī vincet! || Grandma | Yes, if she wins!
Marcus | Et puerō celerī? || Marcus | And to a fast boy?
Avia | Et puerō celerī! Omnēs parēs estis. || Grandma | And to a fast boy! You're all equal.
Avus | Ego quoque curram! Fortis sum! || Grandpa | I'll run too! I'm strong!
Avia | Fortis es, Ave, sed nōn celer. || Grandma | You're strong, Grandpa, but not fast.
Avus | Fortēs fortūna adiuvat! || Grandpa | Fortune helps the brave!
Iōannēs | Estisne omnēs hīc? Currite! || John | Are you all here? Run!
Marcus | Celer sum! Celer sum! || Marcus | I'm fast! I'm fast!
Cornēlia | Et ego celeris sum! || Cornēlia | And I'm fast!
Quīntus | Exspectāte! Crūra mea brevia sunt! || Quīntus | Wait! My legs are short!
Avus | Ēheu! Fessus sum… Pēs mihi dolet! || Grandpa | Oh, no! I'm tired… My foot hurts!
Iōannēs | Ecce! Quis currit cum Marcō? Canis ācer et celer! || John | Look! Who's running with Marcus? A fierce, fast dog!
Omnēs | Cāseus! || Everyone | Cāseus!
Cāseus | Bau! Bau! || Cāseus | Woof! Woof!
Iōannēs | Cāseus omnēs vincit! || John | Cāseus beats everyone!
Avia | Cāseum dabō celerī… Sed ubi est cāseus? || Grandma | I'll give the cheese to the fast one… But where's the cheese?
Marcus | Cāseus? Canis an cāseus ācer? || Marcus | Cāseus? The dog, or the sharp cheese?
Avia | Cāseus ācer! In mēnsā erat… || Grandma | The sharp cheese! It was on the table…
Cornēlia | Ecce! In ōre canis est! || Cornēlia | Look! It's in the dog's mouth!
Quīntus | Canis celer… et fēlīx! || Quīntus | A fast dog… and a lucky one!
Iōannēs | Hodiē cāseus ācer est in stomachō… || John | Today the sharp cheese is in the stomach of…
Omnēs | …Cāseī! || Everyone | …Cāseus!
Avus | Fortēs fortūna adiuvat… sed nōn avōs! || Grandpa | Fortune helps the brave… but not grandpas!
Avia | Nōlī trīstis esse, Ave. Mel dulce tibi dabō. || Grandma | Don't be sad, Grandpa. I'll give you some sweet honey.
Avus | Mel? Ēheu… Cāseum ācrem volō! || Grandpa | Honey? Oh, no… I want sharp cheese!
:::

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Quālis est familia nostra?* Why *quālis*, not *quāle*? | *Familia* is feminine, so the masculine-and-feminine form *quālis*. |
| *Vōx eius nōn dulcis est.* Which family is *vōx*, and does *dulcis* rhyme with it? | *Vōx, vōcis* (f.) is third family (Chapter 13). *Dulcis* agrees (feminine, singular, subject) without rhyming. *Eius*: "his," Chapter 19. |
| Marcus: *Celer sum!* · Cornēlia: *Et ego celeris sum!* Why two forms? | *Celer* is a three-ending adjective: ***celer*** for a boy, ***celeris*** for a girl. With *fortis* they'd both say *fortis*. |
| *Crūra tua brevia sunt!* · *Brevia, sed fortia!* | *Crūra* is neuter plural (Chapter 14), so the adjectives take ***-ia***: "Short, but strong!" |
| *Cāseum dabō celerī.* Who is *celerī*? | An adjective standing alone, in the dative: "to the fast **one**," boy or girl. Grandma is being careful. |
| *Puellae celerī* · *Puerō celerī*. Same case? | Yes, both dative: the nouns say *-ae* and *-ō*, the adjective says *-ī* for both. The Law of the Two Families. |
| *Omnēs parēs estis.* | "You're all equal": *parēs*, the plural of *pār* (*-ēs*, like *fortēs*), with *omnēs*. Grandma plays fair: a prize for a fast boy **or** a fast girl. |
| *Cāseus? Canis an cāseus ācer?* · *Sed nōn avōs!* | The dog is *Cāseus*, the cheese is *cāseus*: Marcus asks which one. Grandpa's twist on the proverb: fortune helps *fortēs* (the brave), but not *avōs* (grandpas). Both are objects. |

## 9. Sound check

:: Lingua celer, mēns ācris. || A quick tongue, a sharp mind.

Audī et dīc.

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *dulcis · dulce · dulcia · fortium*? | DUL-kis · DUL-ke · DUL-ki-a · FOR-ti-um. Classical *c* is always K; church Latin says DOOL-chis. The final *-e* is a short "eh," never silent. |
| *ācer · ācris · ācre · ācrī*? | AH-ker · AH-kris · AH-kre · AH-kree. Church Latin: AH-cher. |
| *celer · celeris · celere · celerī*? | KE-ler · KE-le-ris · KE-le-re · KE-le-ree. Short vowels, so the stress (emphasis) goes back to the first syllable. Church: CHEH-ler. |
| *fēlīx · fēlīcis · fēlīcī · ingēns · ingentis*? | FAY-leeks · fay-LEE-kis · fay-LEE-kee · IN-gayns · in-GEN-tis. *X* is KS. A vowel before *ns* is long: *ingēns*, *sapiēns*. |
| *pār · paris · sapiēns · sapientis · quālis*? | PAHR · PA-ris · SA-pi-ayns · sa-pi-EN-tis · KWAH-lis. |
| *fortūna · adiuvat · trīstis · omnium*? | for-TOO-na · AD-yu-wat · TREES-tis · OM-ni-um. In *adiuvat* the *i* is a *y* sound and the *v* is a *w*. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking.

1. Chant *fortis* across, case by case: *fortis, forte · fortis · fortī · fortem, forte · fortī*. Then the plural: *fortēs, fortia · fortium · fortibus · fortēs, fortia · fortibus*.
2. Mixed pairs, out loud: *rēx bonus · rēgis bonī · rēgī bonō · rēgem bonum · rēge bonō*. Then *fēmina fortis · fēminae fortis · fēminae fortī · fēminam fortem · fēminā fortī*.
