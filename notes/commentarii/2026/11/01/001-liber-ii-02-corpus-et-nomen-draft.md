# Caput XIV: Corpus et Nōmen

*Liber II, Rēs Cotīdiānae · Chapter 14*

*In which the very first word you asked about in Chapter 1, nōmen, finally gets all its forms. It belongs to the third family's "neither" branch, the neuters, and they turn out to be easy: you already know their two big rules. Then we go from head to foot, find out what cheese is made of, and a new member of the family arrives without a name. Guess what the kids call him.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *In nōmine Patris* · *Caput mihi dolet.* · *Cāseus ex lacte est.* · *Mēns sāna in corpore sānō.* · *Ō tempora, ō mōrēs!*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** neuter (again: "neither" gender), now in the third family
    - **Forms:** one: naming = object, *-is, -ī, -e*; many: *-a* (subject and object), *-um, -ibus*; all cases of *nōmen, corpus, caput*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Neuter Twins (back, and bigger), the Law of the Disguise
    - **Words:** the body from head to foot; milk and honey; name, time, wound; a puppy
    - **Reading:** *Catulus sine nōmine* (a puppy with no name, and a cheese that goes missing)
    - **Speaking:** point at yourself and name the parts; say your head hurts; name a pet

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep saying every new noun as a **pair**, naming form and "of" form: *nōmen, nōminis*. In this chapter the pair also tells you the gender, so add it: *nōmen, nōminis, neuter*.

---

## 1. *Nōmen, nōminis*

:: Nōmen est ōmen. || The name is a sign. (proverb, after Plautus)

Quid est nōmen tibi? Nōmen mihi est Iōannēs. In nōmine Patris.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 1: *Quid est nōmen tibi?* Which family does *nōmen* belong to? It ends in neither *-a* nor *-us*. | The third family. Its pair is ***nōmen, nōminis***, and it's **neuter**: "neither" masculine nor feminine (Chapter 2). |
| Chapter 13's Law of the Stem: take *-is* off *nōminis*. What's left? | ***Nōmin-***. The naming form hides the stem again: *-men* becomes *-min-*. |
| *Nōmen mihi est Iōannēs.* Which case has *nōmen* been all along? | The naming form (nominative): it's the subject. "A name is to me: John." You've been using the third family's neuter since your first day. |
| *In nōmine Patris.* Find the case. | *In* + the ablative: stem *nōmin-* + short **-e**, just like *cum patre*. "In the name of the Father": *Patris* is Chapter 13's "of Father." You know the rest from church: *et Fīliī, et Spīritūs Sanctī* (*Spīritūs* waits for Chapter 23). |
| "I love the name." | *Nōmen amō.* The object looks exactly like the subject: the **Law of the Neuter Twins** (Chapter 4: *ōvum, ōvum*) holds in the third family too. |
| Chapter 2: a neuter's plural always ends in…? Then "names." | ***-a***. *Nōmina*, NOH-mi-na: stem + *-a*. |
| "The children's names." | *Nōmina līberōrum.* |
| Chapter 5 said a pronoun stands *prō nōmine*, "instead of a name." Which case is *nōmine*? | The ablative: *prō* takes it, like *prō rēge* (Chapter 13). |
| The motto, *Nōmen est ōmen.* *Ōmen, ōminis*: a sign of what's coming. Stem? | *Ōmin-*: another *-men* word, another neuter. "The name is a sign." Plautus jokes that a man's name is *nōmen atque ōmen*, "a name and an omen" (*Persa* 625). |

## 2. *Corpus, corporis*

:: Hoc est corpus meum. || This is my body. (the Gospels, Matthew 26:26)

Ecce corpus. Ecce pectus. Tempus fugit.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The motto, *Hoc est corpus meum.* *Corpus* is a body. It ends in *-us*, like *hortus*. Second family? | No! Its pair is ***corpus, corporis***, neuter, third family. The *-us* is a disguise. |
| Stem? | ***Corpor-***. Long ago it was *corpos-*; an *s* between two vowels turned into *r*. Corporal, corporation. |
| *Hortum videō* is "I see the garden." "I see the body"? | *Corpus videō.* Not *corpum*: a neuter's object is its naming form. |
| *Hoc est corpus meum.* Why *meum*, not *meus*? | *Corpus* is neuter, so the possessive is neuter: *meum*, like *mālum meum*. Same gender, not same letters. (*Hoc*, "this," is a chunk for now: Chapter 19.) |
| Chapter 6's motto: *Fugit irreparābile tempus.* *Tempus, temporis*: time. Stem? | *Tempor-*. Temporary, tempo. Another *-us* in disguise. |
| *Pectus, pectoris*: chest, the heart's home. "In the chest"? | *In pectore.* Pectoral. |
| *Vulnus, vulneris*: a wound. Stem? Careful. | *Vulner-*, with an **e** this time, not an o. You can't guess which: learn the pair. Vulnerable: "woundable." |
| "Quīntus has a wound." | *Quīntus vulnus habet.* (Object = naming form, again.) |
| "In the body"? | *In corpore.* KOR-po-re. Keep it: you'll need it in section 7. |

!!! success "The Law of the Disguise"
    In the third family, *-us* can be a **neuter** naming form: *corpus, corporis · tempus, temporis · pectus, pectoris · vulnus, vulneris*. It never turns into *corpum* or *corpō*. When you see *-us*, check the "of" form: *-ī* means second family (*hortus, hortī*); *-oris* or *-eris* almost always means third-family neuter. (The famous exception is a goddess: *Venus, Veneris*, feminine. Meaning wins.)

## 3. *Caput, capitis*

:: Rōma caput mundī. || Rome, head of the world. (medieval saying)

Ecce caput. Ecce capita. Corōna in capite rēgis est.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Caput, capitis*: head, neuter (KA-put). Stem? | ***Capit-***: the u turns into i, as in *mīles, mīlit-*. Capital, capitol, captain. |
| "The doll's head." | *Caput pūpae.* |
| "There's a crown on the king's head." | *Corōna in capite rēgis est.* *In* + ablative *capite*, KA-pi-te. |
| The motto, *Rōma caput mundī.* (*Mundus*, the world.) | "Rome, the head of the world": *caput* names Rome, *mundī* is "of the world." Medieval emperors stamped it on their seals. |
| Economists say "per capita." What is it in Latin? | *Per capita*: "by heads," one share per head. *Capita* is the neuter plural, and *per* takes the accusative (Chapter 8). |
| Chapter 11: *Nōmen mihi est.* Now try *Caput mihi dolet.* (*Doleō, dolēre*: to hurt.) | "My head hurts": "the head hurts to me." The same dative *mihi*. A sentence worth keeping. |
| "Grandpa's head hurts." Keep the dative. | *Caput avō dolet.* |
| Your turn: run *caput* through the singular: naming, of, to, object, with, calling. | *Caput, capitis, capitī, caput, capite, caput.* Three of the six look alike: the neuter twins, plus the calling form. |

## 4. *Ō tempora!*

:: Ō tempora, ō mōrēs! || Oh, the times! Oh, the customs! (Cicero, *Against Catiline* 1.2)

Nōmina, corpora, capita, tempora. Multa nōmina!

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The motto, *Ō tempora!* One time or many? | Many: *tempor-* + **-a**. "Oh, the times (we live in)!" |
| Right beside it, *ō mōrēs*. *Mōs, mōris* (m.) is a custom. Why *-ēs* there, but *-a* in *tempora*? | *Mōs* is masculine: plural *-ēs*, like *rēgēs*. *Tempus* is neuter: plural *-a*. Same family, two plural endings, sorted by gender. |
| "The soldiers' bodies." | *Corpora mīlitum.* The "of many" is *-um*, same as Chapter 13. |
| "The kings' names." | *Nōmina rēgum.* |
| "I see the heads." | *Capita videō.* Subject and object are both *-a*: the Neuter Twins, in the plural. |
| "There are crowns on the kings' heads." | *Corōnae in capitibus rēgum sunt.* *-Ibus*, with/from many, same as *mīlitibus*. |
| *Corpora* and *puella* both end in *-a*. Same job? | No. *Puella* is one girl (first family). *Corpora* is many bodies (neuter plural). Chapter 2 said it: an *-a* means one feminine thing or many neuter things. |
| Compare *rēx* and *nōmen* across all twelve forms. Where do they differ? | Only in the object (sg. *rēgem* vs *nōmen*) and in the subject, object, and calling forms of the plural (*rēgēs* vs *nōmina*). *-is, -ī, -e, -um, -ibus* are the same. |
| Cicero's *Ō tempora!* Is it subject or object? | You can't tell from the ending: neuter twins! (Grammarians say it's the object form, used for exclaiming, like English "Oh, me!") The meaning is clear either way. |

!!! success "Tertia dēclīnātiō: neuters"
    **Singular**

    | case | ending | *nōmen* | *corpus* | *caput* |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | nom. (naming) | (varies) | *nōmen* | *corpus* | *caput* |
    | gen. (of, whose) | *-is* | *nōminis* | *corporis* | *capitis* |
    | dat. (to, for) | *-ī* | *nōminī* | *corporī* | *capitī* |
    | acc. (object; where to) | = nom. | *nōmen* | *corpus* | *caput* |
    | abl. (where, from, with) | *-e* | *nōmine* | *corpore* | *capite* |
    | voc. (calling) | = nom. | *nōmen* | *corpus* | *caput* |

    **Plural**

    | case | ending | *nōmen* | *corpus* | *caput* |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | nom. | *-a* | *nōmina* | *corpora* | *capita* |
    | gen. | *-um* | *nōminum* | *corporum* | *capitum* |
    | dat. | *-ibus* | *nōminibus* | *corporibus* | *capitibus* |
    | acc. | *-a* | *nōmina* | *corpora* | *capita* |
    | abl. | *-ibus* | *nōminibus* | *corporibus* | *capitibus* |
    | voc. | *-a* | *nōmina* | *corpora* | *capita* |

!!! success "The Law of the Neuter Twins (every family)"
    A neuter noun's **naming, object, and calling** forms are always the same, in every family: *mālum · mālum*, *nōmen · nōmen*. In the plural all three end in ***-a***: *māla, nōmina, corpora, capita*. Everything else follows its family.

## 5. *Lac et mel*

:: Mel et lac sub linguā tuā. || Honey and milk are under your tongue. (Song of Songs 4:11)

Ecce ōs. Quid est in ōre? Lac et mel. Et cāseus!

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Ōs, ōris*: the mouth, neuter (OHS). "In the mouth"? | *In ōre.* Oral. |
| "What's in your mouth?" | *Quid est in ōre tuō?* (*Tuō* matches *ōre*: ablative, one, neuter.) |
| Careful: *os, ossis*, with a **short** o, is a bone. Same family, same gender. How do you keep them apart? | Say the vowel: OHS (long, mouth) vs OSS (short, bone). The "of" forms differ too: *ōris* vs *ossis*. Osteopath. Only *ōs* is today's word. |
| *Lac, lactis*: milk, neuter. Stem? | *Lact-*. Lactose: milk sugar. |
| *Cāseus ex lacte est.* | "Cheese is (made) from milk." *Ex* + ablative *lacte*. All this book's cheese started here. |
| *Mel, mellis*: honey, neuter. Stem? | *Mell-*, with a double l. Mellifluous: "flowing like honey." |
| "Grandma gives Quīntus bread and honey." | *Avia Quīntō pānem et mel dat.* *Mel* is the object, and it doesn't change. |
| Exodus promises a land *quae fluit lacte et melle.* Which case are *lacte* and *melle*? | Ablative: "which flows **with** milk and honey." The *-e* again. |
| Plural milks? Plural honeys? | Hardly ever. Like English "milk," they mostly stay singular. Learn the singular well. |
| The motto: *Mel et lac sub linguā tuā.* (*Lingua*, tongue.) | A love poem says the beloved's words are sweet: "honey and milk are under your tongue." *Sub* + ablative: under. |

## 6. *Ā capite ad pedēs*

:: Ā capite ad calcem. || From head to heel: through and through. (proverb)

Caput, oculī, nāsus, ōs, lingua, pectus, bracchia, digitī, crūra, pedēs.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Oculus, oculī* (m.): eye. Which family? Two of them? | Second family: *oculī*, "eyes." Binoculars: two eyes. |
| *Nāsus, nāsī* (m.): nose. *Lingua, linguae* (f.): tongue. Families? | Second and first. Book I families still do the most body work. |
| *Bracchium, bracchiī* (n.): arm. *Digitus, digitī* (m.): finger. "Arms"? "Fingers"? | *Bracchia, digitī.* *Bracchium* is a second-family neuter: plural *-a*, like *mālum, māla*. |
| *Crūs, crūris*: leg, neuter. "Legs"? | *Crūra*, KROO-ra. Third-family neuter: *-a*. |
| *Pēs, pedis*: foot. Neuter too? | No: masculine. "Feet" is *pedēs*, and "I see the foot" is *pedem videō*. Pedestrian, pedal. Learn the gender with the pair. |
| Where's the hand? | *Manus* belongs to a fourth family: Chapter 23. Until then, point with your *digitus*. |
| Chapter 6's motto was *Nōlī mē tangere*, "Don't touch me." *Tangō, tangere*: touch. "Touch your nose!" to one person, then to y'all. | *Tange nāsum!* · *Tangite nāsum!* (One nose each, so Latin is happy with the singular; *nāsōs* works too.) |
| "My legs hurt." | *Crūra mihi dolent.* Plural subject, plural verb. |
| "There's cheese in Marcus's mouth." | *Cāseus in ōre Marcī est.* As usual. |

## 7. *Mēns sāna in corpore sānō*

:: Mēns sāna in corpore sānō. || A healthy mind in a healthy body. (Juvenal, *Satires* 10.356)

Corpus meum. Nōmen tuum. Caput meum dolet.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| A new noun ends in *-men*. Gender? | Neuter: *nōmen, ōmen*, and every other *-men, -minis* word. Safe bet. |
| A new noun ends in *-us*, and its "of" form is *-oris* or *-eris*. Gender? | Almost always neuter: *corpus, tempus, pectus, vulnus*. (The Law of the Disguise; *Venus, Veneris* is a woman, so feminine.) |
| *Soror, uxor, mercātor* end in *-or*. *Corpus* has *-oris*. Which group is neuter? | *Corpus*. The *-or* people are masculine or feminine by meaning (Chapter 13). |
| "My body." "Your name." | *Corpus meum. Nōmen tuum.* Neuter possessives: *-um*. |
| Another way to ask Chapter 1's question: *Quid est nōmen tuum?* Same meaning? | Yes: "What is your name?" *Nōmen tibi* says "the name to you"; *nōmen tuum*, "your name." Answer either way: *Nōmen meum est Iōannēs.* |
| The motto, *Mēns sāna in corpore sānō.* Which word does *sānō* go with? | *Corpore*: ablative, one, neuter. *Sānus*, healthy (sanity, sanitary), is a Book I-style adjective that still matches a third-family noun: same case, not same letters. (Chapter 20 drills this.) |
| And *mēns sāna*? | *Mēns*, the mind (mental), is feminine, so *sāna*. Its family is the next chapter's: keep it as a chunk for now. |
| What did Juvenal actually say? | *Ōrandum est ut sit mēns sāna in corpore sānō*: "you should pray for a healthy mind in a healthy body," not for riches or fame. The gyms borrowed it. |

## 8. Lectiō: *Catulus sine nōmine*

:: Quot capita, tot sententiae. || As many heads, so many opinions. (proverb; Terence has *quot hominēs*)

Pater domum venit. Pater dōnum habet. Dōnum caput parvum habet…

Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. New words: *catulus*, puppy (KA-tu-lus); *Catule*, calling him; *stella*, star; *pulchrum*, pretty; *doctus*, clever, well taught; *rēgīna*, queen.

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Caput parvum, corpus parvum, crūra parva.* Why *parvum* twice, then *parva*? | All three nouns are neuter subjects: *caput, corpus* one (*-um*), *crūra* many (*-a*). The adjective matches gender, number, and case. |
| *Ā muliere in forō.* Which case is *muliere*? | Ablative after *ā*: *mulier, mulieris* → *muliere*, "from a woman." |
| *Spectā uxōrem tuam.* Whose wife? | Cornēlius's: Mother is *uxor Cornēliī*. *Uxōrem* is the object, *-em*. |
| *Catulus sine nōmine est.* | "He's a puppy without a name." *Sine* + ablative *nōmine*. |
| *Nōmen catulō est Rēx.* Which chapter's pattern? | Chapter 11's *Nōmen mihi est*: "the name to the puppy is Rex." *Catulō* is dative. |
| *Dā mihi pedem!* What form is *pedem*, and why not *pedis*? | The object: *pēs* is masculine, so *-em*. *Pedis* would be "of the foot." |
| *Cāseus in ōre catulī est.* Two nouns hang off *in*. Which cases? | *Ōre*, ablative (in the mouth); *catulī*, genitive (of the puppy). |
| *In stomachō… Cāseī!* Why *Cāseī*? | "Of Cheese": the puppy's new name, in the second family's genitive. The cheese is in the stomach of Cheese. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking.

1. Say every new noun as a pair with its gender: *nōmen, nōminis, n. · corpus, corporis, n. · caput, capitis, n. · tempus, temporis, n. · ōs, ōris, n. · pēs, pedis, m.*
2. Decline *nōmen* and *corpus* through all six cases, singular and plural. Then *caput*.
