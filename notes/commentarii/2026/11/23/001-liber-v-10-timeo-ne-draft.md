# Caput LVIII: Timeō Nē

*Liber V, Modus Subiūnctīvus · Chapter 58*

*In which Latin learns to worry. Chapter 49 gave you the wish* Nē mordeat! *("May he not bite!"). Put* Timeō *("I fear") in front of it and you have Latin's way of fearing:* Timeō nē mordeat, *"I'm afraid he may bite." Then the strange twin:* Timeō ut veniās *means "I'm afraid you* won't *come." The same family of clauses also stops things (Grandma's dog* impedit quōminus *Grandpa touches the cheese) and refuses to doubt (*nōn dubitō quīn*). The Latin reads like the old wish it grew from, so you will not need a single new verb form. Real worriers along the way: Caesar fretting about hurting a friend's feelings, a Roman commander posting two legions so a town can't slip away at night, sailors on a storm-tossed ship at night, Cicero afraid Trebatius is freezing in Gaul, and old Cato, not afraid that dead philosophers will laugh at him. Speaking goal: say what you're afraid will (or won't) happen, say what stops something, and say "I don't doubt that…"*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Timeō nē mordeat · Timeō ut veniās · Canis Avum impedit quōminus cāseum tangat · Nōn dubitō quīn Avus cāseum velit · Facere nōn possum quīn rīdeam*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** fear clause (the "fear-that" clause; old-school *clause of fearing*) · prevention clause (the "stop-it" clause; old-school *clause of hindering*)
    - **Forms:** nothing new to build! The subjunctives you know, from Chapter 49 (present), Chapter 52 (imperfect) and Chapter 53 (perfect and pluperfect), and Chapter 54's matching rule.
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Backwards Fear · the Law of the Fear Clock · the Law of the Calm Fear · the Law of Afraid To · the Law of the Stop-It Clause · the Law of *Quīn* · the Law of No Doubt · the Law of Which Word
    - **Words:** *metuō* (fear) · *impediō* (block) · *prohibeō* (keep off) · *dēterreō* (scare off) · *quōminus* · *quīn* · *dubitō* (doubt, hesitate)
    - **Reading:** *Nē cāseum tangat!* (day twenty-six: Grandma goes out, Grandpa sits down with the cheeses, and the dog stays on duty)
    - **Speaking:** say what you're afraid will or won't happen, what stops something, and what you don't doubt

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. For every *nē*, *ut*, *quōminus* or *quīn*, ask first: which verb is in charge, a fear, a stop, or a doubt?

## 1. From wish to worry

:: Nē mordeat! || May he not bite! (teaching line; Chapter 49)

Nē mordeat… timeō nē mordeat.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Grandma points at the dog and says *Nē mordeat!* Which mood is *mordeat*, and what does the line mean? | The **subjunctive** (Chapter 49), with *nē*: "**May he not bite!**" or "Let him not bite!" A wish, not a fact. (Chapter 50's *Utinam nē pluat* was the same kind of wish.) |
| Now put a worry in front of the wish. *Timeō* means "I fear" (Chapter 47). Say the wish and the worry as one sentence. | ***Timeō nē mordeat.*** "I fear he may bite." Think of it as "May he not bite! (but I'm afraid he will)." |
| Which half is the old wish, and which is the worry? | The ***nē* part** is the wish ("may he not bite"); ***timeō*** adds "but I'm afraid he will." What you fear is the very thing you wish wouldn't happen. |
| English says "I fear **that** he will bite," with no "not" anywhere. Does the *nē* make *Timeō nē mordeat* negative? | **No.** Here *nē* means "**that**" (or "lest"). *Timeō nē mordeat* = "I fear **that** he'll bite" = "I fear **lest** he bite." The "not" was the wish's; the fear points at the thing wished away. |
| Grammar books call this a **fear clause**. Plain words? | **The fear-that clause**: a clause that says what you're afraid might happen. Old school books call it a *clause of fearing* (it follows "verbs of fearing"). It's in the subjunctive because the thing isn't a fact: it's only a worry. |

## 2. I'm afraid that it will happen

:: Timeō nē canis mordeat. || I fear the dog may bite. (teaching line; the chapter title)

Timeō nē… Metuō nē… Vereor nē…

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Three Latin verbs of fear so far: *timeō* (Chapter 47), *vereor* (Chapter 43), and one more: *metuō, metuere, metuī*, "fear, dread." Do all three take *nē* + subjunctive? | **Yes.** *Timeō nē…, metuō nē…, vereor nē…* all mean "I fear that…" Pick any. |
| "I fear that Grandpa will eat the cheese." | ***Timeō nē Avus cāseum edat.*** *Nē* + present subjunctive. |
| "Grandma fears that the dog may bite Grandpa." (*Timet*, she fears.) | ***Avia timet nē canis Avum mordeat.*** |
| "I fear that **anyone** will touch the cheese." (Chapter 56's *nē quis*.) | ***Timeō nē quis cāseum tangat.*** After *nē*, *quis* means "anyone": the same trick as Chapter 29's *sī quis* and Chapter 56's *nē quis*. |
| *Timēmus nē crās pluat.* The English says "will rain," the Latin says *pluat* (present). Why? | Fears look **ahead**, and the **present subjunctive** covers "now or soon." Latin doesn't reach for Chapter 54's *-ūrus sit*: just *pluat*. "We fear **it will rain** tomorrow." |
| Can the fear be about **right now**? "I fear Grandpa is eating the cheese this very minute." | ***Timeō nē Avus nunc cāseum edat.*** The same present subjunctive: about the present *or* the near future. Context tells which. |

## 3. I'm afraid that it *won't* happen

:: Timeō ut veniās. || I fear you won't come. (teaching line)

Nē = that. Ut = that…not.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 50: *Utinam veniat!* means "If only he would come!" Drop the *-inam* and put *timeō* in front. What do you get, and what does it mean? | ***Timeō ut veniat.*** The wish "may he come" stays, with the worry "(but I fear he won't)": "**I fear that he won't come.**" |
| Compare the two: *Timeō nē veniat* and *Timeō ut veniat.* | *Nē*: "I fear he **will** come" (you wish he wouldn't). *Ut*: "I fear he **won't** come" (you wish he would). **After a verb of fearing, *ut* means "that…not."** |
| Chapter 52 said *ut nōn* is for results and *nē* for wants. Does *Timeō ut veniās* mean "I fear in order that you come"? | **No.** It isn't a purpose at all. After *timeō*, *metuō*, *vereor*, *ut* has flipped: "that…not." The verb in charge decides what *ut* means. |
| There is another way to say it: *Timeō nē nōn veniās.* How is it different from *Timeō ut veniās*? | It means the same: "I fear **that you won't** come." *Ut* alone is the usual way; *nē nōn* is the other. (*Nē* "that" + *nōn* "not.") |
| Cicero to his wife Terentia, January 49 BC, as Caesar's war reaches Italy (*Letters to Friends* 14.14): *vereor, ut Dolabella ipse satis nōbīs prōdesse possit.* (*Prōsum, prōdesse*: be of use, + dative; Dolabella: Cicero's son-in-law, on Caesar's side.) Translate. | "I fear that Dolabella himself **can't** help us enough." *Ut* after *vereor*: "that…not." |

!!! success "The Law of the Backwards Fear"
    After a verb of **fearing** (*timeō, metuō, vereor*), the clause is in the **subjunctive**, and the little word is backwards from what you'd expect:

    | You fear… | Latin | Example |
    |---|---|---|
    | that it **will** happen | ***nē*** + subjunctive | *Timeō nē veniat.* I fear he'll come. |
    | that it **won't** happen | ***ut*** (or ***nē nōn***) + subjunctive | *Timeō ut veniat.* I fear he won't come. |

    The clause is the old wish pushed inside: *Nē veniat!* (may he not come) → *timeō nē veniat*; *Utinam veniat!* (may he come) → *timeō ut veniat.*

## 4. The clock of a fear

:: Timēbam nē ēdisset. || I was afraid he had eaten it. (teaching line)

Veniat… vēnerit… venīret… vēnisset…

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Timeō nē Avus cāseum edat.* Which team, and which time? | **Now team** (*timeō* is a present), present subjunctive: he may be eating it now, or soon. |
| "I fear Grandpa **has eaten** the cheese." The eating is already over. Which subjunctive is "earlier" on the now team? (Chapter 54.) | The **perfect**: ***Timeō nē Avus cāseum ēderit.*** (Not *edat*, which would be now or soon.) |
| Paul writes to the Galatians (4:11, Vulgate): *Timeō vōs, nē forte sine causā labōrāverim in vōbīs.* (*Forte*: perhaps; *sine causā*: for no reason; *labōrō*: work hard.) Translate. Why *labōrāverim*? | "I'm afraid for you, **that I may have worked** hard among you for nothing." The work is already done, so the **perfect** subjunctive. (*Timeō vōs*: "I fear for you all," with *vōs* put up front.) |
| Now put it on the **past team**: "Grandpa feared that the dog might bite him." (*Sē*, himself, Chapter 21.) | ***Avus timēbat nē canis sē mordēret.*** *Timēbat* is past, so the **imperfect** *mordēret*: "might bite." |
| "Grandma feared Grandpa had eaten the cheese." Past team, and the eating came **before** her fear. | ***Avia timēbat nē Avus cāseum ēdisset.*** The **pluperfect**. |
| "I was afraid you wouldn't come." (*Ut* + the right clock.) | ***Timēbam ut venīrēs.*** Past team, imperfect. Same *ut*, "that…not." |

!!! success "The Law of the Fear Clock"
    A fear clause follows Chapter 54's **matching rule**: *nē* or *ut* + subjunctive, and the verb of fearing sets the team.

    | Fear verb | Fear that it **is happening / will happen** | Fear that it **already happened** |
    |---|---|---|
    | **now team**: *timeō, timēbō* | present: *nē veniat* | perfect: *nē vēnerit* |
    | **past team**: *timēbam, timuī* | imperfect: *nē venīret* | pluperfect: *nē vēnisset* |

## 5. Real fears

:: Valdē metuō, nē frīgeās in hibernīs. || I'm very much afraid you may be freezing in winter quarters. (Cicero, *Letters to Friends* 7.10)

Verēbātur… veritus… timentēs…

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.19.2. He wants to punish the Aeduan Dumnorix, but Dumnorix's brother Diviciacus is Rome's loyal friend: *nam nē eius suppliciō Dīviciācī animum offenderet verēbātur.* (*Supplicium*: punishment; *offendō*: hurt, offend; *animus*: feelings; the Latin runs "for **that by his punishment** he'd hurt Diviciacus's feelings he feared.") Translate. Which team? | "For he **was afraid that** by punishing him he would hurt Diviciacus's feelings." *Verēbātur* (Chapter 43's deponent, imperfect) is **past team**, so the imperfect *offenderet*. |
| *Gallic War* 7.11.6. Caesar camps in front of Cenabum, a town on the river Loire: *veritus nē noctū ex oppidō profugerent, duās legiōnēs in armīs excubāre iubet.* (*Veritus*: the perfect participle of *vereor*, "having feared" = "fearing"; *noctū*: by night; *profugiō*: flee away; *excubō*: keep watch; *iubet* + infinitive, Chapter 56.) Translate. | "**Fearing that** they might flee from the town by night, he orders two legions to keep watch under arms." *Veritus* is already past, so the past team: *profugerent*. (He was right: the people of Cenabum slipped out near midnight.) |
| Acts 27:17 (Vulgate), Paul's ship in the storm: *timentēs nē in Syrtim inciderent, summissō vāse, sīc ferēbantur.* (*Syrtis*: the Syrtes, shifting sandbanks off Libya; *incidō*: fall into; *vās*: gear; *ferō*, passive: be carried along.) Translate. | "**Fearing that they would fall into** the Syrtis, they lowered the gear and were carried along like that." *Timentēs*, a present participle (Chapter 38), then *nē* + imperfect *inciderent*. |
| Acts 27:29 (Nova Vulgata), same night, Luke speaking: *timentēs autem, nē in aspera loca incideremus, dē puppī mittentēs ancorās quattuor optābant diem fierī.* (*Asper*: rough; *locus*: place; *puppis*: stern; *ancora*: anchor; *optō*: wish; *fīō*: become, happen.) Translate. | "And **fearing that we might fall onto rough places**, they dropped four anchors from the stern and **wished for day to come**." *Incideremus*: *we*, because Luke was aboard. A fear and a wish side by side: the old wish *Ut diēs fīat!* behind it. |
| Cicero to Trebatius, a lawyer on Caesar's staff in Gaul (*Letters to Friends* 7.10, December 54 BC), joking about the winter camp in Gaul: *Valdē metuō, nē frīgeās in hibernīs.* (*Valdē*: very much; *frīgeō*: be cold; *hīberna*: winter quarters.) Translate. | "I'm very much afraid that you **may be freezing** in winter quarters." *Metuō*, present, so the present subjunctive *frīgeās*. (He goes on to advise "a good roaring fire.") |
| Cicero to Terentia, January 49 BC (*Letters to Friends* 14.14): *Etiam illud metuō, nē iam intercludāmur.* (*Etiam illud*: this too; *intercludō*: cut off; *iam*: already.) Translate. Which voice is *intercludāmur*? | "I also fear **that we may already be cut off**." The **passive** present subjunctive (Chapter 49 *amētur* family): *-āmur*, "we" passive. |

## 6. Cicero and old Cato

:: Nec sepulcra legēns vereor, … nē memoriam perdam. || Nor, reading tombs, do I fear … that I'll lose my memory. (Cicero, *On Old Age* 21)

Timeō… nōn timeō…

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Cicero, *Against Catiline* 2.15, the day after Catiline left Rome, Cicero confesses his worry: *multōque magis illud timeō, nē mihi sit invidiōsum aliquandō, quod illum ēmīserim potius quam quod ēiēcerim.* (*Invidiōsus*: hated, resented; *aliquandō*: someday; *ēmittō*: let go; *ēiciō*: throw out. The *quod* part is "because, people will say"; just read it.) Translate. | "And I fear much more **that it may someday be held against me** (that I let him go rather than threw him out)." *Timeō nē* + present *sit*, a plain fear that something will happen. |
| *On Old Age* 21, Cato says he never worried about forgetting names: *nec sepulcra legēns vereor, quod aiunt, nē memoriam perdam.* (*Sepulcrum*: tomb; *legēns*: reading; *quod aiunt*: as they say; *perdō*: lose.) Translate. Is the fear really there? | "Nor, **reading tombs**, do I fear, as they say, **that I'll lose my memory**." The fear verb is **negated**, and the clause is **still *nē***: it's still what he'd be afraid *of*. |
| *On Old Age* 85, Cato on the afterlife: *nōn vereor, nē hunc errōrem meum philosophī mortuī inrīdeant.* (*Error*: mistake; *philosophus*: philosopher; *inrīdeō*: laugh at.) Translate. | "I'm **not afraid that** dead philosophers will laugh at this mistake of mine." Again *nōn vereor nē*. |

!!! success "The Law of the Calm Fear"
    *Nōn timeō, nōn vereor, nōn metuō* + ***nē*** + subjunctive = "I'm **not** afraid **that**…" Saying "not afraid" doesn't change the clause: it's still *nē* for the thing you might have feared. (*Nōn timeō ut…* would be "I'm not afraid that…not.")

## 7. Afraid to, afraid of

:: Timuit illūc īre. || He was afraid to go there. (Matthew 2:22)

Timeō īre. Timeō canem.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Matthew 2:22 (Vulgate): Joseph, back from Egypt, hears that Archelaus now rules Judea: *… timuit illūc īre.* (*Illūc*: to that place; *īre*: to go.) How does *timuit* link to *īre*? | "He **was afraid to go** there." *Timeō* + **infinitive** = "I'm afraid **to do** something." Same pattern as Matthew 1:20 (Vulgate), *nōlī timēre accipere Mariam*: "don't be afraid to take Mary." |
| Compare: *Timeō īre* and *Timeō nē eam.* | *Timeō īre*: "I'm afraid **to go**" (I won't, because I'm scared). *Timeō nē eam*: "I'm afraid I **may (end up) going**." The first is about my **choice**; the second is a worry about **what might happen**. |
| *Timeō canem.* Is there a clause here? | **No.** *Canem* is just the **object** (accusative): "I fear **the dog**." Chapter 38's *Timeō Danaōs et dōna ferentīs* (Aeneid 2.49) and Matthew 14:5's *timuit populum* ("he feared the people") work the same way. |
| "Grandpa is afraid to touch the cheese." · "Grandpa is afraid the dog will bite." · "Grandpa is afraid of the dog." | ***Avus cāseum tangere timet.*** · ***Avus timet nē canis mordeat.*** · ***Avus canem timet.*** Infinitive, clause, plain object. |

!!! success "The Law of Afraid To"
    A fear verb can take **three** things:

    | After *timeō* / *vereor* | Meaning | Example |
    |---|---|---|
    | an **infinitive** | afraid **to do** it (my choice) | *Timeō īre.* |
    | ***nē* / *ut*** + subjunctive | afraid **that** something will (or won't) happen | *Timeō nē eat.* |
    | an **accusative** | afraid **of** a person or thing | *Timeō canem.* |

## 8. Stopping things

:: Canis Avum impedit quōminus cāseum tangat. || The dog stops Grandpa from touching the cheese. (teaching line)

Impedit… dēterret… prohibet…

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Five verbs of **stopping**: *impediō, impedīre, impedīvī, impedītum* (block), *dēterreō, dēterrēre, dēterruī, dēterritum* (scare off), *obstō* (stand in the way, Chapter 44), *prohibeō, prohibēre, prohibuī, prohibitum* (keep off), *retineō, retinēre, retinuī, retentum* (hold back). *Canis Avum impedit nē cāseum tangat.* Translate. | "The dog **stops Grandpa from touching** the cheese." Literally "…blocks Grandpa, **that** he not touch the cheese." Same *nē* as Chapter 56's tucked-in command. |
| Latin has a second word for the same job: ***quōminus***. *Canis Avum impedit quōminus cāseum tangat.* Is the meaning different? | **No.** *Quōminus* is old Latin for "(by) which the less": "…blocks Grandpa **so that he touches the cheese the less**" = "from touching." Say it like one word. After stopping verbs, ***nē* and *quōminus* are twins.** |
| Cicero's Cato, *On Old Age* 60, about the farmer's life: *nec aetās impedit, quōminus … studia teneāmus ūsque ad ultimum tempus senectūtis.* (*Aetās*: age; *studium*: interest; *teneō*: hold on to; *ultimus*: last; *senectūs*: old age; the dots are "farming and other things.") Translate. | "Nor does age **stop us from keeping up** our interests right to the last of old age." *Quōminus* + present subjunctive *teneāmus*. |
| Put it on the past team: "The dog stopped Grandpa from touching the cheese." | ***Canis Avum impedīvit quōminus cāseum tangeret.*** *Impedīvit* is a past "did," so the imperfect *tangeret*. |
| *Prohibeō* is the odd one. Acts 8:36 (Nova Vulgata), the Ethiopian by a stream: *Ecce aqua; quid prohibet mē baptizārī?* (*Baptizō*: baptize.) And Mark 10:14 (Vulgate): *Sinite parvulōs venīre ad mē et nē prohibuerītis eōs.* (Chapter 53's *nē* + perfect subjunctive.) What follows *prohibeō*? | An **accusative** and, if needed, an **infinitive**: *quid prohibet **mē baptizārī**?* "What stops **me from being baptized**?" And *nē prohibuerītis **eōs***: "don't stop **them**." *Prohibeō* usually skips *nē* / *quōminus*. |
| Paul to the elders (Acts 20:27, Vulgate): *nōn enim subterfūgī, quōminus annūntiārem omne cōnsilium Deī vōbīs.* (*Subterfugiō*: shrink back from; *annūntiō*: announce; *cōnsilium*: plan.) Translate. Why the imperfect *annūntiārem*? | "For I **did not shrink from announcing** the whole plan of God to you." *Subterfūgī* is a past "did," so the past team: the imperfect. A stopping verb **with a "not"** (*nōn subterfūgī*) is fine with *quōminus*. |

!!! success "The Law of the Stop-It Clause"
    A verb of **stopping, blocking, scaring off** (*impediō, dēterreō, obstō, retineō*) + the person stopped (accusative; dative after *obstō*) + ***nē*** or ***quōminus*** + **subjunctive** = "stops X **from** doing." Past team: imperfect. English "from -ing" is the Latin subjunctive. *Prohibeō* usually takes the accusative and an infinitive instead. Old school books call it a **clause of preventing** (hindering).

## 9. *Quīn* after a "no"

:: Facere nōn possum quīn rīdeam. || I can't help laughing. (teaching line)

Nōn possum quīn… Nōn retinērī potest quīn…

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 39 met *Quīn et ēmissūrus Fūcinum lacum…* (Suetonius: "what's more, about to drain the Fucine Lake…"). That *quīn* was "what's more." Old Latin *quīn* = *quī* + *nē*, "how not?" Why would a "how not?" show up after a "no"? | "I can't help laughing" is "**How not** laugh?" Out of "How could I not?" grew a clause for after a **negative**. It's the same little word, doing a second job. |
| *Facere nōn possum quīn rīdeam.* Translate. | "I **can't help** laughing" (I can't **not** laugh; literally "I can't do it, but that I laugh"). ***Quīn*** + subjunctive. |
| *Avus retinērī nōn potest quīn cāseum tangat.* (*Retinērī*: passive infinitive, "be held back.") Translate. | "Grandpa **can't be held back from touching** the cheese." Stopping verb, with a "can't," so **quīn** is available. |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.47.2, the Germans before a conference: *Germānī retinērī nōn potuerant quīn tēla in nostrōs cōicerent.* (*Tēlum*: missile; *cōiciō*: hurl.) Translate. Which team? | "The Germans **couldn't be held back from hurling** missiles at our men." *Potuerant* is past, so the imperfect *cōicerent*. |
| Can you say *Canis Avum impedit quīn cāseum tangat*? (A plain "the dog stops him," no negative.) | **No.** *Quīn* needs a **no** first: **can't, won't, nothing, not**, or a question that means "no." A plain, positive stop takes *nē* or *quōminus*. |

!!! success "The Law of *Quīn*"
    After a stopping verb with a **negative** (*nōn possum, nihil impedit, nōn retinērī potest*) the clause can start with ***quīn*** ("from, but that") + subjunctive. *Quīn* is allowed **only** after a "no." *Quōminus* also works after a "no." Without a "no," use *nē* or *quōminus*.

## 10. No doubt

:: Nōn dubitō quīn ad tē omnēs tuī scrīpserint. || I don't doubt that all your people have written to you. (Cicero, *Letters to Friends* 5.8.1)

Dubitō… nōn dubitō quīn…

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Dubitō, dubitāre* means "doubt." *Nōn dubitō quīn Avus cāseum velit.* Translate. (*Velit*: Chapter 50's present subjunctive of *volō*.) | "I **don't doubt that** Grandpa wants the cheese." *Nōn dubitō* is a "no" about doubting, so ***quīn*** + subjunctive: "that." |
| Cicero opens a letter to Crassus (*Letters to Friends* 5.8.1, January 54 BC): *Nōn dubitō quīn ad tē omnēs tuī scrīpserint.* (*Tuī*: your people.) Translate. Why *scrīpserint*? | "I don't doubt that **all your people have written** to you." The writing is **earlier** than *nōn dubitō* (now team), so the **perfect** subjunctive: Chapter 54's "earlier." |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.3.6. Orgetorix is rousing his friends to take over Gaul: *nōn esse dubium quīn tōtīus Galliae plūrimum Helvētiī possent.* (*Plūrimum possum*: be the strongest; *Helvētiī*: the Helvetians. Orgetorix is being quoted, so *esse*, not *est*.) Translate. | "that **there was no doubt that** the Helvetians were the strongest in all Gaul." *Nōn esse dubium quīn* = "there's no doubt that." The same *quīn*. |
| Cicero's Cato, *On Old Age* 31, about Nestor, the old Greek: *nōn dubitat, quīn brevī sit Trōia peritūra.* (*Brevī*: soon; *peritūra*: Chapter 39's future participle, "going to perish.") Translate. | "He **doesn't doubt that** Troy will soon be destroyed." *Quīn* + *sit … peritūra*, Chapter 54's "later" form on the now team. |
| *Dubitō* alone, with no "not": *Dubitō num Avus cāseum ēderit.* What kind of clause is it? | A **tucked-in question** (Chapter 53): "I **doubt whether** Grandpa has eaten the cheese." No negative, so **no *quīn***. |
| *Dubitō īre.* What's that? | "I **hesitate** to go." *Dubitō* + **infinitive** = "hesitate." (Cf. *Timeō īre.*) |

!!! success "The Law of No Doubt"
    | You say | Latin | Example |
    |---|---|---|
    | I **don't doubt that** | *nōn dubitō* ***quīn*** + subjunctive | *Nōn dubitō quīn veniat.* |
    | there's **no doubt that** | *nōn est dubium* ***quīn*** + subjunctive | *Nōn est dubium quīn veniat.* |
    | I **doubt whether** | *dubitō* + ***num*** + subjunctive | *Dubitō num veniat.* |
    | I **hesitate to** | *dubitō* + **infinitive** | *Dubitō venīre.* |

## 11. Which word?

:: Quod verbum? || Which word? (teaching line; Chapter 29)

Nē? Ut? Quōminus? Quīn?

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "I fear that Grandma will see." | ***Timeō nē Avia videat.*** A fear that it **will**: *nē*. |
| "I fear that Grandma won't see." | ***Timeō ut Avia videat.*** A fear that it **won't**: *ut* (or *nē nōn*). |
| "The dog stops Grandpa from eating." | ***Canis Avum impedit nē edat*** (or ***quōminus edat***). A **plain stop**: *nē* or *quōminus*. |
| "Grandpa can't be kept from eating." | ***Avus retinērī nōn potest quīn edat.*** A stop with a **no**: *quīn* (or *quōminus*). |
| "I don't doubt that Grandpa is eating." | ***Nōn dubitō quīn Avus edat.*** A **no** doubt: *quīn*. |

!!! success "The Law of Which Word"
    | The main verb is… | The little word | Means |
    |---|---|---|
    | **fear** (*timeō, metuō, vereor*) | *nē* · *ut* / *nē nōn* | that it will · that it won't |
    | **stop** (*impediō, dēterreō, obstō, retineō*) | *nē* · *quōminus* | from -ing |
    | **stop with a "no"** (*nōn possum, nihil impedit*) | *quīn* · *quōminus* | from -ing |
    | **no doubt** (*nōn dubitō, nōn est dubium*) | *quīn* | that |
    | afraid / hesitate / keep off | *infinitive* | to |

    Every one of them is **subjunctive** and obeys the **matching rule**.

## 13. Traps and fix-its

:: Errāre licet; corrigere melius. || Making a mistake is allowed; correcting it is better. (teaching line; Chapter 50)

Quod verbum? Quod tempus?

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "I fear that he will come": *Timeō ut veniat.* | ***Timeō nē veniat.*** *Ut* after a fear means "that…not," so *Timeō ut veniat* is "I fear he won't come." |
| "I fear that he won't come": *Timeō nē veniat.* | ***Timeō ut veniat.*** (or *Timeō nē nōn veniat*.) *Nē veniat* is "I fear he **will** come." |
| "I'm afraid to go (so I won't)": *Timeō nē eam.* | ***Timeō īre.*** *Timeō nē eam* means "I fear I may (end up) going." For "afraid to" use the infinitive. |
| "The dog stops Grandpa from touching it": *Canis Avum impedit quīn tangat.* | ***Canis Avum impedit quōminus tangat*** (or ***nē tangat***). *Quīn* needs a "no" first. |
| "I was afraid Grandpa had eaten it": *Timēbam nē Avus ēderit.* | ***Timēbam nē Avus ēdisset.*** Past team: pluperfect, not perfect. |

## 14. Lectiō: *Nē cāseum tangat!*

:: Canis cāseōs custōdit. || The dog guards the cheeses. (teaching line)

Domī… canis… cāseus.

Diē vīcēsimā sextā: the day after Chapter 57. Grandma has to go out, and Grandpa is left with John and the dog guarding two cheeses. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. Find every fear (*nē* and *ut*), every stop (*quōminus*), every *quīn*. New in passing: *vīcēsimus sextus*, twenty-sixth; *mox*, soon (Chapter 44); *frūstum*, a scrap; *extendō*, stretch out; *retineō*, hold back; *vērē*, truly; *dentēs*, teeth (Chapter 38); *invideō* + dative, envy.

::: dialogue lectio
Iōannēs | Diē vīcēsimā sextā Avia domō exit; sed prius mē vocat. || John | On the twenty-sixth day Grandma leaves home; but first she calls me.
Avia | Iōannēs, timeō nē Avus cāseōs tangat. || Grandma | John, I'm afraid Grandpa may touch the cheeses.
Avia | Et timeō ut tū satis vigilēs! Tū saepe dormīs. || Grandma | And I'm afraid you won't stay awake enough! You often sleep.
Iōannēs | Nōn dormiam, Avia! Avum et cāseōs custōdiam. || John | I won't sleep, Grandma! I'll guard Grandpa and the cheeses.
Avia | Valēte! Mox redībō. || Grandma | Goodbye, y'all! I'll be back soon.
Avus | Nōn dubitō quīn Iōannēs bene vigilet. Ego ipse cāseōs nōn tangam! || Grandpa | I don't doubt John will stay awake nicely. I myself won't touch the cheeses!
Iōannēs | Avia exit. Avus ad mēnsam sedet et cāseōs spectat. || John | Grandma goes out. Grandpa sits at the table and looks at the cheeses.
Iōannēs | Timēbam nē Avus mox cāseum tangeret. || John | I was afraid Grandpa would soon touch the cheese.
Avus | Iōannēs, nōn vereor nē canis mē mordeat. Canis mē amat! || Grandpa | John, I'm not afraid the dog will bite me. The dog loves me!
Iōannēs | Canis cāseum amat, nōn tē, Ave. Timeō nē tē mordeat. || John | The dog loves the cheese, not you, Grandpa. I'm afraid he may bite you.
Avus | Facere nōn possum quīn cāseum tangam! Parvum frūstum tantum… || Grandpa | I can't help touching the cheese! Just a small scrap…
Iōannēs | Avus manum extendit. Canis surgit et Avum spectat. || John | Grandpa stretches out his hand. The dog gets up and watches Grandpa.
Avus | Ēheu! Canis mē impedit quōminus cāseum tangam! || Grandpa | Alas! The dog is stopping me from touching the cheese!
Iōannēs | Nihil canem impedit quōminus tē mordeat! Avia enim canī imperāvit ut cāseōs custōdīret. || John | Nothing stops the dog from biting you! For Grandma ordered the dog to guard the cheeses.
Avus | Canis bone, tē ōrō, nōlī mē impedīre! Dabō tibi parvum frūstum. || Grandpa | Good dog, please, don't stop me! I'll give you a small scrap.
Iōannēs | Avus timet ut canis frūstum accipiat. Et vērē canis frūstum nōn accipit. || John | Grandpa is afraid the dog won't accept the scrap. And indeed the dog doesn't accept it.
Avus | Iōannēs, tē ōrō, mē retinē nē cāseum tangam! || Grandpa | John, please, hold me back so I don't touch the cheese!
Iōannēs | Avum tunicā teneō. Sed Avus retinērī nōn potest quīn manum extendat! || John | I hold Grandpa by the tunic. But Grandpa can't be held back from stretching out his hand!
Iōannēs | Subitō Avia adest! || John | Suddenly Grandma is there!
Avia | Quid agis, Ave? || Grandma | What are you doing, Grandpa?
Avus | Nihil, Avia! Canis mē impedīvit quōminus cāseum tangerem. Ego nōn tetigī! || Grandpa | Nothing, Grandma! The dog stopped me from touching the cheese. I didn't touch it!
Iōannēs | Vērum dīcit. Nōn dubitō quīn Avus cāseum tangere voluerit; sed canis eum impedīvit. || John | He's telling the truth. I don't doubt Grandpa wanted to touch the cheese; but the dog stopped him.
Avia | Canem laudō! Sed timeō ut Avus discat. Nōn dubitō quīn crās iterum temptet. || Grandma | I praise the dog! But I fear Grandpa won't learn. I don't doubt he'll try again tomorrow.
Avia | Hodiē canī parvum cāseum dō, quia Avum impedīvit. || Grandma | Today I give the dog the small cheese, because he stopped Grandpa.
Avus | Ēheu! Vereor ut umquam cāseum edam! || Grandpa | Alas! I'm afraid I'll never eat cheese!
Iōannēs | Avus canī invidet, et canis Avō dentēs ostendit. || John | Grandpa envies the dog, and the dog shows Grandpa his teeth.
Iōannēs | Nōn dubitō quīn crās rūrsus certent. Ego vigilābō! || John | I don't doubt they'll fight it out again tomorrow. I'll stay awake!
:::

{#g-14}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Find the fears that something **will** happen. Who says each, and what is each about? | ***Timeō nē Avus cāseōs tangat*** (Grandma) · ***Timeō nē tē mordeat*** (John, about the dog) · and in the past ***Timēbam nē Avus mox cāseum tangeret*** (John telling the story). |
| Find the fears that something **won't** happen. | ***Timeō ut tū satis vigilēs*** (Grandma) · ***Avus timet ut canis frūstum accipiat*** (John) · ***Timeō ut Avus discat*** (Grandma) · ***Vereor ut umquam cāseum edam*** (Grandpa: *umquam*, "ever," with *ut* = "that…never"). |
| Find the stops and the *quīn*s. Which *quīn* follows a "can't," and which a "don't doubt"? | Stops: ***Canis mē impedit quōminus cāseum tangam · Nihil canem impedit quōminus tē mordeat · Canis mē impedīvit quōminus cāseum tangerem.*** *Quīn* after "can't": ***Facere nōn possum quīn tangam · retinērī nōn potest quīn manum extendat.*** After "don't doubt": ***Nōn dubitō quīn Iōannēs bene vigilet · … quīn crās iterum temptet · … quīn Avus cāseum tangere voluerit · … quīn crās rūrsus certent.*** |
| Grandpa says *Nōn vereor nē canis mē mordeat*. What does the "not" do to the *nē*? | **Calm fear**: ***nōn vereor nē***, "I'm not afraid that…" The clause is still *nē*. |
| Which *nē* in the story is **not** a fear? | Grandpa's ***mē retinē nē cāseum tangam***: *nē* after an **imperative** = "**so that I don't**," a purpose (Chapter 51), not a fear. No verb of fearing, no fear clause. |
| *Nōn dubitō quīn Avus cāseum tangere voluerit*: why the **perfect** *voluerit*? | The wanting is **earlier** than the "don't doubt" (now team). Chapter 54's "earlier" = perfect. |
