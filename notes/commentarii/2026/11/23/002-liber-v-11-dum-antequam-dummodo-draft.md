# Caput LIX: Dum, Antequam, Dummodo

*Liber V, Modus Subiūnctīvus · Chapter 59*

*In which Latin tells time, makes deals, and admits things. Chapter 5 gave you* Dum spīrō, spērō, *"While I breathe, I hope." Today* dum *shows its four faces: "while," "as long as," "until" and "provided that." Next come "before" (*antequam, priusquam*), "after" and "as soon as" (*postquam, ubi, simul atque*), "although" (*quamquam, quamvīs, licet*), and a* quī *clause that describes a* kind *of person:* Sunt quī cāseum nōn ament, *"There are people who don't like cheese" (Grandpa can't believe it). Each one hangs on a single question you already know how to ask: is it a fact (the fact mood), or is it waited for, granted, or imagined (the subjunctive)? Real lines along the way: Caesar waiting at anchor off Britain, Cicero telling Catiline he'd be glad of a wall between them, a cruel king's motto* Ōderint, dum metuant, *Aeneas about to tell the fall of Troy, and Ovid's frogs, still cursing under water. Speaking goal: say while, until, before and after; make a deal with "provided that"; say "although"; and say "there's nobody who…"*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Dum Avia abest, Avus cāseum spectat · Exspectā dum Avia redeat · Spectā, dummodo nē tangās! · Postquam Avia exiit, Avus surrēxit · Quamquam Avus ēsurit, cāseum nōn edit · Nēmō est quīn cāseum amet*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** temporal clause (the time clause) · proviso clause (the provided-that clause; old-school *clause of proviso*) · concessive clause (the although clause) · relative clause of characteristic (the kind-of clause)
    - **Forms:** nothing new to build! The subjunctives you know (Chapters 49, 52, 53) and Chapter 54's matching rule.
    - **Laws:** the Law of the *Dum* Present · the Law of the Four *Dum*s · the Law of Before · the Law of After · the Law of Although · the Law of the Kind-Of Clause · the Law of the Little Words
    - **Words:** *dum* · *dummodo, modo* · *antequam, priusquam* · *postquam* · *ubi* (when) · *simul atque* · *quamquam* · *quamvīs* · *licet* (although) · *sunt quī*
    - **Reading:** *Exspectā dum dormiat!* (day twenty-seven: Grandma goes out again, and Grandpa waits for the dog to fall asleep)
    - **Speaking:** say while, until, before, after and as soon as; make a deal with "provided that"; say "although"; say "there's nobody who…"

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. For every little joining word today, ask one question: **fact or not-yet?** A fact takes the fact mood (indicative). Something waited for, aimed at, granted, or only imagined takes the subjunctive.

## 1. *Dum*: while

:: Dum spīrō, spērō. || While I breathe, I hope. (proverb; Chapter 5)

Dum… dum… dum…

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 5's motto: *Dum spīrō, spērō.* What does *dum* mean, and which mood is *spīrō*? | "**While** I breathe, I hope." *Dum* = "while." *Spīrō* is the plain **fact mood** (indicative), present. Breathing is a fact. |
| "While Grandma is away, Grandpa looks at the cheese." (*Absum*: be away.) | ***Dum Avia abest, Avus cāseum spectat.*** Fact mood in both halves. |
| Now a past story: *Dum Avia abest, Avus cāseum tetigit.* Translate. Which verb tells you it's the past? | "**While** Grandma **was** away, Grandpa touched the cheese." Only ***tetigit*** (perfect) is past. *Abest* stays **present**. |
| Why does Latin keep *abest* in the present, when English says "was away"? | *Dum* "while" just marks a **stretch of time**, and something happens inside it. Latin puts that stretch in the **present** whatever the story's time; the main verb sets the clock. It works like Chapter 54's historic present: a past story told in the present. |
| Grammar books call a clause that tells **when** a **temporal clause**. Plain words? | **The time clause** (*tempus*, time: Chapter 54's *Ōrdō temporum*). *Dum…, cum…* (Chapter 55), *antequam…, postquam…*: each one starts a time clause. Today's job is to know which take the fact mood and which the subjunctive. |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.46.1, 58 BC. Caesar is at a parley with the German king Ariovistus: *Dum haec in conloquiō geruntur, Caesarī nūntiātum est equitēs Ariovistī propius tumulum accēdere.* (*Conloquium*: parley; *geruntur*: are being done, passive of *gerō*; *equitēs*: horsemen; *propius*: nearer; *tumulus*: mound; *accēdō*: come near.) Translate. Which verb is present though the story is past? | "**While** this **was going on** at the parley, word was brought to Caesar that Ariovistus's horsemen were coming nearer to the mound." ***Geruntur*** is present after *dum*. *Nūntiātum est* (Chapter 31's perfect passive) sets the time, and *accēdere* is Chapter 42's "say that" infinitive. *Dum haec geruntur*, "while this was going on," is a Caesar favorite. |

!!! success "The Law of the *Dum* Present"
    ***Dum*** = "**while**" (a stretch of time with something happening inside it) takes the **present indicative**, whatever the time of the main verb: *Dum Avia abest, Avus cāseum spectat / tetigit.* In English, a past story turns it into "was": "while Grandma **was** away."

## 2. *Dum*: as long as

:: Ambulāte dum lūcem habētis. || Walk while you have the light. (John 12:35, Vulgate)

Dum… tam diū…

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Dum Avia aberat, Avus laetus erat.* Translate. Why is *aberat* past this time? | "**As long as** Grandma was away, Grandpa was happy." The two stretches are **the same length**: they start together and end together. Then *dum* = "**as long as**," and both verbs take the **same** tense. |
| Chapter 49's student song: *Gaudeāmus igitur, iuvenēs dum sumus.* "While" or "as long as"? | "So let us rejoice **while** (as long as) we are young." The rejoicing lasts exactly as long as the being young: *sumus* is present like *gaudeāmus*. |
| Jesus, John 12:35: *Ambulāte dum lūcem habētis.* (*Ambulō*: walk, Chapter 5; *lūx, lūcis*: light.) Translate. | "Walk **while** (as long as) you all have the light." The walking lasts as long as the light. |
| Chapter 43's Lectiō: *Et cāseō vescēbātur dum Pater cantābat!* Why *cantābat*, and not *cantat*? | He fed **for exactly as long as** Dad sang: the same stretch, so the **same tense**, imperfect: "as long as." The present after *dum* is for "while": a stretch with one event **inside** it (*Dum Avia abest, Avus cāseum tetigit*). |

## 3. *Dum*: until

:: Exspectā dum Avia redeat. || Wait until Grandma comes back. (teaching line)

Exspectā dum…

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Exspectā dum Avia redeat.* Which mood is *redeat*? Translate. | The **subjunctive** (present, of *redeō*). "Wait **until** Grandma comes back." After a verb of **waiting**, *dum* means "**until**." |
| Why the subjunctive? | Grandma isn't back yet. Her coming back is **expected**, not a fact: you wait **for** it, like Chapter 51's goals. Grammar books call this the **anticipatory subjunctive**: plain words, "**the waiting subjunctive**." |
| Put it on the past team: "Grandpa waited until the dog fell asleep." | ***Avus exspectāvit dum canis dormīret.*** Imperfect subjunctive: Chapter 54's matching rule. "He waited **for** the dog to sleep." |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 4.23.4, 55 BC, his first landing in Britain. His first ships reach the coast and find the cliffs lined with armed Britons: *dum reliquae nāvēs eō convenīrent ad hōram nōnam in ancorīs exspectāvit.* (*Reliquus*: remaining; *nāvis*: ship; *eō*: to that place; *conveniō*: gather; *hōra nōna*: the ninth hour, mid-afternoon; *in ancorīs*: at anchor, Chapter 58's *ancora*.) Translate. Why *convenīrent*? | "He waited at anchor until the ninth hour **for the rest of the ships to gather** there." The ships were **expected**: waiting subjunctive, imperfect on the past team. |
| Does *dum* "until" ever take the fact mood? | **Rarely**, for a plain fact ("until it actually happened"). Classical writers prefer another word for that, ***dōnec*** (Chapter 27 glossed it as "as long as"; it also means "until"). For you: "**wait until**" = ***dum* + subjunctive**. |

## 4. *Dum*, *modo*, *dummodo*: provided that

:: Ōderint, dum metuant. || Let them hate, provided they fear. (a line from an old Roman tragedy, quoted by Cicero, *On Duties* 1.97)

Dum… modo… dummodo… nē…

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Avus cāseum spectet, dum nē tangat.* (*Spectet*: "let him look," Chapter 49.) Translate. | "Let Grandpa look at the cheese, **provided that** he **doesn't** touch it." A fourth *dum*: "**provided that, so long as only**," with the **subjunctive**, and ***nē*** for "not." |
| Grammar books call this a **proviso clause**. Plain words? Which words start one? | **The provided-that clause**: it sets a condition for a deal. Old-school *clause of proviso* (*prōvīsō*: "it being provided"). It starts with ***dum***, ***modo*** ("only") or ***dummodo*** (*dum* + *modo*), always + **subjunctive**; "not" is ***nē***. |
| Cicero, *On Duties* 1.97, quotes a line from an old Roman tragedy, spoken by the cruel King Atreus: *ōderint, dum metuant.* (*Ōdī*, Chapter 46: "I hate," perfect forms with a present meaning; *ōderint* is its perfect subjunctive, Chapter 53's *-erint*; *metuō*, Chapter 58.) Translate. | "**Let them hate, provided they fear.**" *Ōderint* is a "let them…" (Chapter 49's jussive, built on *ōdī*); ***metuant*** is the proviso. Cicero says the audience applauds the line because it fits a villain like Atreus; from a just king it would be all wrong. |
| Cicero, *Against Catiline* 1.10, November 63 BC. He tells Catiline to leave Rome and take his gang with him: *Magnō mē metū līberābis, dum modo inter mē atque tē mūrus intersit.* (*Metus, metūs*: fear; *līberō*: free, + ablative "from"; *mūrus*: wall; *intersum*: be between.) Translate. | "You'll free me from a great fear, **provided only that** there's a wall between me and you." *Dum modo* (often written as one word, ***dummodo***) + subjunctive ***intersit***. The wall: Rome's city wall. |
| "Grandpa may eat, provided he doesn't eat the whole cheese." (*Licet*, Chapter 44; *tōtus*: whole.) | ***Licet Avō edere, dummodo nē tōtum cāseum edat.*** (or *dum nē* / *modo nē*.) The proviso's "not" is ***nē***, never *nōn*. |

!!! success "The Law of the Four *Dum*s"
    | *Dum* means… | Mood | Example |
    |---|---|---|
    | **while** (something happens inside the stretch) | **present** indicative, even in a past story | *Dum Avia abest, Avus cāseum tetigit.* |
    | **as long as** (the same stretch) | indicative, **same tense** as the main verb | *Dum Avia aberat, Avus laetus erat.* |
    | **until** (after waiting) | **subjunctive** (it's expected) | *Exspectā dum Avia redeat.* |
    | **provided that** (also *modo, dummodo*) | **subjunctive**; "not" = ***nē*** | *Spectā, dummodo nē tangās!* |

## 5. Before: *antequam* and *priusquam*

:: Antequam Avia exiit, cāseōs numerāvit. || Before Grandma went out, she counted the cheeses. (teaching line)

Ante… quam… prius… quam…

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Ante* means "before" (Chapter 35's *multō ante*, "long before"), *quam* means "than" (Chapter 33). Glue them: ***antequam*** = "before." Translate *Antequam Avia exiit, cāseōs numerāvit.* Which mood is *exiit*? | "**Before** Grandma went out, she counted the cheeses." ***Exiit***: fact mood, perfect. She really did go out (just later). |
| ***Priusquam***: *prius*, "sooner," + *quam*, "than": "sooner than" = "before." Latin often splits it in two: *Avia nōn prius exiit quam cāseōs numerāvit.* Translate. | "Grandma did**n't** go out **before** she had counted the cheeses" = "She didn't leave **until** she'd counted them." Read *prius … quam* together as one word. *Antequam* splits the same way: *ante … quam*. |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.53.1, 58 BC. Ariovistus's Germans are beaten and run: *nec prius fugere dēstitērunt quam ad flūmen Rhēnum mīlia passuum ex eō locō circiter L pervēnērunt.* (*Dēsistō, dēsistere, dēstitī*: stop; *Rhēnus*: the Rhine; *mīlia passuum*: miles; *circiter*: about; L = 50.) Translate. Which mood is *pervēnērunt*, and why? | "And they did **not** stop running **until** (before) they reached the river Rhine, about fifty miles from that place." ***Pervēnērunt***: fact mood, perfect. They really got there. |
| "Before Grandma comes back, I'll guard the cheese." Which tense for "comes back"? | ***Antequam Avia redit, cāseum custōdiam.*** For a "before" in the **future**, Latin prose uses the **present** indicative (or the future perfect, *redierit*), just as English says "before she **comes**." |

## 6. Before it could: the waiting subjunctive again

:: Antequam gallus cantet, ter mē negābis. || Before the rooster crows, you will deny me three times. (Matthew 26:34, Vulgate)

Priusquam… ederet…

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Canis cāseum cēpit priusquam Avus ederet.* Which mood is *ederet*? Translate. | Imperfect **subjunctive**. "The dog took the cheese **before Grandpa could eat** it." Grandpa never ate it: the eating was only **expected**, and the dog headed it off. |
| Compare *Antequam Avia exiit…* and *…priusquam Avus ederet.* What's the rule? | **Fact mood**: the "before" thing **really happened** (just later). **Subjunctive**: the "before" thing was **waited for, planned, or headed off**: "before it **could**." It's section 3's waiting subjunctive again. |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.19.3, the very next line after Chapter 58's fear about Diviciacus's feelings: *Itaque prius quam quicquam cōnārētur, Dīviciācum ad sē vocārī iubet.* (*Itaque*: and so; *quicquam*: anything; *cōnor*: try, Chapter 43.) Translate. Why subjunctive? | "And so, **before he tried** anything, he has Diviciacus called to him." Nothing had been tried yet: the trying was only **planned**. *Iubet* is a historic present, and *cōnārētur* follows the past team (Chapter 54: after a historic present, either team is allowed). |
| The Last Supper, Matthew 26:34: *In hāc nocte, antequam gallus cantet, ter mē negābis.* (*Gallus*: rooster; *ter*: three times; *negō*: deny, Chapter 42.) Translate. Why *cantet*? | "This night, **before** the rooster **crows**, you will deny me three times." Present subjunctive: the crowing is still to come, looked forward to. (Classical prose would more often say *antequam gallus cantat*; the Bible's Latin likes the subjunctive here.) |

!!! success "The Law of Before"
    ***Antequam*** and ***priusquam*** both mean "**before**" (and both may split: *ante … quam, prius … quam*).

    | The "before" thing… | Mood | Example |
    |---|---|---|
    | **really happened** (a fact) | **indicative**: perfect for the past; present (or future perfect) for the future | *Antequam Avia exiit, cāseōs numerāvit. · Antequam Avia redit, custōdiam.* |
    | was **waited for, planned, headed off** ("before it could") | **subjunctive**, by the matching rule | *Canis cāseum cēpit priusquam Avus ederet.* |

## 7. After, when, as soon as: *postquam, ubi, simul atque*

:: Postquam Avia exiit, Avus surrēxit. || After Grandma went out, Grandpa got up. (teaching line)

Postquam… ubi… simul atque…

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Post* = "after" (Chapter 35's *paulō post*, "a little later"), so ***postquam*** = "after." Translate *Postquam Avia exiit, Avus surrēxit.* Which mood and tense is *exiit*? | "**After** Grandma went out (had gone out), Grandpa got up." **Fact mood, perfect.** English often says "had gone"; Latin is happy with the plain perfect, because the clause just names the event that came first. |
| You know *ubi* as "where?" (Chapter 7). With a past verb and no question: *Ubi Avia rediit, canis lātrāvit.* Translate. | "**When** Grandma came back, the dog barked." *Ubi* + perfect indicative = "**when**, as soon as," just like *postquam*. No question mark, a time clause: "when." |
| ***Simul atque*** (or *simul ac*) = "as soon as." Cicero, *Against Catiline* 1.16: Catiline has walked into the senate and taken a seat: *omnēs cōnsulārēs … simul atque adsēdistī, partem istam subselliōrum nūdam atque inānem relīquērunt.* (*Cōnsulāris*: ex-consul; *adsīdō, adsēdī*: sit down; *subsellium*: bench; *nūdus*: bare; *inānis*: empty.) Translate. | "All the ex-consuls, **as soon as** you sat down, left that part of the benches bare and empty." ***Adsēdistī***: perfect indicative. Chapter 55's ***cum prīmum*** means the same. |
| Caesar twice, *Gallic War* 1.27.3 and 1.7.3. *Eō postquam Caesar pervēnit, obsidēs, arma, servōs … poposcit.* · *Ubi dē eius adventū Helvētiī certiōrēs factī sunt, lēgātōs ad eum mittunt.* (*Eō*: there; *obses, obsidis*: hostage; *poscō, poscere, poposcī*: demand; *adventus*: arrival; *certiōrem facere*: inform, "make surer.") Translate both. | "**After** Caesar arrived there, he demanded hostages, weapons, slaves…" · "**When** the Helvetians were informed of his arrival, they send envoys to him." (Chapter 54 met the rest of that second line.) Both time clauses: **perfect indicative**. |
| *Post cēnam* and *postquam cēnāvimus*: what's the difference? | ***Post*** is a preposition with a noun: "after **dinner**." ***Postquam*** starts a clause with a verb: "after **we had dinner**." Same pair as *ante* / *antequam*. |

!!! success "The Law of After"
    ***Postquam*** (after), ***ubi*** (when), ***simul atque / simul ac*** (as soon as), and Chapter 55's ***cum prīmum*** take the **fact mood**, usually the **perfect**, where English often says "had": *Postquam Avia exiit, Avus surrēxit.* (Chapter 55's story *cum*, "when," takes the subjunctive; these words don't.)

## 8. Although: *quamquam*

:: Quamquam animus meminisse horret… incipiam. || Although my mind shudders to remember… I will begin. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 2.12–13)

Quamquam… tamen…

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 55: *Cum Avus ēsuriat, tamen cāseum nōn edit.* Another "although": *Quamquam Avus ēsurit, cāseum nōn edit.* Which mood is *ēsurit*? Translate. | Fact mood. "**Although** Grandpa **is** hungry, he isn't eating the cheese." ***Quamquam*** grants a **fact**: he really is hungry. |
| Chapter 55 called *cum* "although" **concessive**. Plain words for a **concessive clause**? | **The although clause** (*concēdō*, grant): it grants something that clashes with the main clause. The main clause often has Chapter 38's ***tamen***, "still." |
| Vergil, *Aeneid* 2.12–13. Queen Dido has asked Aeneas to tell how Troy fell: *quamquam animus meminisse horret lūctūque refūgit, / incipiam.* (*Animus*: mind; *meminisse*, Chapter 46: to remember; *horreō*: shudder at; *lūctus*: grief; *refugiō, refūgī*: shrink back.) Translate. Why *horret*? | "**Although** my mind shudders to remember, and has shrunk back in grief, I will begin." ***Horret***: fact mood after *quamquam*. His mind really does shudder. |
| Cicero, *Against Catiline* 1.22, a sentence that **starts** with it: *Quamquam quid loquor?* (*Loquor*, Chapter 43.) Translate. | "**And yet**, what am I saying?" At the **head of a sentence**, with no main clause to clash with, *quamquam* means "**and yet**." |

## 9. Although: *quamvīs* and *licet*

:: Quamvīs sint sub aquā, sub aquā maledīcere temptant. || Though they are under water, under water they try to curse. (Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 6.376)

Quamvīs… licet…

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| ***Quamvīs*** = *quam* "as" + *vīs* "you want" (Chapter 18): "as much as you like." *Quamvīs Avus ēsuriat, cāseum nōn edet.* Which mood? Translate. | **Subjunctive**. "**However hungry** Grandpa **may be**, he won't eat the cheese" = "Let him be as hungry as you like…" *Quamvīs* grants something as a **supposal**, so the subjunctive. |
| Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 6.376. Rude farmers who wouldn't let the goddess Latona drink have been turned into frogs, and still they won't stop: *quamvīs sint sub aquā, sub aquā maledīcere temptant.* (*Maledīcō*: speak ill, curse; *temptō*: try, Chapter 58's Lectiō.) Translate. | "**Although** they are under water, under water they (still) try to curse." *Quamvīs* + ***sint***. Say *sub aquā, sub aquā* aloud: many readers hear the frogs croaking in it, *-quā, -quā*. |
| *Licet*, Chapter 44: "it's allowed." With a subjunctive clause it means "**granted that, although**": *Licet Avus clāmet, cāseum nōn dabō.* Translate. | "**Although** Grandpa shouts (he may shout all he likes), I won't give him the cheese." *Licet* is itself a present verb, so its clause takes only the **present or perfect** subjunctive (the now team). |
| *Quamquam Avus fessus est* or *Quamvīs Avus fessus sit*: what's the difference? | ***Quamquam … est***: "although Grandpa **is** tired" (a fact, granted). ***Quamvīs … sit***: "however tired Grandpa **may be**" (a supposal). In Cicero and Caesar the rule holds; poets sometimes blur it. |

!!! success "The Law of Although"
    | Word | Mood | Feel | Example |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | ***quamquam*** | **indicative** | although (it's a fact) | *Quamquam Avus ēsurit, nōn edit.* |
    | ***quamvīs*** | **subjunctive** | however much, although | *Quamvīs Avus ēsuriat, nōn edet.* |
    | ***licet*** | **subjunctive** (present or perfect) | granted that, although | *Licet Avus clāmet, nōn dabō.* |
    | ***cum*** (Chapter 55) | **subjunctive** | although | *Cum Avus ēsuriat, tamen nōn edit.* |

    A ***tamen*** in the main clause is the signpost. A *quamquam* that **starts a sentence** on its own means "**and yet**."

## 10. The kind-of clause

:: Sunt quī cāseum nōn ament. || There are people who don't like cheese. (teaching line; Grandpa can't believe it)

Sunt quī… nēmō est quī… quis est quī…?

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 51: *Puer quī cantat meus est* (fact mood). Now: *Sunt quī cāseum nōn ament.* Which mood is *ament*? Translate. | Subjunctive. "There are **people who don't** like cheese." The *quī* clause doesn't point at a particular person you could name; it describes a **kind** of person. |
| Grammar books call this a **relative clause of characteristic**. Plain words? | **The kind-of clause**: *quī, quae, quod* + **subjunctive**, saying what **sort** of person or thing ("the sort who would…"). Old-school *relative clause of characteristic* (a "characteristic" is a mark of a kind). |
| It loves these openings: *sunt quī* (there are some who), *nēmō est quī* (there's nobody who), *quis est quī…?* (who is there who…?), *is quī* (the sort of person who). Translate *Nēmō est quī cāseum nōn amet.* | "There's **nobody who doesn't** like cheese." Subjunctive ***amet***: it describes a kind of person (one who doesn't like cheese) and says there is none. |
| *Avus est quī cāseum ēdit* and *Avus nōn is est quī cāseum relinquat.* (*Relinquō*: leave behind.) What's the difference? | The first is a **fact** (indicative *ēdit*): "Grandpa is the one who **ate** the cheese." The second is a **kind**: "Grandpa isn't **the sort to leave** cheese behind." ***Is … quī*** + subjunctive = "the sort of person who would." |
| Cicero, *Against Catiline* 1.30: *Quamquam nōn nūllī sunt in hōc ōrdine, quī aut ea, quae imminent, nōn videant aut ea, quae vident, dissimulent.* (*Nōn nūllī*: some, "not none"; *ōrdō*: here, the senate; *immineō*: hang over, threaten; *dissimulō*: pretend not to see.) Translate. Which *quī* clause is a kind, and which are facts? | "**And yet** there are some in this house **who** either **don't see** what is hanging over us, or **pretend not to see** what they do see." ***Videant, dissimulent***: subjunctive, the kind-of clause after *sunt*. *Imminent, vident*: plain fact clauses. And the opening *Quamquam* is section 8's "and yet." |
| Cicero's Cato, *On Old Age* 24: *nēmō enim est tam senex quī sē annum nōn putet posse vīvere.* (*Senex*: old man; *putō*: think; *annum*: for a year, the accusative of "how long.") Translate. | "For **nobody** is so old **that he doesn't think** he can live one more year." ***Tam … quī*** works like Chapter 52's *tam … ut*: the kind-of clause and the result clause are cousins. |

## 11. *Quīn* = *quī nōn*

:: Nēmō est quīn cāseum amet. || There's nobody who doesn't love cheese. (teaching line)

Quī nōn… quīn…

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Nēmō est quī cāseum nōn amet.* Chapter 58 taught *quīn* after a "no." Shorter: *Nēmō est quīn cāseum amet.* What does *quīn* stand for here? | ***Quīn* = *quī nōn***, "who … not." The same sentence: "There's nobody **who doesn't** love cheese." After a **negative** (*nēmō, nihil*, or a question expecting "nobody"), *quīn* can replace *quī nōn*. |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 6.39.2, 53 BC. German horsemen suddenly attack a Roman camp while many of the men are out gathering grain, and the new recruits panic: *Nēmō est tam fortis quīn reī novitāte perturbētur.* (*Novitās*: newness, surprise; *perturbō*: throw into confusion.) Translate. | "**Nobody** is so brave **that he isn't** thrown into confusion by the surprise of the thing." *Quīn* = *quī nōn* after ***nēmō … tam***. |
| *Quis est quīn cāseum amet?* Translate. Why is *quīn* allowed with no *nōn* or *nēmō*? | "Who is there **who doesn't** love cheese?" The question expects the answer "**nobody**," so it counts as a negative. |
| Can you say *Sunt quīn cāseum ament* for "there are some who don't love cheese"? | **No.** *Quīn* needs a **negative** in front (or a "nobody?" question). With *sunt*, say ***Sunt quī cāseum nōn ament.*** |

!!! success "The Law of the Kind-Of Clause"
    A ***quī*** clause in the **subjunctive** describes a **kind** of person or thing, not a fact about one person:

    | Opening | Example | Means |
    |---|---|---|
    | ***sunt quī*** | *Sunt quī cāseum nōn ament.* | There are people who don't like cheese. |
    | ***nēmō est quī*** · ***quīn*** | *Nēmō est quī cāseum nōn amet = Nēmō est quīn cāseum amet.* | There's nobody who doesn't like cheese. |
    | ***quis est quī…?*** | *Quis est quī hoc nesciat?* | Who is there who doesn't know this? |
    | ***is quī*** · ***tam … quī*** | *Avus nōn is est quī cāseum relinquat.* | Grandpa isn't the sort to leave cheese. |

    Fact mood = a fact about one: *Avus est quī cāseum ēdit.* ***Quīn* = *quī nōn*** only after a negative.

## 12. Which word, which mood?

:: Prīmum quaere modum! || First look for the mood! (teaching line; Chapter 55)

Quod verbum? Quī modus?

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Dum Avus dormit, canis vigilat.* | "**While** Grandpa sleeps, the dog keeps watch." *Dum* "while": **present indicative**. |
| *Avus exspectat dum canis dormiat.* | "Grandpa is waiting **until** the dog falls asleep." *Dum* after waiting: **subjunctive** (expected). |
| *Avus cāseum tangat, dummodo nē edat.* | "Let Grandpa touch the cheese, **provided** he doesn't eat it." Proviso: **subjunctive**, *nē*. |
| *Postquam canis dormīvit, Avus surrēxit.* | "**After** the dog fell asleep, Grandpa got up." *Postquam*: **perfect indicative**. |
| *Quamvīs canis dormiat, Avus timet.* | "**However** soundly the dog may sleep, Grandpa is afraid." *Quamvīs*: **subjunctive**. |
| *Nēmō est quī canem nōn timeat.* | "There's **nobody who doesn't** fear the dog." Kind-of clause: **subjunctive** (or *Nēmō est quīn canem timeat*). |

!!! success "The Law of the Little Words"
    | Fact mood (indicative) | Subjunctive |
    |---|---|
    | *dum* while (present) · *dum* as long as | *dum* until (after waiting) · *dum, modo, dummodo* provided that (*nē*) |
    | *antequam, priusquam* (it really happened) | *antequam, priusquam* (before it could; waited for) |
    | *postquam, ubi, simul atque, cum prīmum* (after, when, as soon as) | Chapter 55's story *cum* |
    | *quamquam* although (a fact) | *quamvīs, licet, cum* although (granted) |
    | *quī* + a fact about one | *sunt quī, nēmō est quī / quīn, is quī*: a kind |

    One question decides: **a fact, or something waited for, granted, or imagined?**

## 15. Lectiō: *Exspectā dum dormiat!*

:: Canis numquam dormit. || The dog never sleeps. (teaching line)

Domī… dum Avia abest…

Diē vīcēsimā septimā: the day after Chapter 58. One cheese is left, the big one, and Grandma has to go out again. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. Find every *dum* (and say which kind), every before/after/as soon as, every "although," every kind-of clause. New in passing: *vīcēsimus septimus*, twenty-seventh; *restō*, be left; *diū*, for a long time; *simulō*, pretend; *culter, cultrī*, knife (Chapter 37); *secō, secāre, secuī*, cut (Chapter 37); *ōs, ōris*, mouth (Chapter 14); *ut ait*, as (he) says (*ait*, Chapter 43); *poēta*, poet; *dīvidō, dīvidere, dīvīsī*, divide.

::: dialogue lectio
Iōannēs | Diē vīcēsimā septimā Avia iterum exīre parat. Ūnus cāseus restat, magnus. || John | On the twenty-seventh day Grandma gets ready to go out again. One cheese is left, a big one.
Avia | Ave, cāseum spectā, dummodo nē tangās! || Grandma | Grandpa, look at the cheese, as long as you don't touch it!
Avia | Iōannēs, dum absum, vigilā! Et canis vigilet. || Grandma | John, while I'm away, stay awake! And let the dog keep watch.
Avus | Ī, Avia! Nōs hīc exspectābimus dum redeās. || Grandpa | Go, Grandma! We'll wait here until you come back.
Iōannēs | Avia exit. Dum Avus cāseum spectat, canis Avum spectat. || John | Grandma goes out. While Grandpa watches the cheese, the dog watches Grandpa.
Avus | Iōannēs, sunt quī dīcant canēs numquam dormīre. Sed nēmō est quīn tandem dormiat. || Grandpa | John, there are people who say dogs never sleep. But there's nobody who doesn't fall asleep in the end.
Avus | Exspectābō dum canis dormiat. || Grandpa | I'll wait until the dog falls asleep.
Iōannēs | Quamquam Avus diū exspectat, canis nōn dormit. || John | Although Grandpa waits a long time, the dog doesn't sleep.
Avus | Canis bone, quamvīs fortis sīs, fessus es. Dormī! || Grandpa | Good dog, however brave you may be, you're tired. Sleep!
Iōannēs | Tandem canis oculōs claudit. Simul atque oculōs clausit, Avus surgit. || John | At last the dog closes his eyes. As soon as he has closed them, Grandpa gets up.
Avus | Iōannēs, nōlī clāmāre! Parvum frūstum tantum… || Grandpa | John, don't shout! Just a small scrap…
Iōannēs | Ave, Avia dīxit: spectā, dummodo nē tangās! || John | Grandpa, Grandma said: look, as long as you don't touch!
Avus | Nōn tangam, Iōannēs. Cultrō secābō! || Grandpa | I won't touch it, John. I'll cut it with a knife!
Iōannēs | Postquam Avus cultrum cēpit, frūstum secuit. || John | After Grandpa picked up the knife, he cut a scrap.
Iōannēs | Sed antequam Avus frūstum ad ōs ferret, canis lātrāvit! || John | But before Grandpa could bring the scrap to his mouth, the dog barked!
Avus | Ēheu! Canis nōn dormiēbat! || Grandpa | Alas! The dog wasn't sleeping!
Iōannēs | Canis dormīre simulābat. Canis nōn is est quī in officiō dormiat. || John | The dog was pretending to sleep. The dog isn't the sort to sleep on duty.
Iōannēs | Ubi canis lātrāvit, Avia intrāvit. || John | When the dog barked, Grandma came in.
Avia | Quid agitur? Ave, cūr cultrum tenēs? || Grandma | What's going on? Grandpa, why are you holding a knife?
Avus | Nōn tetigī, Avia! Tū dīxistī: spectā, dummodo nē tangās. Cultrō tantum secuī! || Grandpa | I didn't touch it, Grandma! You said: look, as long as you don't touch. I only cut with the knife!
Avia | Ō Ave! Nēmō est quī tē nōn rīdeat. || Grandma | Oh, Grandpa! There's nobody who doesn't laugh at you.
Avia | Canem laudō. Ut ait poēta: Ōderint, dum metuant! || Grandma | I praise the dog. As the poet says: let them hate, provided they fear!
Iōannēs | Quamquam Avus cāseum secuit, Avia rīdet. || John | Although Grandpa cut the cheese, Grandma laughs.
Avia | Frūstum tuum tenē, Ave, dummodo nē plūs secēs. Sed prius canī partem dā! || Grandma | Keep your scrap, Grandpa, provided you don't cut any more. But first give the dog a share!
Avus | Dum vīvam, canī nihil dabō! … Bene, dabō. || Grandpa | As long as I live, I'll give the dog nothing! … All right, I'll give it.
Iōannēs | Avus frūstum dīvīsit. Sed antequam Avus suam partem ederet, canis suam iam ēderat! || John | Grandpa divided the scrap. But before Grandpa could eat his part, the dog had already eaten his!
Iōannēs | Crās, dum Avia abest, Avus iterum exspectābit dum canis dormiat. Et ego quoque vigilābō! || John | Tomorrow, while Grandma is away, Grandpa will wait again until the dog sleeps. And I'll stay awake too!
:::

{#g-15}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Find every *dum* and say which of the four it is. | "While": ***dum absum · Dum Avus cāseum spectat · dum Avia abest*** (present). "As long as": ***Dum vīvam*** (future, the same tense as *dabō*: the same stretch). "Until": ***exspectābimus dum redeās · Exspectābō dum canis dormiat · exspectābit dum canis dormiat*** (subjunctive). "Provided that": ***dummodo nē tangās*** (three times) · ***dum metuant · dummodo nē plūs secēs***. |
| Find the "before," "after," "when" and "as soon as" clauses. Which are fact mood, and which subjunctive? | Fact mood: ***Simul atque oculōs clausit · Postquam Avus cultrum cēpit · Ubi canis lātrāvit*** (all perfect). Subjunctive: ***antequam Avus frūstum ad ōs ferret · antequam Avus suam partem ederet***: the eating never happened, the dog headed it off. |
| Find the "although"s. Why is one indicative and one subjunctive? | ***Quamquam Avus diū exspectat · Quamquam Avus cāseum secuit***: *quamquam*, facts (indicative). ***Quamvīs fortis sīs***: *quamvīs*, "however brave you may be" (subjunctive). |
| Find the kind-of clauses, including the one with *quīn*. | ***sunt quī dīcant · nēmō est quīn tandem dormiat · Canis nōn is est quī in officiō dormiat · Nēmō est quī tē nōn rīdeat.*** All subjunctive. *Quīn* = *quī nōn*. |
| Grandma quotes "the poet": *Ōderint, dum metuant.* Where is it from, and what does *dum* mean in it? | The cruel King Atreus's line from an old Roman tragedy (Cicero, *On Duties* 1.97; section 4). *Dum* + subjunctive = "**provided that**": "let them hate, **provided** they fear." Grandpa may hate the dog, as long as he fears it. |
| Why is Grandpa's excuse *Cultrō tantum secuī* a lawyer's trick? | Grandma's deal was ***dummodo nē tangās***, "provided you don't **touch**." Grandpa kept to the letter of the proviso (the knife touched it, not his hand) and broke its spirit. |
