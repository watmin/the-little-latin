# Caput LX: Recapitulātiō V

*Liber V, Modus Subiūnctīvus · Chapter 60*

*In which nothing is new and every subjunctive comes home. Book V gave you the mood of "maybe": let's and let him, wishes and "would," goals and results, tucked-in questions, the matching rule, the story* cum, *tucked-in orders, the four ifs, fears and "no doubt," and the little time words. Today they all meet. It's the twenty-eighth day; one big cheese is left, and Grandpa is still waiting for the dog to fall asleep. So he reads the kids the life of the cleverest enemy Rome ever had: Hannibal of Carthage, as Cornelius Nepos told it. Hannibal swore as a boy never to be Rome's friend, marched elephants over the Alps, and once fooled a whole island with jars of lead. It's adapted from Nepos, then lived (badly) by the family in the kitchen. Speaking goal: tell a real Roman story with every Book V subjunctive.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Eāmus! · Utinam adessēs! · Edō ut vīvam · tam fessus ut dormīret · Nesciō quid fēcerīs · Cum Avia vēnisset… · Imperat ut veniam · Sī rēx essem… · Timeō nē mordeat · Exspectā dum dormiat*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** nothing new. This is a **recapitulātiō**, a "re-heading": going back over the main points (English "recap"). One old word explained: *Poenus*, "a Carthaginian" (English *Punic*)
    - **Forms:** the four subjunctives: present (*amem, habeam, sim*), imperfect (*amārem, essem*), perfect (*amāverim, fuerim*), pluperfect (*amāvissem, fuissem*), active and passive; and the matching rule that picks among them
    - **Laws:** all of Book V's, side by side: Three Moods, Swapped Vowel, Let's, Let Him, and What Now? · If Only, Would and Might, Polite Ask · Goal, *Nē*, Sending *Quī* · Infinitive Plus, Past Goal, So … That, Two Nots · Tucked-In Question, *-erim*, Infinitive Plus Again, Now and Earlier · Two Teams, "Have Done", Free Result, Matching Rule · Two *Cum*s, Plain "When", Story *Cum*, "Since" and "Although" · Person Asked, Tucked-In Command, *Iubeō* and *Vetō*, "That" and "To" · Fact If, *Nisi* and *Sīn*, Should-Would, Not Really, Four Ifs, Frozen Clock · Backwards Fear, Fear Clock, Calm Fear, Afraid To, Stop-It Clause, *Quīn*, No Doubt, Which Word · *Dum* Present, Four *Dum*s, Before, After, Although, Kind-Of Clause, Little Words
    - **Words:** a few for the story: Carthaginian, swear, oath, altar, friendship, clever, greedy, gold, silver, lead, keep, trick, elephant
    - **Reading:** *Hannibal* (Nepos's life of Hannibal, adapted into Book V Latin) and *Lapis in pannō* (the end of the cheese, and of Book V)
    - **Speaking:** tell Hannibal's story yourself, with every Book V subjunctive

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. This chapter goes fast on purpose: if a group feels shaky, its answers name the chapter to go back to. For every subjunctive, ask Chapter 55's question first: *Prīmum quaere modum!* Then ask what job the clause does. The last sections are the real exam: the story, Nepos's own words, and *Quid didicistī?*

## 1. *Eāmus!*: the mood of maybe

:: Surgite, eāmus hinc. || Get up, let's go from here. (John 14:31; Chapter 49)

Īmus. Īte! Eāmus!

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the Three Moods: read the blurb. Name the mood of each, in plain words. | ***Īmus***: "we go," the **fact** mood (indicative). ***Īte!***: "go, y'all!", the **command** mood (imperative). ***Eāmus!***: "let's go!", the **maybe / let's / should** mood (subjunctive). (Chapter 49.) |
| Law of the Swapped Vowel: the "I" form of the present subjunctive of *amō, habeō, dūcō, capiō, audiō*, and of *sum, possum, eō, ferō*. | ***Amem, habeam, dūcam, capiam, audiam · sim, possim, eam, feram.*** The first family swaps *a* for *e*; every other family takes *a*; *sum* and *possum* (with *velim, nōlim, mālim*) take *-im*. |
| Law of Let's, Let Him, and What Now?: "Let's cross the Alps! Let Hannibal come! What am I to do?" (*Trānseō*: cross, Chapter 32's *eō* with *trāns*.) | ***Alpēs trānseāmus! Hannibal veniat! Quid faciam?*** "Let's" is a "we" subjunctive (hortatory); "let him" is a "he" subjunctive (jussive); "what am I to do?" is a question in the subjunctive (deliberative). |
| The "not"s: "Let's not be afraid of the Romans!" "Shouldn't I go?" And Chapter 49's passive and deponent: "Let it be done!" "Let's speak Latin!" | ***Nē Rōmānōs timeāmus! Nōn eam?*** "Let's" and "let him" say "not" with ***nē***; the "what now?" question uses ***nōn***. ***Fīat! Loquāmur Latīnē!*** The same vowel swap in passive clothes. |

## 2. *Utinam!*: wishes, "would," and please

:: Dī tē ament! || May the gods love you! (Plautus, *The Pot of Gold* 183; Chapter 50)

Utinam veniat! Velim cāseum.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of If Only: "If only Hannibal doesn't come!" "May the gods keep you safe!" | ***Utinam nē Hannibal veniat! Dī tē servent!*** A wish is the optative subjunctive, the "if only / may it" subjunctive; a wish that something **won't** happen takes ***nē***. *Utinam* is optional (Chapter 50). |
| Law of Would and Might: *Dīcat aliquis… · Crēdās…* Translate. | "**Someone might say…**" · "**You'd think…**" The potential subjunctive: the "would / might" subjunctive, for what **could** be. |
| Law of the Polite Ask: make these polite. *Volō cāseum! · Dā mihi cāseum! · Quid vīs?* | ***Velim cāseum. · Dā mihi, sīs, cāseum. · Quid velīs?*** "I'd like some cheese" · "Please give me cheese" (*sīs*, *quaesō*, *amābō tē*) · "What would you like?" |
| The wish clock (Chapters 50, 52 and 53): "If only Grandma comes!" "If only Grandma were here!" (she isn't) "If only Grandma had been here!" (she wasn't) | ***Utinam Avia veniat! · Utinam Avia adesset! · Utinam Avia adfuisset!*** Present: still possible. Imperfect: not really, **now**. Pluperfect: not really, **then**. |

## 3. *Ut discam*: goals

:: Dō ut dēs. || I give so that you may give. (Roman law; Chapters 11 and 51)

Ad forum eō ut cāseum emam.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the Goal: "I eat so that I may live." "Grandma goes to the forum to buy cheese." | ***Edō ut vīvam. Avia ad forum it ut cāseum emat.*** A purpose clause (the "so that / in order to" clause): ***ut*** + subjunctive. The goal isn't a fact yet. Latin prose never uses a plain infinitive for a goal. (Chapter 51.) |
| Law of *Nē*: "Grandma hides the cheese so that the dog won't eat it, so that nobody touches it." | ***Avia cāseum cēlat nē canis eum edat, nē quis tangat.*** A goal that should **not** happen takes ***nē***, never *ut nōn*. "So that nobody": *nē quis*. |
| Law of the Sending *Quī*: "Grandma sends Marcus to buy cheese." | ***Avia Marcum mittit quī cāseum emat.*** After **sending** or **choosing**, *quī, quae, quod* + subjunctive says what the person is sent **for**. |
| Nepos, *Life of Hannibal* 7.7: Hannibal has slipped away from Carthage by ship. *Poenī nāvēs duās, quae eum comprehenderent, … mīsērunt.* (*Comprehendō*: seize, arrest.) Translate. Why *quae*, and why the imperfect? And who are the *Poenī*? | "The Carthaginians sent two ships **to seize him**." A sending *quī* (*quae*, to match *nāvēs*): what the ships were sent for. *Mīsērunt* tells a past story, so the goal goes **imperfect** (Chapter 52's Law of the Past Goal). The old word: ***Poenus*** is the Romans' name for a Carthaginian. Carthage was founded by Phoenicians, and *Poenus* is the same word as Greek *Phoinix*, "Phoenician." Its adjective *Pūnicus* gives English "**Punic**," as in the Punic Wars, Rome's three wars with Carthage. Hannibal fought the second. |

## 4. *Tam fessus ut*: the imperfect subjunctive and results

:: Avus tam fessus erat ut ad mēnsam dormīret. || Grandpa was so tired that he fell asleep at the table. (teaching line; Chapter 52)

Amāret, habēret, dūceret, esset, posset, īret.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the Infinitive Plus: build the "he" form of the imperfect subjunctive of *amō, habeō, dūcō, capiō, audiō, sum, possum, eō*. | ***Amāret, habēret, dūceret, caperet, audīret, esset, posset, īret.*** Present infinitive + ending: every verb, every family (*fīō* makes *fierem*). The *ē* is long except before a final *-m, -t, -nt*: *amārēmus*, but *amāret*. (Chapter 52.) |
| Law of the Past Goal: "Hannibal crossed the Alps so that he might come into Italy." | ***Hannibal Alpēs trānsiit ut in Ītaliam venīret.*** A goal in a past story: **imperfect** subjunctive. |
| Law of So … That: Nepos 3.4, Hannibal at the Alps: *effēcit, ut eā elephantus ōrnātus īre posset, quā anteā ūnus homō inermis vix poterat rēpere.* (*Efficiō, effēcī*: bring it about; *eā … quā*: by the way … by which; *ōrnātus*: fully loaded; *inermis*: unarmed; *vix*: barely; *rēpō*: crawl.) Translate. | "He **made it possible** for a fully loaded elephant to go where before one unarmed man could barely crawl." *Efficiō ut* ("bring it about that") works like a result clause with the signpost built in: ***ut*** + imperfect subjunctive *posset* on the past team. *Poterat* is plain fact mood: it's what really was true before. |
| Law of the Two Nots: "Grandma hid the cheese so that Grandpa wouldn't find it." "Grandma hid the cheese so well that Grandpa didn't find it." | ***Avia cāseum cēlāvit nē Avus eum invenīret. · Avia cāseum tam bene cēlāvit ut Avus eum nōn invenīret.*** A **goal** that should not happen: *nē*. A **result** that did not happen: *ut nōn*. The signpost *tam* warns you it's a result. |

## 5. *Rogō quid faciās*: the tucked-in question

:: Nesciō ubi sit. || I don't know where it is. (teaching line; Chapter 53)

Ubi est cāseus? Nesciō ubi sit cāseus.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the Tucked-In Question: read the blurb. Then tuck *Ubi est cāseus?* into a past story: "I asked where the cheese was." | ***Rogāvī ubi cāseus esset.*** Asked straight out, a question keeps its fact mood (*est*); tucked in after a verb of asking, knowing, telling or seeing, it goes **subjunctive** (*sit*, and on the past team *esset*). (Chapter 53.) |
| Law of *-erim*: "Grandpa doesn't know who ate the cheese." And how is *ēderit* different from the future perfect *ēderit*? | ***Avus nescit quis cāseum ēderit.*** Perfect stem + *-erim, -erīs, -erit, -erīmus, -erītis, -erint*. The "he" forms look the same; only the "I" form gives it away: subjunctive *ēderim*, future perfect *ēderō*. The sentence decides. |
| Law of the Infinitive Plus, Again: the pluperfect subjunctive "I" of *amō, sum, faciō, edō*, and the passive of *amō*. Then "If only I hadn't eaten it!" | ***Amāvissem, fuissem, fēcissem, ēdissem · amātus essem.*** Perfect infinitive + ending; passive and deponents: participle + *essem*. ***Utinam nē ēdissem!*** |
| Law of Now and Earlier: "I don't know what Hannibal did." "I didn't know what Hannibal had done." "I didn't know what Hannibal was going to do." | ***Nesciō quid Hannibal fēcerit. · Nesciēbam quid Hannibal fēcisset. · Nesciēbam quid Hannibal factūrus esset.*** Two questions: **now or past?** and **same time, earlier, or later?** Later uses the *-ūrus* participle + *sim / essem*. |
| Nepos 5.4, after listing Hannibal's victories: *hoc ūnum satis erit dictum, ex quō intellegī possit, quantus ille fuerit.* (*Satis*: enough; *ex quō*: from which; *intellegī*: to be understood; *quantus*: how great.) Translate. Why *fuerit*? | "This one thing will be enough to say, from which it **can be understood how great he was**." ***Quantus ille fuerit*** is a tucked-in question: perfect subjunctive, **earlier** than the now-team verb (*possit*, after the future *erit*). *Ex quō … possit* is a *quī* clause in the subjunctive: "the sort of thing from which one can understand" (Chapter 59's kind-of clause). |

## 6. *Ōrdō temporum*: the matching rule

:: Verbum rēgnat, subiūnctīvus sequitur. || The verb rules; the subjunctive follows. (teaching line; Chapter 54)

Rogō quid faciās. Rogāvī quid facerēs.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the Two Teams: sort these main verbs. *Rogō · rogābam · rogāvī* ("I asked") *· rogābō · rogā! · rogāveram*. | **Now team** (primary): ***rogō, rogābō, rogā!*** → present or perfect subjunctive. **Past team** (secondary, historic): ***rogābam, rogāvī, rogāveram*** → imperfect or pluperfect subjunctive. (Chapter 54.) |
| Law of "Have Done": *Ēmī ut edāmus.* Why a present subjunctive after a perfect? | "**I have bought** (it) so that we may eat." A perfect that means "have done," looking from today at something still true now, may play on the **now team**. |
| Law of the Free Result: Nepos 5.2. Hannibal, trapped by night, ties burning bundles of sticks to the horns of a herd of young bulls and lets them loose: *tantum terrōrem iniēcit exercituī Rōmānōrum, ut ēgredī extrā vallum nēmō sit ausus.* (*Iniciō, iniēcī*: throw into; *ēgredior*: go out, a deponent; *extrā vallum*: outside the rampart; *ausus sit*: from *audeō*, Chapter 43's half-deponent.) Translate. Why *sit ausus*, not *esset ausus*? | "He threw **so much terror** into the army of the Romans **that nobody dared** to go out beyond the rampart." A **result** after a past verb may take the **perfect** subjunctive, to stress that it really happened. And it's a result that did **not** happen, so *ut … nēmō*, not *nē* (Chapter 52's Two Nots). |
| Law of the Matching Rule: *Utinam scīrem ubi cāseus esset!* Why *esset*, when nothing in the sentence is past? | "If only I knew where the cheese was!" The main verb ***scīrem*** is an imperfect subjunctive, and an imperfect or pluperfect subjunctive plays on the **past team**. So the tucked-in question matches it: *esset*. |

## 7. *Cum vēnisset*: the two *cum*s

:: Quae cum ita sint, Catilīna, perge quō coepistī. || Since this is so, Catiline, carry on where you began. (Cicero, *Against Catiline* 1.10; Chapter 55)

Cum Hannibal in Ītaliam vēnisset…

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the Two *Cum*s: what does *cum* mean in each? *cum Avō · Cum Avus dormit, stertit · Cum Avia in forō esset, Avus cāseum spectābat · Cum fessus sīs, dormī! · Cum ēsurīret, tamen nōn ēdit.* | "**with** Grandpa" (a preposition) · "**when** Grandpa sleeps, he snores" (fact mood: a plain "when") · "**while** Grandma was in the forum…" (story *cum*) · "**since** you're tired, sleep!" · "**although** he was hungry, he still didn't eat" (*tamen* gives it away). First look right after *cum*, then at the mood. (Chapter 55.) |
| Law of the Story *Cum*: "When Hannibal had crossed the Alps, he came into Italy." "While Hannibal was crossing the Alps, the soldiers were cold." (*Frīgeō*: be cold, Chapter 58.) | ***Cum Hannibal Alpēs trānsiisset, in Ītaliam vēnit. · Cum Hannibal Alpēs trānsīret, mīlitēs frīgēbant.*** Pluperfect = **earlier**; imperfect = **same time**. Always the past team. |
| Nepos 12.4, near the end of Hannibal's life: *Hūc cum lēgātī Rōmānōrum vēnissent ac multitūdine domum eius circumdedissent…* (*Hūc*: to this place; *lēgātus*: envoy, Chapter 48; *multitūdō*: a crowd, here of soldiers; *circumdō*: surround.) Translate. Which *cum*? | "**When** the Roman envoys **had come** here and **had surrounded** his house with a crowd of men…" The story *cum*: pluperfect subjunctive, **earlier** than the main verb. One *cum* can carry two verbs. |
| The plain "when": *Cum vēneris, cēnābimus* · *Cum Avia vēnit, Avus surrēxit.* Why no subjunctive? | "When you come (will have come), we'll have dinner" · "**At the moment** Grandma came, Grandpa got up." *Cum* with the **fact mood** just tells or dates the time: future perfect for later, perfect in a story when it pins down the moment. |

## 8. *Imperat ut*: tucked-in orders

:: Imperāvit ut venīrent. || He ordered them to come. (teaching line; Chapter 56)

Avia mihi imperat ut veniam.

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the Person Asked and the Tucked-In Command: "Grandma orders me to come." "Grandma asks you to come." "Grandma begs Grandpa not to touch." (Use *petō ab*.) | ***Avia mihi imperat ut veniam. · Avia tē rogat ut veniās. · Avia ab Avō petit nē tangat.*** *Imperō* takes the person in the dative, *rogō* in the accusative, *petō* with *ā / ab*. Then ***ut*** ("to") or ***nē*** ("not to") + subjunctive. (Chapter 56.) |
| Law of *Iubeō* and *Vetō*: "Hamilcar ordered Hannibal to swear," two ways: with *iubeō*, and with *imperō*. | ***Hamilcar Hannibalem iūrāre iussit. · Hamilcar Hannibalī imperāvit ut iūrāret.*** *Iubeō* (and *vetō*) take an accusative and infinitive; the other ordering verbs take *ut / nē* + subjunctive. Past team: *iūrāret*. |
| Nepos 12.4: a boy looking out from the door tells Hannibal there are more armed men about than usual. *Quī imperāvit eī, ut omnēs forēs aedificiī circumīret ac properē sibi nūntiāret, num eōdem modō undique obsidērētur.* (*Quī*: and he; *forēs*: doors; *aedificium*: building; *circumeō*: go around; *properē*: quickly; *undique*: on all sides; *obsideō*: besiege, Chapter 48.) Translate. | "He **ordered him to go round** all the doors of the building and quickly **report** to him **whether** he was being besieged in the same way on all sides." *Imperāvit eī ut* + imperfect (past team). Inside the order, a tucked-in question with ***num*** (Chapter 53). *Sibi*: Hannibal, the one giving the order. |
| Law of "That" and "To": *Avia Avō persuāsit ut dormīret · Avia Avō persuāsit cāseum abesse.* Translate both. | "Grandma persuaded Grandpa **to sleep**" (an order: *ut* + subjunctive) · "Grandma persuaded Grandpa **that the cheese was gone**" (a fact: accusative and infinitive, Chapter 42). The construction tells you which. |

## 9. *Sī…*: the four ifs

:: Sī tacuissēs… || If you had kept quiet… (after Boethius; Chapter 57)

Sī rēx essem…

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the Four Ifs: name each kind. *Sī valēs, bene est · Sī vēneris, gaudēbō · Sī veniās, gaudeam · Sī adessēs, gaudērem · Sī adfuissēs, gāvīsus essem.* | **Fact if** (fact mood, says nothing about truth) · **will-if** (future perfect, then future) · **should-would** (present subjunctive both halves) · **not really, now** (imperfect) · **not really, then** (pluperfect). Look at the verbs. (Chapter 57.) |
| Law of *Nisi* and *Sīn*: "Unless you give me your word, I won't take you." "If Grandma comes, we'll eat; but if Grandpa comes, nothing will be left." (*Fidem dō*: give one's word.) | ***Nisi mihi fidem dederis, tē nōn dūcam. · Sī Avia vēnerit, edēmus; sīn Avus vēnerit, nihil manēbit.*** *Nisi* = "unless, if … not"; *sīn* = "but if," taking the other road. Will-ifs: future perfect in the if-half. |
| Law of Not Really: Nepos 1.2, on why Hannibal lost the war: *Quod nisi domī cīvium suōrum invidiā dēbilitātus esset, Rōmānōs vidētur superāre potuisse.* (*Quod nisi*: but if … not, Chapter 57's *quod sī* with *nisi*; *domī*: at home, Chapter 45; *invidia*: jealousy; *dēbilitō*: weaken; *superō*: beat.) Translate. Which kind of if? | "But **if he had not been weakened** at home by the jealousy of his own citizens, he seems to have been able to beat the Romans." **Not really, then**: pluperfect subjunctive *dēbilitātus esset* (he **was** weakened). Nepos softens the then-half: instead of "he could have beaten" (*superāre potuisset*), "he **seems to have been able** to beat" (*vidētur* + perfect infinitive, Chapter 41). |
| *Quid facerēs, sī Hannibal essēs?* Answer, then tuck the question in after *Rogō*. | ***Sī Hannibal essem, Alpēs trānsīrem*** (or your own). ***Rogō quid facerēs, sī Hannibal essēs.*** Law of the Frozen Clock: the not-really imperfect stays imperfect even after a now-team *rogō*. |

## 10. *Timeō nē*: fears, stops, and "no doubt"

:: Valdē metuō, nē frīgeās in hībernīs. || I'm very much afraid you may be freezing in winter quarters. (Cicero, *Letters to Friends* 7.10; Chapter 58)

Timeō nē veniat. Timeō ut veniat.

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the Backwards Fear: "I'm afraid the Romans will come." "I'm afraid the Romans won't come." | ***Timeō nē Rōmānī veniant. · Timeō ut Rōmānī veniant*** (or *nē nōn veniant*). Backwards: *nē* = "that it **will**," *ut* = "that it **won't**." The old wish pushed inside: *Nē veniant!* → *timeō nē veniant*. (Chapter 58.) |
| Law of the Fear Clock and the Calm Fear: "Hannibal was afraid the Romans had come." "I'm not afraid that he'll come." | ***Hannibal timēbat nē Rōmānī vēnissent. · Nōn vereor nē veniat.*** Past team, **earlier**: pluperfect. Saying "not afraid" doesn't change the clause: still *nē*. |
| Laws of Afraid To, the Stop-It Clause and *Quīn*: "I'm afraid to go." "The dog stops Grandpa from touching the cheese." "I can't help laughing." | ***Timeō īre. · Canis Avum impedit quōminus (nē) cāseum tangat. · Facere nōn possum quīn rīdeam.*** Afraid **to**: infinitive. Stopping **from**: *nē* or *quōminus* + subjunctive. After a stop with a **"no,"** *quīn* works too. |
| Law of No Doubt: Nepos 11.2. Before a sea battle, Hannibal sends a letter-carrier to King Eumenes, his enemy: *statim ad Eumenem dēductus est, quod nēmō dubitābat, quīn aliquid dē pāce esset scrīptum.* (*Statim*: at once; *dēdūcō*: bring; *quod*: because; *pāx, pācis*: peace.) Translate. Why *esset scrīptum*? | "He was brought at once to Eumenes, because **nobody doubted that** something had been written about peace." A "no" + doubt (*nēmō dubitābat*) → ***quīn*** + subjunctive. Past team, **earlier**: pluperfect passive. (The letter only made fun of the king.) |
| Law of Which Word: *Dubitō venīre · Dubitō num veniat · Nōn dubitō quīn veniat.* | "I **hesitate** to come" (infinitive) · "I **doubt whether** he's coming" (*num*) · "I **don't doubt that** he's coming" (*quīn*). |

## 11. *Dum, antequam, dummodo*: the little time words

:: Ōderint, dum metuant. || Let them hate, provided they fear. (a line from an old Roman tragedy, quoted by Cicero, *On Duties* 1.97; Chapter 59)

Exspectā dum canis dormiat.

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Law of the Four *Dum*s: *Dum Avia abest, Avus cāseum tetigit · Dum Avia aberat, Avus laetus erat · Exspectā dum canis dormiat · Spectā, dummodo nē tangās.* | "**While** Grandma was away…" (present, even in a past story: Law of the *Dum* Present) · "**as long as** Grandma was away…" (same stretch, same tense) · "wait **until** the dog sleeps" (subjunctive: it's expected) · "look, **provided** you don't touch" (subjunctive, "not" = *nē*). (Chapter 59.) |
| Law of Before: Nepos 11.1, the same sea battle: *priusquam signum pugnae darētur, Hannibal, ut palam faceret suīs, quō locō Eumenēs esset, tabellārium in scaphā cum cādūceō mittit.* (*Signum pugnae*: the signal for battle; *palam faciō*: make clear; *suīs*: to his own men; *tabellārius*: letter-carrier; *scapha*: small boat; *cādūceus*: a herald's staff, the sign of a messenger.) Find three subjunctives and name their jobs. | "**Before** the signal for battle **could be given**, Hannibal, **to make clear** to his men **where** Eumenes **was**, sends a letter-carrier in a small boat with a herald's staff." ***Priusquam … darētur***: before it could happen (the waiting subjunctive). ***Ut palam faceret***: a goal. ***Quō locō … esset***: a tucked-in question. *Mittit* is a historic present leading the past team. |
| Law of After: "After Hannibal came into Italy, he beat the Romans." "As soon as the dog slept, Grandpa got up." | ***Postquam Hannibal in Ītaliam vēnit, Rōmānōs vīcit. · Simul atque canis dormīvit, Avus surrēxit.*** *Postquam, ubi, simul atque*: the **fact mood**, usually the perfect. |
| Law of Although: "Although Grandpa is hungry, he doesn't eat," three ways. | ***Quamquam Avus ēsurit, nōn edit*** (a fact: indicative) · ***Quamvīs Avus ēsuriat, nōn edit*** ("however hungry he may be") · ***Licet Avus ēsuriat, nōn edit*** ("granted he's hungry"). *Quamvīs* and *licet*: subjunctive. |
| Law of the Kind-Of Clause: "Hannibal wasn't the sort to love the Romans." "There's nobody who doesn't know Hannibal." (*Nōvī*: I know, Chapter 46.) | ***Hannibal nōn is erat quī Rōmānōs amāret. · Nēmō est quīn Hannibalem nōverit.*** A *quī* clause in the subjunctive describes a **kind**. *Quīn* = *quī nōn* after a negative. *Nōverit*: the perfect subjunctive of a perfect that means "now." |

## 12. Lectiō: *Hannibal*

:: Vir omnium callidissimus. || The cleverest man of all. (Nepos, *Life of Hannibal* 9.2)

Hannibal, Hamilcaris fīlius, Carthāginiēnsis.

Diē duodētrīcēsimā: the day after Chapter 59. Grandma has to go out once more, and the big cheese (minus Grandpa's scrap) lies on the table wrapped in a cloth, with the dog on guard. Grandpa reads the kids a story while he waits. Scenes I and II are **adapted** from Cornelius Nepos, *Life of Hannibal* chapters 1–3, 5 and 9: the plot and a few phrases are Nepos's, but the sentences are rebuilt in Book V Latin (Nepos's own words come in §13). Nepos (about 110–24 BC) was a friend of Cicero and of Atticus, and Catullus dedicated his little book of poems to him; his short lives of famous men were the first real Latin that generations of schoolchildren read. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. As you read, give every subjunctive its job. New words: *Poenus*, a Carthaginian; *iūrō*, swear; *iūs iūrandum*, oath; *āra*, altar; *amīcitia*, friendship; *callidus*, clever; *avārus*, greedy; *aurum*, gold; *argentum*, silver; *plumbum*, lead (the metal); *cōnservō*, keep; *fallō*, trick; *elephantus*, elephant. Glossed: *duodētrīcēsimus*, twenty-eighth; *iaceō*, lie; *pannus*, cloth (Chapter 37); *ut putāmus*, as we think (*ut* + fact mood, "as," like Chapter 59's *ut ait*); *amīca*, friend; *corbis, corbis*, basket; *Cornēlius Nepōs*, Cornelius Nepos; *dēscrībō*, describe; *dux, ducis*, leader, general; *Carthāgō*, Carthage (Chapter 45); *bellum gerō*, wage war; *Hamilcar, Hamilcaris*, Hamilcar, Hannibal's father; *Hispānia*, Spain; *proficīscor*, set out; *hostia*, an animal for sacrifice; *immolō*, sacrifice; *rēs dīvīna*, a sacred rite; *castra*, camp (Chapter 45); *dubitō* + infinitive, hesitate (Chapter 58); *fidem dō*, give one's word; *cēterī*, the others; *removeō, remōtum*, send away; *catulus*, puppy (Chapter 14); *hahae*, ha ha (Chapter 54); *iuvenis*, young man; *Alpēs, Alpium*, the Alps; *aciēs*, battle line (*in aciē*, in battle); *mūniō*, build (a road); *vix*, barely; *invidia*, jealousy; *dēbilitō*, weaken; *multīs post annīs*, many years later (Chapter 45); *Crēta*, Crete; *trādō*, hand over; *Crētēnsēs*, the Cretans; *amphora*, a tall clay jar; *compleō*, fill; *summus*, top; *operiō*, cover; *simulō*, pretend (Chapter 59); *fidēs*, good faith; *statua*, statue; *aēneus*, bronze; *abiciō*, toss aside; *illūdō, illūsum*, make a fool of; *flāvus*, yellow; *auctor*, author.

### II. *Cōnsilium Hannibalis*

::: dialogue lectio-2
Avus | Hannibal iuvenis imperātor factus est. Cum exercitū magnō et elephantīs ad Alpēs vēnit. || Grandpa | Hannibal was made commander as a young man. He came to the Alps with a big army and elephants.
Avus | Alpēs tam altae erant ut vix ūnus homō eās trānsīre posset. || Grandpa | The Alps were so high that one man could barely cross them.

Avus | Dum in Ītaliā fuit, nēmō eum in aciē vīcit. || Grandpa | As long as he was in Italy, nobody beat him in battle.
Quīntus | Sī tam callidus erat, cūr Rōmam nōn cēpit? || Quintus | If he was so clever, why didn't he take Rome?

Avus | Magnam pecūniam sēcum portābat, et timēbat nē Crētēnsēs, quī avārī erant, eam caperent. || Grandpa | He was carrying a lot of money with him, and he was afraid that the Cretans, who were greedy, would take it.

Avus | Amphorās multās plumbō complēvit; summās aurō et argentō operuit. || Grandpa | He filled many jars with lead; he covered the tops with gold and silver.
Avus | Hās amphorās, Crētēnsibus spectantibus, in templō posuit, et simulāvit sē pecūniam suam eōrum fideī crēdere. || Grandpa | He put these jars in a temple while the Cretans watched, and pretended that he was trusting his money to their good faith.
Avus | Vēram autem pecūniam in statuīs aēneīs cēlāvit, quās ante domum suam abiēcit. || Grandpa | But he hid his real money in bronze statues, which he tossed aside in front of his house.
Avus | Crētēnsēs templum magnā cūrā custōdiēbant, nē Hannibal amphorās tolleret. || Grandpa | The Cretans guarded the temple with great care, so that Hannibal wouldn't carry off the jars.
Avus | Tum Hannibal, Crētēnsibus omnibus illūsīs, cum statuīs suīs abiit. || Grandpa | Then Hannibal, with all the Cretans made fools of, went away with his statues.
Marcus | Callidissimus erat! || Marcus | He was the cleverest!
Avus | Ita. Nepōs eum "virum omnium callidissimum" vocat. Nēmō est quem nōn fefellerit. || Grandpa | Yes. Nepos calls him "the cleverest man of all." There's nobody he didn't trick.

## 13. Nepos's own words

:: Hannibal, Hamilcaris fīlius, Carthāginiēnsis. || Hannibal, son of Hamilcar, a Carthaginian. (Nepos, *Life of Hannibal* 1.1, the first line)

Pater meus Hamilcar puerulō mē…

Nepos's *Hannibal* is short, thirteen little chapters, and it's built out of exactly the clauses you've learned this book. In chapter 2 Hannibal, now an exile at the court of King Antiochus of Syria (in the 190s BC), is suspected of being friendly with Rome. To prove he isn't, he tells the king about the oath. These are his words, as Nepos gives them.

## 15. Sound check

!!! success "Liber Quīntus: tuus est!"
    Book V is yours. You can say "let's" and "let him," wish and ask politely, give a goal and a result, tuck a question or an order inside a sentence, match every subjunctive to its main verb, set a scene with *cum*, say all four kinds of "if," fear and doubt in Latin's backwards way, and tell time with *dum, antequam, postquam*. And you've read Nepos. Roman grammar teachers named this mood *modus subiūnctīvus*, "the joined-under mood" (Chapter 49), from *sub* + *iungō*: yoked under another verb, like Chapter 48's *iugum*. Book VI, *Ad Auctōrēs*, "To the Authors," opens with Chapter 61 *Ars Legendī*, "The Art of Reading," and the verb's last missing piece: the gerund.

## Speak it aloud

:: Nārrandō discimus. || We learn by telling. (Chapter 26)

1. Chant the four subjunctives of *sum* and *amō*, "he" form: *sit, esset, fuerit, fuisset · amet, amāret, amāverit, amāvisset*.
2. Three "let's," two "let him," one "what am I to do?"; then three wishes (one still possible, one not really now, one too late).
3. Two goals (one with *nē quis*), one sending *quī*, two results (one with *ut nōn*).
4. Tuck *Ubi est cāseus?* into six sentences: *Nesciō / Nesciēbam* + same time, earlier, later.
5. One story *cum*, one "since," one "although"; then three orders (one with *iubeō*, one with *nē*).
6. The four ifs, with Hannibal: *Sī Hannibal venit… · Sī Hannibal vēnerit… · Sī Hannibal veniat… · Sī Hannibal venīret… · Sī Hannibal vēnisset…*
7. Fear that it will and won't happen; stop someone *quōminus*; "I don't doubt that…"; wait *dum*; "before," "after," "although."
8. Tell Hannibal's oath in your own Latin in six to eight lines (his father, the altar, *velletne*, *nē dubitāret*, *sī fidem dederis*, *numquam … fore*). Then read the *Lectiō* aloud, all three scenes, and hide the Latin and rebuild it.
