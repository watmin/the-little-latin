# Caput XXXIV: Celeriter!

*Liber III, Fābulae · Chapter 34*

*In which words learn to run. Chapter 1 gave you a little scale,* optimē, bene, satis bene, nōn male, male, *and the survival line* Lentius, quaesō. *Chapter 2 gave you* minimē, *Chapter 12 Augustus's* Fēstīnā lentē *and the proud* Latīnē legō, *and Chapter 33 ended with the Olympic motto* Citius, altius, fortius *and a promise: "That's Chapter 34's whole job." Today every one of those turns out to be the same kind of word, the kind that tells you* how. *You'll make them from any adjective (slowly, quickly, bravely, wisely), compare them (faster, fastest, as fast as possible), and meet the five old rebels again in running shoes. Along the way: Augustus's favorite way to say "in a flash," the thing Jesus told Judas to do quickly, and a cheese that rolls down a hill with the whole family behind it. Speaking goal: say how someone does something, and how fast, how well, and how often.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Celeriter! · Lentius, quaesō! · Quōmodo? · Quam celeriter currit! · Bene cantās. Melius cantās. Optimē cantās! · Quam celerrimē! · Valdē bene!*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** adverb (a "how-word"; old-school "it modifies a verb, an adjective, or another adverb")
    - **Forms:** *lentē, clārē, longē* (*-ē*) · *fortiter, celeriter, ācriter, fēlīciter* (*-iter*) · *sapienter, dīligenter* (*-nter*) · *facile, multum, citō* · *lentius, celerius* · *lentissimē, celerrimē, facillimē* · *bene/melius/optimē, male/peius/pessimē, multum/plūs/plūrimum, parum/minus/minimē, magnopere/magis/maximē*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the How-Word · the Law of the Running Steps · the Law of the Five Rebels Running
    - **Words:** *lentus, lentē, celeriter, fortiter, fēlīciter, sapienter, dīligenter, facile, citō, paulum, valdē, quōmodo?, quam* (how!)
    - **Reading:** *Celeriter!* (a cheese rolls down a hill, the whole family runs after it, and Grandpa doesn't run at all)
    - **Speaking:** say how, how fast, how well, and how often anyone does anything

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep two old friends close: Chapter 20's *fortis, ācer, celer, fēlīx, sapiēns* (today they learn to run) and Chapter 33's whole chapter (today's comparisons are built from it, and they're easier).

## 1. How-words

:: Optimē, bene, satis bene, nōn male, male. || Very well, well, well enough, not bad, badly. (Chapter 1's scale)

Iōannēs bene cantat. Pater male cantat.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 5's Iūlia: *Iōannēs bene cantat. Sed tū male cantās!* Read the blurb. What do *bene* and *male* tell you about the singing? | **How** it's done: John sings **well**, Dad sings **badly**. Not *who* sings, not *what* he sings: **how**. |
| A word that tells **how** is an **adverb**: think "a how-word." The name is Latin *ad verbum*, "next to the verb," because it usually sits by a verb and colors it. Find two more on Chapter 1's scale. | ***Optimē*** "very well" and ***satis bene*** "well enough." (And *nōn* "not": the commonest adverb of all.) |
| Old-school grammars say an adverb "modifies a verb, an adjective, or another adverb." Sort these: *bene cantat* · *nimis ācer* (Chapter 33: "too sharp") · *satis bene*. | ***Bene*** colors a **verb** (*cantat*). ***Nimis*** colors an **adjective** (*ācer*). ***Satis*** colors another **adverb** (*bene*). |
| You've used dozens already. What do these tell? *Heri, hodiē, crās, saepe, semper, subitō, diū, statim, tandem* (Chapters 5, 6, 12, 16, 26). *Hīc, ibi, ubi?* (Chapters 5, 7). | **When** and **how often** (*heri… tandem*), and **where** (*hīc, ibi, ubi?*). All adverbs. "How-word" is the nickname; adverbs answer how, when, where, and how much. |
| *Avus bene cantat. Avia bene cantat. Līberī bene cantant.* Does *bene* change for a man, a woman, or a crowd? | **Never.** An adverb has **no** case, gender, or number. Like Chapter 22's numbers 4 to 10, it's **indeclinable**: one form, always. That's what makes it easy. |

## 2. Slowly: *-ē*

:: Fēstīnā lentē. || Make haste slowly. (Augustus's favorite saying, in Greek, per Suetonius, *Augustus* 25)

Lentus, lentē. Clārus, clārē. Longus, longē.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| A new adjective: ***lentus, lenta, lentum***, "slow." Chapter 12's Augustus said *Fēstīnā lentē*, "hurry **slowly**." Read the blurb. How did *lentus* become "slowly"? | Off with *-us*, on with a **long *-ē***: *lent-ē*. "Slow" → "**slowly**." |
| *Avus lentus est* · *Avus lentē ambulat*. What's the difference? | ***Lentus*** describes Grandpa himself (an adjective, agreeing with *Avus*): "Grandpa **is slow**." ***Lentē*** describes his walking (an adverb): "Grandpa **walks slowly**." |
| Chapter 3's *longus* "long," Chapter 33's *clārus* "clear" and *pulcher, pulchra* "beautiful." Make "clearly," "far (off)," "beautifully." | ***Clārē · longē · pulchrē.*** Like the comparative, the *-ē* goes on the **stem**: *pulchr-ē*, from *pulchra, pulchrī*. (*Longē* also means "**by far**.") |
| Say "Speak clearly!" and "Grandma cooks beautifully." (*Coquō, coquere*: cook.) | ***Dīc clārē!*** · ***Avia pulchrē coquit.*** |
| Chapter 12's proud line: *Latīnē legō et scrībō.* Which adjective is hiding in *Latīnē*? | ***Latīnus***, "Latin": *Latīnē* is "**in the Latin way**," "in Latin." The same pattern gives ***Graecē*** "in Greek" and, in modern Latin, ***Anglicē*** "in English." |

## 3. *Bene* and *male*

:: Bene quī latuit, bene vīxit. || Whoever has hidden well has lived well. (Ovid, *Tristia* 3.4.25)

Bonus, bene. Malus, male.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 3's *bonus* "good" and *malus* "bad." Read the blurb. Expected: *bonē, malē*. What do Romans actually say? | ***Bene*** "well" and ***male*** "badly." *Bene* even has a different vowel from *bonus* (both grew from an older *duenos*); it's the *bene-* in English *benefit* and *benevolent*. |
| Look at the last letter. *Lentē* has a long *ē*. What about *bene* and *male*? | **Short**: BE-ne, MA-le. These two everyday words got worn smooth by use; every other *-ē* adverb keeps its *ē* long. |
| Chapter 1's motto, the way Roman letters began: *Sī valēs, bene est.* And Chapter 12's motto *Ubi bene, ibi patria.* Adjective or adverb? | **Adverb** both times: "If you're well, it is **well**" and "Where (it goes) **well**, there (is my) homeland." *Bene* never agrees with anything. |
| Ovid, exiled at the edge of the world, writes to a friend: *Bene quī latuit, bene vīxit.* (*Lateō, latēre, latuī*: lie hidden. *Quī* with no antecedent: Chapter 28's "whoever.") | "**Whoever has hidden well has lived well.**" Two *bene*s and two perfects: the safe life is the quiet one. |

## 4. Bravely, quickly: *-iter*

:: Inopī beneficium bis dat quī dat celeriter. || He gives a kindness twice to the needy who gives it quickly. (Publilius Syrus, *Sentences* I6; later shortened to *Bis dat quī citō dat*)

Fortis, fortiter. Celer, celeriter!

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 20's third family: *fortis* "brave, strong," *celer* "fast." Read the blurb. How do they make adverbs? | They add ***-iter***: *fort-iter* "**bravely, strongly, hard**," *celer-iter* "**quickly, fast**." *Celeriter!*, "Quick!", is this chapter's title. |
| Chapter 13's Law of the Stem: the genitive shows the stem. *Ācer, ācris* "sharp." *Fēlīx, fēlīcis* "lucky, happy." *Gravis* "heavy, serious." *Brevis* "short." Make the adverbs. | ***Ācriter*** "sharply, fiercely" · ***fēlīciter*** "luckily, happily" · ***graviter*** "heavily, seriously" · ***breviter*** "briefly." Always the genitive stem + *-iter*. |
| Why *celeriter* with an *e*, but *ācriter* without? | The **stem** decides: *celer, celeris* keeps its *e* (like *puer*); *ācer, ācris* drops it (like *niger*). Chapter 20 told you so; the adverb just follows the stem. |
| At a rich man's dinner party in a Roman novel (Petronius, *Satyricon* 60), the guests jump to their feet and shout: *Augustō, patrī patriae, fēlīciter!* (*Patria*: fatherland, Chapter 12.) | "**To Augustus, father of the fatherland: good luck!**" ***Fēlīciter!*** was the Roman "Cheers! Hooray! Good luck!", shouted at feasts and scratched on walls at Pompeii. |
| The motto, from Publilius Syrus's book of one-liners: *Inopī beneficium bis dat quī dat celeriter.* (*Inops, inopis*: needy, poor. *Beneficium*: kindness, favor, "a good deed," from *bene* + *faciō*. *Bis*: twice.) | "**He gives a kindness twice to the needy who gives it quickly.**" Later Latin boiled it down to the famous ***Bis dat quī citō dat***: "He gives twice who gives quickly" (*citō*: quickly, §6). |

## 5. Wisely, easily: *-nter* and *facile*

:: Facile omnēs, quom valēmus, rēcta cōnsilia aegrōtīs damus. || When we're well, we all easily give good advice to the sick. (Terence, *Andria* 309)

Sapiēns, sapienter. Facilis, facile.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 20's *sapiēns, sapientis* "wise." By the rule, the adverb would be *sapientiter*. Read the blurb. What do Romans say? | ***Sapienter***, "**wisely**." Adjectives whose stem ends in ***-nt-*** add only ***-er***: *sapient-er*. Short and sweet. |
| You met one in Chapter 17 without knowing it: Mark 6:20, *libenter eum audiēbat*, and Caesar's *Quae volumus, et crēdimus libenter* (Chapter 18). Its adjective is *libēns, libentis*, "willing." What's *libenter*? | "**Gladly, willingly**": *libent-er*, the same *-nter* pattern. Herod listened to John **gladly**; we believe what we want **gladly**. |
| New: *dīligēns, dīligentis*, "careful, hard-working" (English *diligent*). "Read carefully!" (to one, then to y'all) | ***Dīligenter lege! · Dīligenter legite!*** (Command forms, Chapter 6.) |
| Chapter 20's *facilis* "easy." Read the blurb again. The adverb is just ***facile***. Where have you seen that form? | It's the **neuter** of *facilis* (*facilis, facile*), simply used as a how-word: "**easily**." *Hoc facile est* "this is easy"; *hoc facile faciō* "I do this easily." |
| The motto, from the comedian Terence: *Facile omnēs, quom valēmus, rēcta cōnsilia aegrōtīs damus.* (*Quom*: old spelling of *cum*, "when." *Rēctus*: right, straight. *Cōnsilium*: advice, Chapter 33. *Aegrōtus*: sick.) | "**When we're well, we all easily give good advice to the sick.**" *Facile* colors *damus*: we give it **easily**. Advice is cheap when you're not the one in bed. |

!!! success "The Law of the How-Word"
    To turn an adjective into an adverb, a how-word:

    - **first and second family** (*-us, -a, -um*): stem + long ***-ē*** (*lentē, clārē, pulchrē, Latīnē*), but ***bene, male*** with a short *e*
    - **third family**: genitive stem + ***-iter*** (*fortiter, celeriter, ācriter, fēlīciter*)
    - stem in ***-nt-***: just ***-er*** (*sapienter, dīligenter, libenter*)
    - and some just borrow the **neuter**: ***facile***

    An adverb never changes: no case, no gender, no number.

## 6. Other how-words: *-ō* and *-um*

:: Sat citō, sī sat bene. || Soon enough, if well enough. (Cato the Elder, as quoted by Saint Jerome, *Letters* 66.9)

Subitō. Sērō. Citō! Multum. Paulum.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 26's *subitō* "suddenly" and Chapter 27's *sērō* "too late." Read the blurb. What ending do they share? | A long ***-ō***. Some adverbs are old **ablatives**: *subitō*, *sērō*, and ***citō*** "**quickly, soon**" (from *citus*, "quick," Chapter 33's *citius*). |
| Chapter 12's motto *Nōn multa, sed multum*; Chapter 26's Grandpa *multum edēbam*; Chapter 27's *prīmum*. What ending do these share? | ***-um***, the **neuter** again: ***multum*** "**much, a lot**," ***prīmum*** "**first**." New: ***paulum***, "**a little**." Like *facile*, they're neuters put to work as how-words. |
| "Grandpa eats a lot; Quīntus eats a little." "Come quickly!" | ***Avus multum edit; Quīntus paulum edit. · Venī citō!*** |
| A new everyday word: ***valdē***, "**very, very much**" (from *validē*, "strongly"). "Very good!" "Very well!" "I love cheese very much." | ***Valdē bonum! · Valdē bene! · Cāseum valdē amō!*** An adverb on an adjective, on another adverb, and on a verb: all three jobs from §1. |
| The motto. Cato the Elder's old saying, quoted by Saint Jerome: *Sat citō, sī sat bene.* (*Sat* is short for *satis*, Chapter 1.) | "**Soon enough, if well enough.**" Chapter 1's *satis bene* comes home: do it well, and the speed will look after itself. Augustus would have agreed. |

## 7. Asking how: *quōmodo?* and *quam!*

:: Ecce quam bonum et quam iūcundum habitāre frātrēs in ūnum! || Look how good and how pleasant it is for brothers to live together as one! (Psalm 133:1)

Quōmodo currit Marcus? Quam celeriter currit!

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| A new question word: ***quōmodo?***, "**how? in what way?**" It's two words glued: *quō modō*, "in what way" (*modus*: way, manner). *Quōmodo cantat Pater?* Answer for Dad. | ***Male cantat!*** (Or ***Pessimē cantat!*** in §10.) *Quōmodo?* asks for an adverb, and an adverb answers it. |
| Read the blurb. *Quam celeriter currit!* Here *quam* is neither "whom" nor "than." What is it? | "**How** fast he runs!" ***Quam*** before an adjective or adverb means "**how**": *quam celeriter* "how fast," *quam bene* "how well." With a question mark it asks: *Quam celeriter currit?* "How fast does he run?" |
| Chapter 19's Cicero, attacking the traitor Catiline: *Quam diū etiam furor iste tuus nōs ēlūdet?* Now you can see why it starts *Quam diū*. | "**How long** will that madness of yours still mock us?" *Quam* "how" + *diū* "long." His speech opens with another how-word pair: *Quō ūsque tandem…?*, "**How far, at last…?**" (*Ūsque*: all the way.) |
| The motto, from a psalm that's still sung today: *Ecce quam bonum et quam iūcundum habitāre frātrēs in ūnum!* (*Iūcundus*: pleasant. *Habitō*: live, dwell. *In ūnum*: together, "into one.") | "**Look how good and how pleasant it is for brothers to live together as one!**" Two exclamations with *quam* "how." (The neuters *bonum, iūcundum* go with the infinitive *habitāre*, as in Chapter 20's *Dulce et decōrum est*; *frātrēs* says who does the living, a pattern Chapter 42 explains.) |
| Now there are four *quam*s. Sort: *puella quam videō* · *altior quam Marcus* · *mālō… quam* · *Quam pulchra est!* | Relative "**whom**" (Ch28) · "**than**" (Ch33) · "**rather than**" (Ch32) · "**how**" (today). The word after it gives it away: an adjective or adverb right after *quam* (with nothing being compared) means "how." |

## 8. Faster: the comparative how-word

:: Citius, altius, fortius. || Faster, higher, stronger. (Olympic motto, 1894; since 2021 with *commūniter*, "together")

Lentē, lentius. Celeriter, celerius.

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 1's survival line: *Lentius, quaesō!*, "**More slowly**, please!" Chapter 33 taught you a word that ends exactly like *lentius*. Which? | The **neuter comparative**: *altius, fortius, melius*. ***Lentius*** is the neuter of *lentior*, "slower," put to work as a how-word: "more slowly." |
| So the rule for "more …ly": take the neuter comparative. "Faster" (*celer*). "More bravely" (*fortis*). "More clearly" (*clārus*). "More easily" (*facilis*). | ***Celerius · fortius · clārius · facilius.*** The same form as the neuter adjective (*corpus fortius*): the company tells you which. |
| Chapter 33 ended with the Olympic motto *Citius, altius, fortius.* Now read it properly. | "**Faster, higher, stronger**": three comparative how-words (*citius* from *citō*). Run faster, jump higher, fight harder. Since 2021 the motto adds ***commūniter***, "**together**," an *-iter* adverb from *commūnis*, "shared." |
| Comparative how-words take *quam* like comparative adjectives (Chapter 33). "Marcus runs faster than Quīntus." | ***Marcus celerius currit quam Quīntus.*** *Quam* + the same case on both sides: both are subjects. |
| Augustus, when something happened in a flash, used to say it was done *celerius quam asparagī cocuntur* (Suetonius, *Augustus* 87; *asparagus*: asparagus; *cocuntur* = *coquuntur*, "are cooked"). | "**Quicker than asparagus gets cooked.**" *Celerius* + *quam*: a comparative how-word. The emperor's "faster than you can boil an egg." |
| Chapter 33: a comparative with no "than" means "rather, too." In John's Gospel, at the Last Supper, Jesus says to Judas: *Quod facis, fac citius.* (John 13:27) | "**What you are doing, do quickly.**" *Citius* with no *quam*: "more quickly (than you're doing)," "quickly, without delay." Chapter 6's command *fac*, Chapter 28's *quod* "what." |

## 9. Fastest: the superlative how-word

:: Hic altē, dēmissius ille volābat. || This one flew high, that one lower. (Ovid, *Tristia* 3.4.24, on Icarus and his father Daedalus)

Celeriter, celerius, celerrimē!

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 33's superlative *celerrimus, -a, -um* is a plain *bonus* adjective. Read the blurb. How does it become "most quickly, very quickly"? | Like any *-us* adjective in §2: ***-ē***. *Celerrimus* → ***celerrimē***. The superlative how-word is just §2's rule again. |
| "Very bravely." "Most easily." "Very slowly." "Very wisely." | ***Fortissimē · facillimē · lentissimē · sapientissimē.*** Chapter 33's three superlative types (*-issimus, -errimus, -illimus*), each with *-ē*. |
| *Quam* + a superlative means "**as … as possible**." *Quam celerrimē!* | "**As fast as possible!**" Caesar's war reports are full of it: the army marches *quam celerrimē*. And ***quam prīmum*** is "**as soon as possible**." |
| "Come home as fast as possible!" (*Domum*, Chapter 8.) | ***Domum venī quam celerrimē!*** |
| The motto. Ovid on the famous flight: Icarus flew too near the sun, his father Daedalus stayed low. *Hic altē, dēmissius ille volābat.* (*Volō, volāre*: fly, Chapter 18. *Dēmissus*: low, "let down.") Find a positive and a comparative how-word. | ***Altē***, "**high**" (from *altus*, -ē) and ***dēmissius***, "**lower**" (comparative). "This one flew high, that one lower." Guess which one fell. (You'll meet the whole story soon.) |

!!! success "The Law of the Running Steps"
    How-words have three steps too, all borrowed from Chapter 33:

    | plain | more | most |
    |---|---|---|
    | *lentē* slowly | *lentius* more slowly | *lentissimē* very / most slowly |
    | *fortiter* bravely | *fortius* more bravely | *fortissimē* very / most bravely |
    | *celeriter* quickly | *celerius* more quickly | *celerrimē* very / most quickly |
    | *facile* easily | *facilius* more easily | *facillimē* very / most easily |

    - **more**: the **neuter comparative** (*-ius*), with *quam* for "than"
    - **most**: the **superlative** with ***-ē*** (*-issimē, -errimē, -illimē*); *quam* + it = "as … as possible"

## 10. The rebels run

:: Beātius est magis dare quam accipere. || It is more blessed to give than to receive. (Acts 20:35, Saint Paul quoting Jesus)

Bene, melius, optimē!

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 33's rebel: *bonus, melior, optimus*. Read the blurb. You know *bene* and Chapter 1's *optimē*. What's in the middle? | ***Melius***, "**better**": the neuter comparative, as always. *Bene, melius, optimē*: well, better, best (very well). Chapter 1's *optimē* was a superlative all along. |
| *Malus, peior, pessimus.* "Badly, worse, worst." Then: "Dad sings worse than the dog. Dad sings terribly." | ***Male, peius, pessimē.*** · ***Pater peius cantat quam canis. Pater pessimē cantat.*** |
| *Multus, plūs, plūrimus.* "Much, more, most" as how-words? And "Grandpa eats more than Quīntus." | ***Multum, plūs, plūrimum.*** · ***Avus plūs edit quam Quīntus.*** |
| A new plain one: ***parum***, "**too little, not enough**." Its steps are *parum, minus, minimē*. Now crack Chapter 2's answer word ***minimē***. | "**Least**," so "**not in the least, not at all, no!**" Chapter 2's "no" was a superlative how-word. (*Minus* "less" is the English *minus*.) |
| Chapter 18's *magis* "more" (in *mālō* = *magis volō*) belongs to a set: ***magnopere, magis, maximē***, "greatly, more, most / especially." Chapter 33 said *magis caeruleus*. "Most blue"? | ***Maximē caeruleus.*** (*Magnopere* is *magnō opere*, "with great work"; Chapter 35 explains that ablative.) |
| Two time-words have steps too: *diū, diūtius, diūtissimē* (long, longer, longest) and *saepe, saepius, saepissimē* (often, more often, most often). "Grandpa sleeps longer than Grandma." "Read more often!" | ***Avus diūtius dormit quam Avia.*** · ***Lege saepius!*** |
| The motto, Saint Paul quoting Jesus in Acts: *Beātius est magis dare quam accipere.* (*Beātus*: blessed, happy, Chapter 28's Psalm 1. *Accipiō*: receive.) | "**It is more blessed to give than to receive.**" *Beātius*: neuter comparative adjective with the infinitive *dare* (like *Dulce et decōrum est*). *Magis… quam*: "**rather** … than," like Chapter 32's *mālō… quam*. |

!!! success "The Law of the Five Rebels Running"
    The five rebels of Chapter 33 run too. The comparative is still the neuter; the superlative still ends in *-ē* (or *-um*).

    | plain | more | most |
    |---|---|---|
    | *bene* well | *melius* better | *optimē* best, very well |
    | *male* badly | *peius* worse | *pessimē* worst, very badly |
    | *multum* much | *plūs* more | *plūrimum* most |
    | *parum* too little | *minus* less | *minimē* least, not at all |
    | *magnopere* greatly | *magis* more | *maximē* most, especially |

    Also *diū, diūtius, diūtissimē* and *saepe, saepius, saepissimē*.

## 11. Adjective or adverb?

:: Bonus cantor bene cantat. || A good singer sings well. (teaching line)

Lentus an lentē? Facile an facile?

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The big test, every time: does the word **describe a noun** (and agree with it) or **tell how** something is done? *Cāseus bonus est* · *Avus bene edit*. | ***Bonus***: adjective, agreeing with *cāseus*: "the cheese is **good**." ***Bene***: adverb, telling how Grandpa eats: "eats **well**." English does the same: good / well. |
| "Cornēlia is fast." "Cornēlia runs fast." | ***Cornēlia celeris est.*** (adjective, feminine to match her) · ***Cornēlia celeriter currit.*** (adverb, no agreement at all). |
| Read the blurb. *Hoc facile est* · *Hoc facile faciō*. Same spelling: which is which? | ***Facile est***: adjective, neuter with *hoc*: "This is **easy**." ***Facile faciō***: adverb: "I do this **easily**." The company decides. |
| *Mālum melius est* · *Melius cantās*. | "The apple is **better**" (neuter adjective with *mālum*) · "You sing **better**" (adverb with *cantās*). |
| *Plūs cāseī edit* · *Plūs dormit*. | Both "more": "He eats **more cheese**" (Chapter 33's noun *plūs* + genitive) · "He sleeps **more**" (how much he sleeps: a how-word). |
| The vocative *Marce* (Chapter 9) and the ablative *rēge* (Chapter 13) end in a short *-e*. *Lentē* and *clārē* end in a long *-ē*. Say all four. | MAR-ke · RAY-ge · LEN-tay · KLAH-ray. The long *ē* is the how-word's badge; only *bene* and *male* (and the neuter *facile*) get away with a short *e*. |

## 12. Fix it

:: Nē quid nimis. || Nothing too much. (Terence, *Andria* 61, after the Greek "nothing in excess")

Bonē? Fortiē? Celerissimē?

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "You sing well": *Bonē cantās.* | ***Bene cantās.*** *Bonus* is a rebel: *bene* (short *e*). |
| "Marcus fights bravely": *Marcus fortiē pugnat.* (*Pugnō*: fight.) | ***Marcus fortiter pugnat.*** Third family: *-iter*. |
| "Grandma speaks wisely": *Avia sapientiter dīcit.* | ***Avia sapienter dīcit.*** Stems in *-nt-* add just *-er*. |
| "The dog runs very fast": *Canis celerissimē currit.* | ***Canis celerrimē currit.*** *Celer* → *celerrimus* → *celerrimē*. |
| "John sings better": *Iōannēs magis bene cantat.* | ***Iōannēs melius cantat.*** The rebel has its own word. |
| "Cornēlia runs fast": *Cornēlia celeris currit.* Not wrong Latin exactly ("Cornēlia, being fast, runs"), but what did you mean? | ***Cornēlia celeriter currit.*** To say **how** she runs, use the how-word. |

## 13. Lectiō: *Celeriter!*

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Grandma: *Lentē ambulās.* Grandpa: *Celeriter currō!* Which adjectives are behind the two adverbs? | ***Lentus*** "slow" (*-ē*, first and second family) and ***celer*** "fast" (*-iter*, third family). |
| Cornēlia: *Ego celerius curram quam Marcus.* Marcus: *Ego celerrimē omnium currō.* Name the steps. | ***Celerius***: comparative how-word (with *quam*). ***Celerrimē***: superlative how-word, with Chapter 22's *omnium*, "of all." (*Curram*: Chapter 17's future.) |
| Cornēlia asks *Marce, quōmodo valēs?* (Chapter 1's *valeō*, "be well") and Marcus answers *Nōn male*. What's the question, and why is the answer an adverb? | "**How are you doing?**" (*Quōmodo?* "how?") Marcus answers with how-words from Chapter 1's scale: "**not bad**." |
| *Canis sapienter facit.* Why not *sapientiter*? | ***Sapiēns, sapientis***: the stem ends in *-nt-*, so just *-er*. "The dog acts **wisely**" (he knows Grandpa's sharp cheese from Chapter 33). |
| Who won the race, and how? | **Grandpa**, without running: *Lentē ambulāvī, sed vīcī!* The dog caught the cheese but wouldn't eat it, so he brought it to Grandpa. *Fēstīnā lentē* wins, and then Grandpa eats it *celerrimē*, "quicker than asparagus gets cooked." |

## Vocābula: Words of this chapter

:: Verba celeriter, verba bene! || Words quickly, words well! (teaching line)

New forms:

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| *-ē* | the how-word ending for *-us, -a, -um* adjectives: *lentē, clārē, longē* |
| *-iter* | the how-word ending for third-family adjectives: *fortiter, celeriter, ācriter* |
| *-nter* | the how-word ending for *-ns* adjectives: *sapienter, dīligenter, libenter* |
| *-ius* (how-word) | "more …ly": the neuter comparative: *lentius, celerius* |
| *-issimē* | "most / very …ly" (also *-errimē, -illimē*): *lentissimē, celerrimē, facillimē* |
| *bene, melius, optimē* | well, better, best / very well |
| *male, peius, pessimē* | badly, worse, worst / very badly |
| *multum, plūs, plūrimum* | much, more, most |
| *parum, minus, minimē* | too little, less, least / not at all |
| *magnopere, magis, maximē* | greatly, more, most / especially |
| *diū, diūtius, diūtissimē* | for a long time, longer, longest |
| *saepe, saepius, saepissimē* | often, more often, most often |

New words:

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| *lentus, lenta, lentum* | slow |
| *lentē* | slowly |
| *celeriter* | quickly, fast |
| *fortiter* | bravely, strongly, hard |
| *fēlīciter* | luckily, happily; *Fēlīciter!* Cheers! Good luck! |
| *sapienter* | wisely |
| *dīligēns, dīligentis* | careful, hard-working |
| *dīligenter* | carefully |
| *facile* | easily |
| *citō* | quickly, soon |
| *paulum* | a little |
| *valdē* | very, very much |
| *quōmodo?* | how? in what way? |
| *quam* (+ adjective or adverb) | how! (*Quam celeriter!*); with a superlative, "as … as possible" (*quam celerrimē*) |
| *adverb* | a "how-word": tells how, when, where, how much; "modifies a verb, an adjective, or another adverb" |

Glossed in passing (no card): *coquō* cook, *pulchrē* beautifully, *Graecē* in Greek, *Anglicē* in English (modern Latin), *lateō* lie hidden, *ācriter* sharply, *graviter* heavily, *breviter* briefly, *patria* fatherland, *inops* needy, *beneficium* kindness, *bis* twice, *libēns* willing, *libenter* gladly, *quom* (= *cum*) when, *rēctus* right, *aegrōtus* sick, *sat* enough, *prīmum* first, *modus* way, *ūsque* all the way, *iūcundus* pleasant, *habitō* live, *in ūnum* together, *commūniter* together, *asparagus* asparagus, *dēmissus* low, *quam prīmum* as soon as possible, *accipiō* receive, *beātus* blessed, *pugnō* fight, *lūdus* game, *dē* down from, *volvō* roll, *cadō* fall, *petō* chase.

## Speak it aloud

:: Lentē, deinde celeriter! || Slowly, then quickly! (teaching line)

1. Turn adjectives into how-words: *lentus, clārus, longus, pulcher · fortis, celer, ācer, fēlīx, gravis · sapiēns, dīligēns · facilis · bonus, malus*.
2. Chant the running steps: *lentē, lentius, lentissimē · fortiter, fortius, fortissimē · celeriter, celerius, celerrimē · facile, facilius, facillimē*.
3. Chant the five rebels running: *bene, melius, optimē · male, peius, pessimē · multum, plūs, plūrimum · parum, minus, minimē · magnopere, magis, maximē*.
4. Say the three Laws in your own words: the How-Word, the Running Steps, the Five Rebels Running.
5. Describe your family: how each one walks, sings, eats, and reads (*Avus lentē ambulat. Pater pessimē cantat…*), then compare two of them with *quam*.
6. Ask and answer: *Quōmodo cantās? · Quam celeriter curris? · Venī quam celerrimē!*
7. The real lines: *Fēstīnā lentē · Bene quī latuit, bene vīxit · Inopī beneficium bis dat quī dat celeriter · Sat citō, sī sat bene · Citius, altius, fortius · Quod facis, fac citius · Beātius est magis dare quam accipere.*
8. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part (the dog too). Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
