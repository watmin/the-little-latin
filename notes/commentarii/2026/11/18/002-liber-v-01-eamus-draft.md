# Caput XLIX: Eāmus!

*Liber V, Modus Subiūnctīvus · Chapter 49*

*In which the verb learns to say "maybe." For forty-eight chapters, almost every verb you met stated a fact (*Avus dormit*) or gave an order (*Dormī!*). But a few strange forms kept slipping in: Genesis's* Fīat lūx, *the lawyers'* Dō ut dēs, *Horace's* quid sit futūrum, *the angel's* Nē timeās. *Chapter 32 promised that* Fīat *was "the 'let it be' form, Chapter 49." Here it is. Book V is the book of the* modus subiūnctīvus, *the mood of wishing, urging, and maybe. Today: what a mood is, how to build the new one in every verb family (one swapped vowel does most of the work), and its three simplest jobs: "let's…," "let him…," and "what am I to do?" Along the way: Catullus and his Lesbia, shepherds who decide to go and see, a Roman who can't lie, a lover who can't decide, Cicero's toga, and the Lord's Prayer. Speaking goal: make plans with "let's," give a wish-order about someone else, and ask yourself what to do.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Eāmus! · Gaudeāmus! · Nē cantēmus! · Fīat lūx · Marcus cāseum ferat · Quid faciam? · Quō eam?*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** mood (the verb's attitude) · indicative (the "fact" mood) · imperative (the "command" mood) · subjunctive (the "maybe / let's / should" mood) · hortatory subjunctive (the "let's" subjunctive) · jussive subjunctive (the "let him" subjunctive) · deliberative subjunctive (the "what am I to do?" subjunctive)
    - **Forms:** *amem, amēs, amet…* · *habeam, dūcam, capiam, audiam…* · *sim, possim, velim, nōlim, mālim* · *eam, feram, fīam* · *dem, dēs* · passive and deponent *amētur, loquāmur*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Three Moods · the Law of the Swapped Vowel · the Law of Let's, Let Him, and What Now?
    - **Words:** *igitur, hinc, surgō, ōrō, laetor, cēdō* (+ dative), *arma, emptor, caveō, requiēscō, adveniō, voluntās, convīvium, quārē, revocō*
    - **Reading:** *Eāmus!* (the first morning of Book Five: no cheese in the house, everybody has a plan, and Grandpa is left at home)
    - **Speaking:** propose five plans with "let's"; say what someone else should do; ask "what am I to do?"

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep three old friends close: Chapter 6's commands (*amā, dūc, audī*), Chapter 17's future (*legam, legēs; audiam, audiēs*), and Chapter 32's rebels (*eō, ferō, fīō, volō*).

---

## 1. Fact, command… and maybe

:: Surgite, eāmus hinc. || Get up, let's go from here. (John 14:31)

Īmus. Īte! Eāmus!

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Read the blurb's three words. *Īmus* you know (Chapter 8's chant). Meaning? | "**We go**," "we are going." A plain statement. |
| *Īte!* (Chapter 32's *eō*.) | "**Go!**", to y'all. A command. |
| And *Eāmus!*, the chapter's title? | "**Let's go!**" Not a fact (nobody is going yet), not quite an order to someone else: an **urging**, and the speaker comes along. |
| Grammar books call this difference **mood**. Plain words? | A verb's **attitude**: is it telling a fact, giving an order, or reaching for something that isn't real yet? English has it too: "you are quiet" vs "be quiet!" vs "if only you were quiet." |
| The "fact" mood has an old-school name you saw once in Chapter 25 ("perfect active indicative"). What is it, and what does it cover? | The **indicative** ("pointing out"): every *amō, amābam, amāvī, amātur* you've learned. Plain words: **the "fact" mood**. Questions about facts too: *Quid facis?* |
| And the "command" mood, Chapter 6's *amā, amāte* and Chapter 47's *amātō*? | The **imperative** ("ordering"). Plain words: **the "command" mood**. |
| *Eāmus* is a third mood. Old-school name: **subjunctive** (*modus subiūnctīvus*, "the joined-under mood," because it so often sits in a clause hooked under another verb; some books say "conjunctive"). Plain words? | **The "maybe / let's / should" mood**: for things wished, urged, or wondered about, not stated as fact. This whole book, Liber V, is about it. |

!!! success "The Law of the Three Moods"
    | mood | plain words | example |
    |---|---|---|
    | **indicative** | the "fact" mood | *Īmus.* We go. |
    | **imperative** | the "command" mood | *Īte!* Go! |
    | **subjunctive** | the "maybe / let's / should" mood | *Eāmus!* Let's go! |

    Tense says **when**; voice says **who does it**; mood says **how the speaker feels about it**: fact, order, or not-yet.

## 2. Strangers you've already met

:: Fīat lūx. || Let there be light. (Genesis 1:3; Chapters 31–32)

Fīat! Dēs. Sit. Timeās.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 31's *Fīat lūx*; Chapter 32 said *fīat* was "the 'let it be' form, Chapter 49." What does Chapter 32's indicative *fit* mean, and how is *fīat* different? | *Fit*: "it **becomes**, it is made" (a fact). ***Fīat***: "**let it be made**, let it happen." Same verb, *fīō*, in the subjunctive. |
| Chapter 11's motto *Dō ut dēs*, "I give so that you may give." *Dō* is the fact. What's *dēs*? | The subjunctive of *dō*, "**you may give**." (Why the *ut* wants it: Chapter 51.) |
| Chapter 4's first motto: *Ēsse oportet ut vīvās, nōn vīvere ut edās.* Which two words are in the new mood? | ***Vīvās*** and ***edās***: "(so that) **you may live**," "(so that) **you may eat**." Compare the facts *vīvis, edis*. (*Ut* again: Chapter 51.) |
| Chapter 17's Horace: *Quid sit futūrum crās, fuge quaerere.* Which word was "still waiting"? | ***Sit***, the subjunctive of *sum*. Compare the fact *est*. (Why a question hidden inside a sentence wants it: Chapter 53.) |
| Chapter 47's fifth "don't": *Nē timeās*, Luke 1:30. Fact form: *timēs*, "you fear." What changed? | One vowel: *tim**ē**s* → *tim**eā**s*. That swap is the whole trick of today's chapter. |

## 3. The first family: *a* becomes *e*

:: Vīvāmus, mea Lesbia, atque amēmus. || Let's live, my Lesbia, and let's love. (Catullus 5.1)

Amāmus. Amēmus!

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Amāmus* vs *amēmus*. One is "we love," the other "let's love." Which is which? | *Amāmus* (indicative): "**we love**." ***Amēmus*** (subjunctive): "**let's love**." The family's *ā* has become *ē*. |
| So the first family's present subjunctive: swap the *a* for *e*. Chant *amō* all six (I, you, he, we, y'all, they). | ***Amem, amēs, amet, amēmus, amētis, ament.*** The "I" form is *-em* (not *-ō*). |
| Macrons: why short *e* in *amem, amet, ament* but long in *amēs, amēmus, amētis*? | The same rule as *amās / amat / amant*: a long vowel goes **short before final *-m*, final *-t*, and *-nt***. |
| "Let's sing!" "Let's walk!" "Let's work!" (*cantō, ambulō, labōrō*) | ***Cantēmus! Ambulēmus! Labōrēmus!*** |
| Chapter 11's *dō, dare*: a first-family verb with a short *a*. Build "I may give, you may give." | ***Dem, dēs***, then *det, dēmus, dētis, dent*. Same swap. So *Dō ut dēs* was just the first family. |
| Catullus 5.1, the motto. (*Vīvāmus* comes from *vīvō*, a third-family verb: see the next section.) Which word is first-family? | ***Amēmus***, from *amō*: "and **let's love**." Catullus to his girlfriend Lesbia, in a poem about kisses. |

## 4. Every other family: *-am*

:: Gaudeāmus igitur, iuvenēs dum sumus. || So let's be glad, while we are young. (medieval student song)

Habeam. Dūcam. Capiam. Audiam.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The other families swap in an ***a***. Take the "I" form of *habeō*, drop the *-ō*, add *-am*. | ***Habeam***, then *habeās, habeat, habeāmus, habeātis, habeant*. |
| Same move for *dūcō, capiō, audiō*: the "I" forms? | ***Dūcam, capiam, audiam.*** The *i* of *capiō* and *audiō* stays, the *e* of *habeō* stays: just swap *-ō* for *-am*. |
| Chant *dūcam* all six, and "let's hear!" | *Dūcam, dūcās, dūcat, dūcāmus, dūcātis, dūcant.* ***Audiāmus!*** |
| Grammar books give a memory line for the "we" forms: **"We fEAr A lIAr."** What does it stand for? | The vowels of the four families: amEmus (*e*), habEAmus (*ea*), dūcAmus (*a*), capIAmus / audIAmus (*ia*). |
| The motto: *Gaudeāmus igitur, iuvenēs dum sumus.* (*Gaudeō*: Chapter 43; *igitur*: so, therefore; *iuvenis*: young person; *dum*: while.) | "**So let's rejoice**, while we're young." *Gaudeō* → *gaudeam* → ***gaudeāmus***. A medieval student song, still sung at graduations. |
| Catullus's *vīvāmus* now? | *Vīvō* → *vīvam* → ***vīvāmus***, "let's live." Third family, *-am*. |

## 5. Look-alikes

:: Quid faciam? || What am I to do? (teaching line)

Dūcam? Dūcam!

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 17: what was *dūcam*? | The **future**: "**I will lead**." (Third and fourth families make their future with *-am, -ēs, -et…*) |
| So *dūcam, capiam, audiam, legam, faciam* are… | **Both**: future "I will…" **and** subjunctive "I may / should I…?" The "I" form is the same word. Only sense tells you which. |
| The other persons split apart. "You will lead" vs "you may lead"? | Future ***dūcēs***; subjunctive ***dūcās***. Future has *ē*, subjunctive *ā*. |
| *Habēs* and *amēs*: same ending. Which is the fact? | ***Habēs*** ("you have": *habeō* is second family, its *ē* is home). ***Amēs*** ("you may love": *amō* is first family, so *ē* means subjunctive). Know the family first! |
| *Habet* or *habeat*? *Legit* or *legat*? | *Habet, legit*: facts, "he has, he reads." ***Habeat, legat***: "**let him** have, **let him** read." |
| So how do you build any present subjunctive without guessing? | Know the **family** (from the infinitive). First family: *a* → *e*. All others: the "I" form with *-ō* → *-am*. |

## 6. The rebels with *-im*

:: Quid sit futūrum crās, fuge quaerere. || Don't ask what tomorrow will bring. (Horace, *Odes* 1.9.13; Chapter 17)

Sim. Possim. Velim.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Sum* won't follow the swap. Horace gave you its "he" form, *sit*. Guess "I" and "they." | ***Sim*** … ***sint***. All six: *sim, sīs, sit, sīmus, sītis, sint*. |
| Chapter 18's *possum* = *pot-* + *sum*. Its subjunctive? | ***Possim, possīs, possit, possīmus, possītis, possint.*** *Pot-* + *sim*, with *t* → *s* as in *possum*. |
| Chapter 32's *volō, nōlō, mālō* join the *-im* club. Their "I" forms? | ***Velim, nōlim, mālim.*** Note ***vel-***, not *vol-*. |
| Chant *velim* all six. | *Velim, velīs, velit, velīmus, velītis, velint.* (*Nōlim* and *mālim* go the same way.) |
| *Velim* is a famous polite "I'd like…". Do we use it that way today? | **Not yet.** Wishing and politeness are Chapter 50's job. Today *velim* is only a form to know. |

## 7. The rebels that behave: *eam, feram, fīam*

:: Fīat voluntās tua. || Thy will be done. (Matthew 6:10; Chapter 32)

Eāmus! Ferāmus! Fīat!

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 32's *eō*: its "I" form is *eō*. Drop *-ō*, add *-am*. | ***Eam, eās, eat, eāmus, eātis, eant.*** So *Eāmus!* is perfectly regular. |
| "Let's go away!" "Let's go back!" "Let's go across!" (Chapter 32's *abeō, redeō, trānseō*) | ***Abeāmus! Redeāmus! Trānseāmus!*** |
| *Ferō* and *fīō*? | ***Feram, ferās, ferat…*** and ***fīam, fīās, fīat…*** Chapter 32's *fīō* keeps its long *ī* here: *fīat, fīant*. |
| Matthew 6:9–10, the Lord's Prayer that Chapter 32 began: *adveniat rēgnum tuum, fīat voluntās tua.* (*Adveniō*: arrive, come.) | "**May** your kingdom **come**, **may** your will **be done**": "Thy kingdom come, thy will be done." *Adveniat*: fourth family, *adveniō* → *adveniam*. |
| "Eat!" Careful: in Latin, *eat* is a word. Which? | ***Eat***: "**let him go**," the subjunctive of *eō*. Not dinner! (Rhymes with "ay-at": E-at.) |

!!! success "The Law of the Swapped Vowel"
    The present subjunctive: one vowel swaps, the endings stay (*-m* for "I," then *-s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt*).

    | family | fact (we) | subjunctive (I … they) |
    |---|---|---|
    | 1st *amō* | *amāmus* | *amem, amēs, amet, amēmus, amētis, ament* |
    | 2nd *habeō* | *habēmus* | *habeam, habeās, habeat, habeāmus, habeātis, habeant* |
    | 3rd *dūcō* | *dūcimus* | *dūcam, dūcās, dūcat, dūcāmus, dūcātis, dūcant* |
    | 3rd *capiō* | *capimus* | *capiam, capiās, capiat, capiāmus, capiātis, capiant* |
    | 4th *audiō* | *audīmus* | *audiam, audiās, audiat, audiāmus, audiātis, audiant* |
    | *sum* | *sumus* | *sim, sīs, sit, sīmus, sītis, sint* |
    | *possum* | *possumus* | *possim, possīs, possit, possīmus, possītis, possint* |
    | *volō · nōlō · mālō* | *volumus* | *velim · nōlim · mālim* (*-īs, -it, -īmus, -ītis, -int*) |
    | *eō · ferō · fīō* | *īmus* | *eam · feram · fīam* (*-ās, -at, -āmus, -ātis, -ant*) |
    | *dō* | *damus* | *dem, dēs, det, dēmus, dētis, dent* |

    First family: ***a* → *e***. Everyone else: the "I" form, ***-ō* → *-am***. Five rebels take ***-im***. Beware: *dūcam, audiam, capiam* are also future.

## 8. Passive and deponent: the same swap

:: Haec est diēs quam fēcit Dominus: exultēmus et laetēmur in eā. || This is the day the Lord has made: let's rejoice and be glad in it. (Psalm 117:24, Vulgate; 118 in English Bibles)

Laudētur! Loquāmur!

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 30's passive endings were *-r, -ris, -tur, -mur, -minī, -ntur*. Put them on the swapped vowel: "let it be praised." | ***Laudētur***: *laud-ē-* + *-tur*. And *dūcātur, audiātur*, "let it be led, heard." |
| Matthew 6:9, the line before *adveniat*: *Pater noster, quī es in caelīs, sanctificētur nōmen tuum.* (*Sanctificō*: make holy.) | "Our Father, who art in heaven, **hallowed be** thy name": "**may** your name **be made holy**." A first-family passive subjunctive. |
| Chapter 43's deponents wear passive clothes. "Let's speak!" "Let's follow!" (*loquor, sequor*) | ***Loquāmur! Sequāmur!*** Passive clothes, active meaning, as always. |
| The motto: *exultēmus et laetēmur in eā.* (*Exultō*: leap for joy; *laetor, laetārī*: rejoice, a deponent.) Which is which? | ***Exultēmus***: active, first family, "let's leap for joy." ***Laetēmur***: deponent, first family, "let's be glad." |
| Vergil, *Aeneid* 2.353. Troy is burning; Aeneas tells his last friends: *moriāmur et in media arma ruāmus.* (*Morior*: die, Chapter 43; *arma*: weapons; *ruō*: rush.) | "**Let's die**, and **rush** into the middle of the fighting." *Moriāmur*: deponent; *ruāmus*: active. |

## 9. "Let's!": the hortatory subjunctive

:: Trānseāmus usque Bethleem et videāmus hoc verbum quod factum est. || Let's go over to Bethlehem and see this thing that has happened. (Luke 2:15)

Eāmus! Edāmus! Lūdāmus!

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The "we" subjunctive that urges your own group is called the **hortatory subjunctive**. Chapter 43's *hortor* is the clue. Plain words? | **The "let's" subjunctive**: *hortor* means "urge, encourage." *Eāmus! Edāmus!* "Let's go! Let's eat!" |
| Luke 2:15, the motto. The shepherds of Chapter 47 (*Nōlīte timēre*) and Chapter 37 (*pannīs involūtum*) talk it over. (*Usque*: all the way; *verbum*: word, here "thing.") | "**Let's go over** all the way to Bethlehem **and see** this thing that has happened." *Trānseāmus* (Chapter 32's *trānseō*) and *videāmus*. |
| John 14:31, the motto of §1: *Surgite, eāmus hinc.* (*Surgō*: get up; *hinc*: from here.) Two moods? | *Surgite*: **command** ("get up, y'all"). *Eāmus*: **let's** ("let's go from here"). A command for them, a "let's" that includes the speaker. |
| Genesis 1:26: *Faciāmus hominem ad imāginem et similitūdinem nostram.* (*Imāgō*: image; *similitūdō*: likeness.) | "**Let us make** man in our image and likeness." *Faciō* → *faciam* → ***faciāmus***. |
| 1 Corinthians 15:32 quotes what people say who think death ends all: *Manducēmus et bibāmus, crās enim moriēmur.* (*Manducō*: eat, munch.) | "**Let's eat and drink**, for tomorrow we will die." *Moriēmur* is the plain **future** of *morior*; *manducēmus, bibāmus* are "let's." |
| Vergil, *Eclogues* 10.69, which Chapter 44 left half-read: *Omnia vincit Amor: et nōs cēdāmus Amōrī.* (*Cēdō* + dative: give way, yield.) | "Love conquers all: **let us** too **yield** to Love." The verb form that "waited for Liber V" is *cēdāmus*. |
| What does the priest say before a prayer at Mass, from *ōrō, ōrāre* (pray)? | ***Ōrēmus***, "**let us pray**." First family, *a* → *e*. |

## 10. "Let him!": the jussive subjunctive

:: Cēdant arma togae. || Let weapons give way to the toga. (Cicero, his own verse, quoted in *On Duties* 1.77)

Marcus cāseum ferat!

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Marcus cāseum ferat!* Who is told, and who should do it? | We say it **about** Marcus: "**Let Marcus carry** the cheese," "Marcus **should** carry the cheese." A wish-order for someone not addressed. |
| Grammar books call this the **jussive subjunctive** (*iubeō*, order, Chapter 48's *iussū*). Plain words? | **The "let him / let them" subjunctive**: an order about a third person. (Some books lump it with the hortatory; it's the same move in another person.) |
| Chapter 47 had a third-person order too: *Marcus cāseum portātō.* How is *portet* different? | *Portātō* is the "shall" command of **laws and recipes**, for later or always. *Marcus cāseum portet* is **talk**: "Let Marcus carry it" right now. |
| *Fīat lūx* again. Which kind? | **Jussive**: "**Let** there **be** light." God orders light, which isn't there to be spoken to. |
| Cicero, the motto. (*Arma, armōrum* n. pl.: weapons, war; *toga*: Chapter 10's toga, the citizen's gown, here peace and civil government.) | "**Let** weapons **give way** to the toga": war should yield to peaceful government. *Cēdant* + dative *togae*. Plural "let them." |
| Two later Latin sayings: *Requiēscat in pāce* (on tombs) and *Caveat emptor* (a legal maxim). (*Requiēscō*: rest; *caveō*: beware; *emptor*: buyer, from *emō*.) | "**May** he (she) **rest** in peace" (R.I.P.) and "**Let** the buyer **beware**." |
| Luke 1:38. After *Nē timeās*, Mary answers the angel: *Ecce ancilla Dominī; fīat mihi secundum verbum tuum.* (*Ancilla*: servant girl; *secundum*: according to.) | "Here is the servant of the Lord; **let it be done** to me according to your word." |

## 11. "Don't" and "let's not": *nē*

:: Nē timeās. || Don't be afraid. (Luke 1:30; Chapter 47)

Nē cantēmus! Nē cantet Pater!

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 47: *nōn* is the "not" of facts, ***nē*** the "not" of don'ts and wishes. "Let's not sing!" | ***Nē cantēmus!*** |
| "Dad shouldn't sing!" "Let Grandpa not eat the cheese!" | ***Nē cantet Pater! Nē Avus cāseum edat!*** |
| *Nē timeās*, to Mary. A "let" form aimed at **you**. Is that common? | In everyday classical prose, no: Romans told a "you" with *nōlī* + infinitive (Chapter 6), or with *nē* + another subjunctive form (Chapter 47's *nē timuerītis*, explained in Chapter 53). ***Nē timeās*** belongs to poetry, comedy, and the Bible. |
| Horace, *Odes* 1.11.6–7, the poem that ends in Chapter 6's *carpe diem*: *sapiās, vīna liquēs.* (*Sapiō*: be wise; *liquō*: strain, clear.) | "**Be wise**, **strain** the wine." Poetry tells "you" with the subjunctive; a line later comes the command *carpe diem*. |
| So when you talk, how do you tell one person "don't"? | Still ***Nōlī*** + infinitive: *Nōlī timēre!* Use ***nē*** + subjunctive for "let's not" and "let him not." |

## 12. "What am I to do?": the deliberative subjunctive

:: Quid Rōmae faciam? Mentīrī nesciō. || What am I to do in Rome? I don't know how to lie. (Juvenal, *Satires* 3.41)

Quid faciam? Quō eam?

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Quid faciam?* is no fact and no order. You're asking yourself. Meaning? | "**What am I to do?**", "What should I do?" |
| Grammar books call this the **deliberative subjunctive** (*dēlīberō*, weigh up). Plain words? | **The "what am I to do?" subjunctive**: a question to yourself (or friends) about what to do. *Quid faciāmus?* "What are we to do?" |
| Juvenal 3.41, the motto. A friend is leaving Rome: honest men can't make a living there. (*Rōmae*: Chapter 45's locative; *mentior*: lie, Chapter 43; *nesciō*: Chapter 46.) | "**What am I to do** in Rome? I don't know how to lie." *Faciam* could look future; the sense ("what *should* I do?") makes it deliberative. |
| Terence, *The Eunuch* 46. A young lover, shut out by his girlfriend, is invited back: *Quid igitur faciam? Nōn eam…?* | "So **what am I to do? Shouldn't I go…**?" ***Eam*** is unmistakable: the future would be *ībō*. |
| Three lines later he tries again: *Exclūsit; revocat: redeam?* (*Exclūdō*: shut out; *revocō*: call back.) | "She shut me out; she calls me back: **should I go back?**" *Redeam*: Chapter 32's *redeō*. |
| Look at Terence's "not." *Nōn* or *nē*? | ***Nōn eam?*** The deliberative asks about a fact-to-be, so its "not" is ***nōn***. Only "let's" and "let him" use *nē*. |
| "Where am I to go?" "What are we to say?" "Am I to sing?" (*cantō*) | ***Quō eam? Quid dīcāmus? Cantem?*** |

!!! success "The Law of Let's, Let Him, and What Now?"
    | job | plain words | person | "not" | example |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | **hortatory** | "let's…" | we | *nē* | *Eāmus! Nē cantēmus!* |
    | **jussive** | "let him / let them…," "he should…" | he, she, they | *nē* | *Fīat lūx. Nē cantet Pater!* |
    | **deliberative** | "what am I to do?" | I, we (in a question) | *nōn* | *Quid faciam? Nōn eam?* |

    All three stand alone, as the main verb. Telling **you** "don't": still *nōlī* + infinitive in talk; *nē timeās* in poetry and the Bible.

## 13. Old chunks, opened

:: Ōdī et amō. Quārē id faciam, fortasse requīris. || I hate and I love. Why I do that, perhaps you ask. (Catullus 85.1; Chapter 46)

Faciam. Faciam?

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 46 promised: Catullus 85 line 1 asks its question "in a mood you'll meet in Liber V." Find the word. (*Quārē*: why; *requīrō*: ask.) | ***Faciam***: "**why I do** that." Not the future (Catullus isn't talking about tomorrow): it's the subjunctive. |
| Is *faciam* there a "let's," a "let him," or a "what am I to do?" | **None of today's three.** It's a question tucked inside a sentence ("you ask *why I do it*"). That job is Chapter 53's. Today: just recognize the form. |
| Horace's *sit*, Catullus's *faciam*, Juvenal's *faciam*. Which one is a main-verb question you can already explain? | **Juvenal's**: *Quid Rōmae faciam?* stands alone, deliberative. Horace's and Catullus's sit inside a bigger sentence: Chapter 53. |
| Matthew 27:22. Pilate asks the crowd: *Quid igitur faciam dē Iēsū, quī dīcitur Christus?* | "What **then am I to do** with Jesus, who is called Christ?" Deliberative: Pilate weighs his choice out loud. |
| Chapter 32's *Fīat!* (Grandma's "So be it!"). Now? | Jussive *fīat*: "**let it happen**." The chunk is a sentence now. |

## 15. Traps and fix-its

:: Errāre hūmānum est; corrigāmus! || To err is human; let's correct it! (teaching line)

Īmus? Ībimus? Eāmus!

{#g-15}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Īmus · Ībimus · Eāmus.* Three meanings? | "**We go**" (fact, now) · "**we will go**" (fact, later) · "**let's go**" (urging). |
| "Let's not sing": *Nōn cantēmus.* | ***Nē cantēmus.*** "Let's" takes *nē*. |
| "Let's have dinner": *Cēnam habēmus.* | ***Cēnam habeāmus.*** *Habēmus* is the fact, "we have." Second family: *-eāmus*. |
| "Let's lead the dog": *Canem dūcēmus.* | ***Canem dūcāmus.*** *Dūcēmus* is the future, "we will lead." |
| "Let him be good": *Bonus est.* / *Bonus eat.* | ***Bonus sit.*** *Sum* → *sit*. (*Eat* is "let him go"!) |
| "Let's want the cheese": *Volāmus cāseum.* | ***Velīmus cāseum.*** *Volō* goes *vel-* + *-im*. |
| "Should I not go?": *Nē eam?* | ***Nōn eam?*** The deliberative's "not" is *nōn*. |

## 16. Lectiō: *Eāmus!*

:: Duo cāseī meliōrēs quam ūnus. || Two cheeses are better than one. (teaching line)

Quid hodiē faciāmus?

Diē septimā decimā: the morning after Chapter 48. Grandpa laid down his one-day dictatorship and went to bed, and the dinner cheese was shared eight ways. Now there is no cheese in the house. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. Hunt every subjunctive and ask: "let's," "let him," or "what am I to do?" New in passing: *septimus decimus*, seventeenth; *flūmen*, river; *mercātor*, merchant; *convīvium*, feast; *invēnī*, I found (perfect of *inveniō*); *aliā viā*, by another road. Old friends: *iī*, I went (Chapter 32); *domī* (Chapter 45); *meliōrēs quam* (Chapter 33).

Marcus | Lūdāmus! || Marcus | Let's play!
Quīntus | Ad flūmen eāmus! || Quintus | Let's go to the river!
Pater | Cantēmus! || Dad | Let's sing!
Omnēs | Nē cantet Pater! || Everyone | Dad shouldn't sing!
Avia | Audīte. Nūllus cāseus domī est. Prīmum cāseum novum emāmus. || Grandma | Listen. There's no cheese in the house. First let's buy a new cheese.
Māter | Bene. Eāmus ad forum! || Mom | Good. Let's go to the forum!
Avia | Sed Avus domī maneat. Et canis quoque domī maneat. || Grandma | But let Grandpa stay home. And let the dog stay home too.

Avia | Dormiās, Ave. Nēmō tē vexet. || Grandma | Sleep, Grandpa. Let nobody bother you.
Iōannēs | Familia ad forum it. Avus et canis domī manent. || John | The family goes to the forum. Grandpa and the dog stay home.

Cornēlia | Quis cāseum ferat? || Cornelia | Who should carry the cheese?
Marcus | Quīntus ferat! || Marcus | Let Quintus carry it!
Quīntus | Cūr ego? Canis ferat! || Quintus | Why me? Let the dog carry it!
Cornēlia | Canis domī est, stulte! || Cornelia | The dog is at home, silly!
Māter | Marcus et Quīntus cāseum ūnā ferant. Domum redeāmus! || Mom | Let Marcus and Quintus carry the cheese together. Let's go back home!
Iōannēs | Domum redīmus. In mēnsā est… alter cāseus! Avus sedet et rīdet. || John | We come back home. On the table is… another cheese! Grandpa is sitting and smiling.

Avus | Edāmus, bibāmus, gaudeāmus! || Grandpa | Let's eat, let's drink, let's be glad!
Pater | Et cantēmus? || Dad | And let's sing?
Omnēs | Nē cantet Pater! || Everyone | Dad shouldn't sing!

Iōannēs | Mēnsa vacua est. Sub mēnsā canis sedet et lambit… || John | The table's empty. Under the table the dog sits and licks…

{#g-16}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Find the three "let's" lines of the morning. Which one wins? | *Lūdāmus! · Ad flūmen eāmus! · Cantēmus!* Then Grandma's ***Cāseum novum emāmus*** wins. |
| *Nē cantet Pater!* Which job, and why *nē*? | **Jussive**, "let Dad not sing," said about Dad. "Let him" takes ***nē***. (Chapter 47's law four, *Pater nē cantātō*, in talk form.) |
| Grandpa: *Quid faciam? Dormiam?* | **Deliberative**: "What am I to do? Am I to sleep?" Questions to himself. |
| *Avus domī maneat · Canis ferat · Marcus et Quīntus cāseum ūnā ferant.* Which mood and job? Why *ferant*? | Subjunctive, **jussive**: let him / let them. *Ferant* is plural: Marcus **and** Quintus. |
| *Quis cāseum ferat?* Cornelia isn't ordering. What is she doing? | Asking "**who is to** carry the cheese?": a **deliberative** question in the third person. |
| Grandma: *Dormiās, Ave.* A "you" subjunctive. Why does it sound soft? | It's a gentle, "you may sleep" kind of push, not a command: grandmothers and poets talk this way. In plain talk, *Dormī!* |
| How did the house end up with two cheeses, and then one? | Grandpa went to the forum **by another road** (*aliā viā*) and bought one too. While everyone celebrated, the dog ate Grandpa's: ***In stomachō… canis!*** |

## 17. Sound check

:: Clārē loquāmur! || Let's speak clearly! (teaching line)

Audī et dīc.

{#g-17}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *amem · amēmus · amētis · ament*? | A-mem · a-MAY-mus · a-MAY-tis · A-ment. In *amēmus* the long *ē* next to last takes the emphasis (stress). |
| *habeam · habeāmus · audiam · audiāmus*? | HA-be-am · ha-be-AH-mus · OW-di-am · ow-di-AH-mus. The short *e* and *i* before a vowel never take the emphasis. |
| *eam · eāmus · eat · fīat*? | E-am · e-AH-mus · E-at · FEE-at. Two syllables in *eam* and *eat*, not "yam," not "eat." |
| *sim · sīmus · possim · velim · velīmus*? | SIM · SEE-mus · POS-sim · WE-lim · we-LEE-mus. *V* is *w*. |
| *gaudeāmus · laetēmur · loquāmur · moriāmur*? | gow-de-AH-mus · lye-TAY-mur · lo-KWAH-mur · mo-ri-AH-mur. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Loquāmur Latīnē! || Let's speak Latin! (teaching line)

1. The three moods in your own words, with *Īmus · Īte! · Eāmus!*
2. Chant the present subjunctive of *amō, habeō, dūcō, capiō, audiō*: I, you, he, we, y'all, they.
3. Chant *sim, possim, velim*, and *eam, feram, fīam*; then *dem, dēs*.
4. Propose five plans with "let's" (*Eāmus! Cāseum emāmus! Lūdāmus!…*) and one "let's not."
5. Give three "let him / let them" orders about people who aren't there (*Marcus cāseum ferat · Nē cantet Pater…*).
6. Ask yourself three deliberative questions (*Quid faciam? Quō eam? Nōn eam?*).
7. The three Laws in your own words: Three Moods, Swapped Vowel, Let's / Let Him / What Now.
8. The real lines: *Surgite, eāmus hinc · Vīvāmus, mea Lesbia, atque amēmus · Gaudeāmus igitur · Fīat lūx · Adveniat rēgnum tuum, fīat voluntās tua · Trānseāmus usque Bethleem et videāmus · Faciāmus hominem · Et nōs cēdāmus Amōrī · Cēdant arma togae · Quid Rōmae faciam?
