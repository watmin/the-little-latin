# Caput XXXIII: Melior et Optimus

*Liber III, Fābulae · Chapter 33*

*In which everything gets bigger, better, and best. Back in Chapter 3 you learned* optima, *"the very best," and were told it was "the strongest form of* bona.*" Chapter 19's Grandma said* Antīquus cāseus optimus est. *Chapter 27 called the pluperfect* plūs quam perfectum, *"more than perfect," and Chapter 28 promised: "comparisons, Chapter 33." Chapter 32 gave you* mālō… quam, *"I'd rather… than." Today all of it pays off: taller and tallest, sweeter than honey, the bravest of all, and the five famous rule-breakers* good, bad, big, small, much. *Along the way: why English says* major, minor, plus, *and* minus, *a verse from the Gospel with John's own name in it, and a cheese contest that Grandpa is sure he'll win. Speaking goal: compare people, food, and things, and say who or what is the best.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Marcus altior est quam Quīntus. · Melle dulcior! · Cāseus meus melior est quam tuus! · Quis est optimus? · Fortissimus omnium! · Plūs cāseī, quaesō!*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** positive (the plain form); comparative (the "more" form); superlative (the "most / very" form); old-school "degrees of comparison"; ablative of comparison (the "than" ablative)
    - **Forms:** *altior, altius* and the whole comparative (*altiōris, altiōre, altiōra, altiōrum*) · *altissimus* · *ācerrimus, celerrimus, pulcherrimus* · *facillimus, difficillimus, simillimus* · *melior/optimus, peior/pessimus, maior/maximus, minor/minimus, plūs/plūrimus*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Comparative · the Law of *Quam* and the Ablative · the Law of the Superlative · the Law of the Five Rebels
    - **Words:** *quam* (than); *altus, pulcher, clārus, difficilis, similis*; *multō* (by much)
    - **Reading:** *Quis cāseus optimus?* (Grandpa's homemade cheese against the one from the forum, and a judge nobody expected)
    - **Speaking:** say "taller than," "sweeter than," "the best of all," and "more cheese, please"

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep three old friends close: Chapter 13's *rēx, rēgis* (its endings come back today), Chapter 20's *fortis, forte* (watch where today's words *differ* from it), and Chapter 22's Law of the Part ("of all").

## 1. Good, better, best

:: Bonus, melior, optimus. || Good, better, best. (teaching line)

Cāseus bonus. Cāseus melior. Cāseus optimus!

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 3 taught *optimus, optima, optimum* as a single word: "best, very good." Read the blurb. Which word is "good," and which is "best"? | ***Bonus*** is "good." ***Optimus*** is "best." So the one in the middle, ***melior***, must be "**better**." |
| English does this to almost every describing word. "Tall, taller, tallest." "Sweet, sweeter, sweetest." How many steps are there? | **Three**: the plain word, the "more" word (*-er*), and the "most" word (*-est*). Latin has the same three. |
| Plain English: the plain form (*bonus*, "good") is called the **positive**. Why "positive"? | It just "sets down" the quality (Latin *positus*, "placed," Chapter 31's fourth key of *pōnō*). Nothing "plus or minus" about it: just *bonus*. |
| The "more" form (*melior*, "better") is the **comparative**, from *comparō*, "match up, compare." Why that name? | Because it always compares two things: "**better than** something." Cheese A is better than cheese B. |
| The "most" form (*optimus*, "best") is the **superlative**, from *superlātus*, "carried over the top" (*super* + Chapter 32's *lātum*!). Why? | It puts one thing above **all** the others: "the best of all." Old-school grammars call the three steps the **degrees of comparison**: positive, comparative, superlative. |
| One more trick. Chapter 3's farmer said *Ita, optima!*, "Yes, the very best!" Is a superlative always "the best of all"? | **No.** Often it just means "**very**": *optimus* = "best" or "very good." Context decides. (Chapter 3's vocabulary said both: "best, very good.") |

## 2. *Altior*: the "more" form

:: Altior et altior. || Higher and higher. (teaching line)

Altus, altior. Longus, longior. Dulcis, dulcior.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| A new adjective: ***altus, alta, altum***, "high, tall, deep." Read the blurb. How do you say "taller"? | ***Altior.*** Just as "taller" is "tall" + *-er*, Latin puts ***-ior*** on the stem: *alt-ior*. |
| "Longer" (Chapter 3's *longus*). "Older" (Chapter 3's *antīquus*). "Colder" (*frīgidus*). | ***Longior. Antīquior. Frīgidior.*** Take off *-us*, add *-ior*. |
| Does *-ior* change for a woman? "Marcus is taller." "Cornēlia is taller." | **No**: ***Marcus altior est. Cornēlia altior est.*** One form for masculine **and** feminine, like Chapter 20's *fortis*. |
| And for a neuter? Chapter 14's *corpus*: "The body is taller." Guess from English "plus" and "minus." | ***Corpus altius est.*** The neuter is ***-ius***: *alt-ius* (short *u*). Two naming forms: *altior* (m./f.), *altius* (n.). |
| Now Chapter 20's third-family adjectives. *Fortis* "brave," *dulcis* "sweet," *gravis* "heavy." Where does *-ior* go? | On the stem, after you drop *-is*: ***fortior, dulcior, gravior*** ("braver, sweeter, heavier"); neuters ***fortius, dulcius, gravius***. |
| Chapter 13's Law of the Stem: the **genitive** shows the stem. *Fēlīx, fēlīcis* "lucky"; *sapiēns, sapientis* "wise"; *ācer, ācris* "sharp." Make "luckier," "wiser," "sharper." | ***Fēlīcior, sapientior, ācrior.*** Always the genitive stem: *fēlīc-*, *sapient-*, *ācr-*, never the naming form. |

## 3. Declining the comparative

:: Exēgī monumentum aere perennius. || I have finished a monument more lasting than bronze. (Horace, *Odes* 3.30.1)

Altior, altiōris. Cum puerō altiōre. Corpora altiōra.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The dictionary gives a comparative as a pair: ***altior, altiōris***. The genitive ends in *-is*. Which family? | The **third** (Chapter 13's *rēx, rēgis*). The stem is ***altiōr-***, and every ending goes on it. Notice the *ō* is **long** in every form except *altior* itself. |
| Chapter 13's endings on *altiōr-*: "of the taller boy," "to the taller boy," "I see the taller boy." | ***Puerī altiōris · puerō altiōrī · puerum altiōrem videō.*** Just like *rēgis, rēgī, rēgem*. |
| Now the big one. "With the taller boy." Chapter 20 said *cum rēge fortī*, with *-ī*. What about *altiōr-*? | ***Cum puerō altiōre***, with ***-e***, like *cum rēge*. Comparatives are **not** i-stems: they decline like *rēx*, not like *fortis*. |
| Plurals: "taller boys," "of the taller boys." | ***Puerī altiōrēs · puerōrum altiōrum.*** *-um*, like *rēgum*, not *-ium* like *fortium*. |
| The neuter plural. Chapter 14's *corpus, corporis → corpora*. "Taller bodies." | ***Corpora altiōra***: *-a*, like *corpora*, not *-ia* like *fortia*. |
| Look again at the neuter: *altius, altiōris*. Chapter 14's Law of the Disguise said *-us, -oris* is a third-family **neuter**. Does it fit? | **Yes.** *Altius, altiōris* goes just like *corpus, corporis*: neuter, third family, *-us* in disguise. |
| Horace, ending his book of poems: *Exēgī monumentum aere perennius.* (*Exigō, exēgī*: finish. *Aes, aeris*, n.: bronze. *Perennis*: lasting.) Which word is a comparative, and why does it end in *-ius*? | ***Perennius***, "**more lasting**": neuter, agreeing with *monumentum*, the object. "I have finished a monument more lasting than bronze." (And *aere*, "than bronze," has no "than" word at all: §5.) |

!!! success "The Law of the Comparative"
    To say "more …," take the **genitive stem** and add ***-ior*** (masculine and feminine) or ***-ius*** (neuter). Then decline it like ***rēx*** and ***corpus***: a plain third-family word, **not** an i-stem.

    | | m./f. | n. | m./f. pl. | n. pl. |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | naming | *altior* | *altius* | *altiōrēs* | *altiōra* |
    | of | *altiōris* | *altiōris* | *altiōrum* | *altiōrum* |
    | to, for | *altiōrī* | *altiōrī* | *altiōribus* | *altiōribus* |
    | object | *altiōrem* | *altius* | *altiōrēs* | *altiōra* |
    | by, with | *altiōre* | *altiōre* | *altiōribus* | *altiōribus* |

    Watch the three places it parts from *fortis*: ***-e*** (not *-ī*), ***-a*** (not *-ia*), ***-um*** (not *-ium*).

## 4. *Quam*: than

:: Plūs quam perfectum. || More than perfect. (Chapter 27's name for the pluperfect)

Marcus altior est quam Quīntus.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 32: *Domī manēre mālō quam ad forum īre*, "I'd rather stay home **than** go." Chapter 28 called this ***quam*** "than." Read the blurb. | "**Marcus is taller than Quīntus.**" The comparative, then ***quam***, then the other thing. |
| What case is *Quīntus*? Why? | **Nominative**, the same as *Marcus*. Both are being matched up as subjects: "Marcus is taller than Quīntus (is)." |
| "Grandma is wiser than Grandpa." | ***Avia sapientior est quam Avus.*** Both nominative. |
| Now an object. "I see a taller boy than Marcus." Hmm, simpler: "I have a bigger … " wait. Try: "Dad loves Grandpa's cheese more than…" No: just this. "I see Marcus, taller than Quīntus." | ***Marcum, altiōrem quam Quīntum, videō.*** *Marcum* is the object, so *altiōrem* agrees with it, and after *quam* comes ***Quīntum***, the **same case**. |
| The rule so far: what case comes after *quam*? | **The same case as the thing it's matched against.** *Quam* is like an equals sign with a tilt: both sides wear the same case. |
| Chapter 27's name for the pluperfect: *plūs quam perfectum*. Now you can read it. *Plūs* is a comparative (§9). | "**More than perfect**": *plūs* "more," *quam* "than." Chapter 28's promise kept. |

## 5. Sweeter than honey

:: Lūce sunt clāriōra nōbīs tua cōnsilia omnia. || All your plans are clearer to us than daylight. (Cicero, *Against Catiline* 1.6, 63 BC)

Mel dulce est. Cāseus melle dulcior est?

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Read the blurb. *Melle* is Chapter 14's *mel, mellis* in the ablative. There's no *quam*. What does the question ask? | "**Is cheese sweeter than honey?**" (No.) The "than" thing can drop *quam* and go into the **ablative**. |
| Plain English: this is the **ablative of comparison**, the "than" ablative. Say "Marcus is taller than Quīntus" without *quam*. | ***Marcus Quīntō altior est.*** *Quīntō*: ablative, "than Quīntus." |
| So two ways, one meaning. Say "Grandma is wiser than Grandpa" both ways. | ***Avia sapientior est quam Avus.*** · ***Avia Avō sapientior est.*** |
| Cicero, about Nestor, the old king in Homer: *ex eius linguā melle dulcior fluēbat ōrātiō* (*Dē Senectūte* 31; *fluō*: flow; *ōrātiō*: speech). | "From his tongue flowed speech **sweeter than honey**." Cicero is quoting Homer (*Iliad* 1.249). Chapter 14's *lingua* and *mel*, all grown up. |
| Cicero to Catiline, the plotter of Chapter 32: *Lūce sunt clāriōra nōbīs tua cōnsilia omnia.* (*Lūx, lūcis*: light, Chapter 31. *Clārus*: clear, bright. *Cōnsilium*: plan.) Find the comparative and its "than." | ***Clāriōra***, "clearer," neuter plural to match *cōnsilia*; and ***lūce***, "than daylight," the ablative of comparison. "All your plans are clearer to us than daylight." |
| Back to Horace's *aere perennius*. Which noun is the "than"? | ***Aere***, ablative of *aes*: "more lasting **than bronze**." |
| When can you skip *quam*? Look at the examples: was the first thing a subject or object each time? | Yes: *Marcus, Avia, ōrātiō, cōnsilia* (subjects), *monumentum* (object). The ablative shortcut is for comparing a **subject or an object**. *Quam* works everywhere, so when in doubt, use *quam*. |

!!! success "The Law of *Quam* and the Ablative"
    "Than" can be said two ways:

    - ***quam*** + the **same case** on both sides: *Marcus altior est quam Quīntus.*
    - **no *quam***, the "than" thing in the **ablative**: *Marcus Quīntō altior est. Melle dulcior.*

    The ablative way is for when the first thing is a subject or an object. *Quam* always works.

## 6. *Altissimus*: the "most" form

:: Hōrum omnium fortissimī sunt Belgae. || Of all these, the bravest are the Belgians. (Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.1)

Altus, altior, altissimus.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Read the blurb. *Altissimus*: what does the ending *-issimus* add? | "**Tallest, highest**," or "**very tall**." Stem + ***-issimus***: *alt-issimus*. |
| *-issimus, -issima, -issimum*. Which family? | Chapter 3's ***bonus, bona, bonum***: the first and second families. The superlative is an ordinary *-us, -a, -um* adjective. |
| "The longest road." "The coldest water." "The bravest girl." | ***Via longissima. Aqua frīgidissima. Puella fortissima.*** (Chapter 20's *fortis* → *fort-issima*.) |
| "Very sweet honey" (*mel*, neuter). "Very lucky boys." | ***Mel dulcissimum. Puerī fēlīcissimī.*** Genitive stem again: *fēlīc-issimus*. |
| Caesar, right after Chapter 15's *Gallia est omnis dīvīsa in partēs trēs*: *Hōrum omnium fortissimī sunt Belgae.* (*Hōrum*: Chapter 19's *hic*. *Belgae*: the Belgians.) What case is *hōrum omnium*, and why? | **Genitive**: "**of** all these." Chapter 22's Law of the Part: the whole you pick from goes in the genitive. "Of all these, the **bravest** are the Belgians." |
| Make your own: "Grandpa is the oldest of all." "Cornēlia is the wisest of the kids." | ***Avus antīquissimus omnium est. Cornēlia sapientissima līberōrum est.*** (*Līberōrum*, Chapter 10.) |

## 7. *-errimus* and *-illimus*

:: Ācerrimus omnium cāseōrum. || The sharpest of all cheeses. (teaching line)

Ācer, ācrior, ācerrimus. Facilis, facilior, facillimus.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 20's *ācer, ācris, ācre*, "sharp." You made *ācrior* from the stem. Read the blurb. What happens in the superlative? | ***Ācerrimus***, "sharpest." Adjectives whose masculine ends in ***-er*** add ***-rimus*** to that **naming form**: *ācer-rimus*. |
| Chapter 20's *celer*, "fast." "Faster." "Fastest." | ***Celerior · celerrimus.*** (*Celer-rimus*.) |
| Chapter 3's *niger*, "black." A new one: ***pulcher, pulchra, pulchrum***, "beautiful, handsome." Make the superlatives. | ***Nigerrimus · pulcherrimus.*** Their comparatives come from the stem, as usual: *nigrior, pulchrior*. |
| Chapter 20's *facilis*, "easy." The superlative is ***facillimus***. What became of *-issimus*? | ***Facil-limus***: the *l* doubles and takes the place of *-issimus*'s *ss*. "Easiest, very easy." |
| Only six adjectives do this, all ending in *-ilis*. Learn two more: ***difficilis*** "hard, difficult" and ***similis*** "like, similar." Their superlatives? | ***Difficillimus · simillimus.*** (The other three are rare for now: *dissimilis, gracilis, humilis*.) Every other *-lis* word is regular. |
| Chapter 3's *caeruleus*, "sky blue," would make an ugly pile of vowels: *caeruleior*. Romans said ***magis caeruleus***, "more blue," with Chapter 18's *magis*, "more." Why do you think? | To dodge the vowel crash: adjectives with a vowel before *-us* (*-eus, -ius*) usually use ***magis*** instead of *-ior*. Recognize it; you'll rarely need it. |

!!! success "The Law of the Superlative"
    To say "most …" or "very …":

    - most adjectives: genitive stem + ***-issimus*** (*altissimus, fortissimus, fēlīcissimus*)
    - masculine in ***-er***: naming form + ***-rimus*** (*ācerrimus, celerrimus, pulcherrimus*)
    - six ***-ilis*** words: stem + ***-limus*** (*facillimus, difficillimus, simillimus*)

    Then decline it like ***bonus, bona, bonum***.

## 8. *Melior* and *optimus*, *peior* and *pessimus*

:: Corruptiō optimī pessima. || The corruption of the best is the worst. (a later Latin proverb)

Bonus, melior, optimus. Malus, peior, pessimus.

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| English doesn't say "gooder" or "goodest." Latin doesn't say *bonior*. Read the blurb. What are the three steps of "good"? | ***Bonus, melior, optimus***: good, better, best. Three different roots, just like English "good, better, best" (and "bad, worse, worst"). |
| But *melior* still behaves. Decline it from what you learned in §3: neuter, genitive, "with a better cheese." | Neuter ***melius***; genitive ***meliōris***; ***cum cāseō meliōre***. A regular comparative (Law of the Comparative); only its root is wild. |
| "Bad, worse, worst" (Chapter 3's *malus*). | ***Malus, peior, pessimus.*** Neuter *peius*. (Careful: the *a* in *malus* is short, unlike *mālum*, apple.) |
| Say *peior* and Chapter 33's other wild one, *maior*: there's a hidden double sound. | **PEI-yor**, **MAI-yor**: the *i* is said twice, once in the diphthong and once as a *y*, so the first syllable is heavy. There's no macron because the vowel itself is short. |
| Chapter 19's Grandma: *Antīquus cāseus optimus est.* Chapter 14's kids: *Nōmen optimum!* Translate both two ways. | "Old cheese is **the best** / **very good**." · "**The best** name! / A **very good** name!" |
| The motto: *Corruptiō optimī pessima.* (*Corruptiō, -ōnis*, f.: rotting, corruption. *Est* is understood.) Why *optimī* and *pessima*? | ***Optimī***: genitive, "of the best (man / thing)." ***Pessima***: feminine, agreeing with *corruptiō*: "the worst." "**When the best goes bad, it's the worst.**" |

## 9. *Maior* and *minor*

:: Nōn surrēxit inter nātōs mulierum maior Iōanne Baptistā. || Among those born of women there has not risen one greater than John the Baptist. (Matthew 11:11)

Magnus, maior, maximus. Parvus, minor, minimus.

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 3's *magnus*, "big, great." Read the blurb. "Bigger," "biggest"? | ***Maior · maximus.*** Neuter *maius*. English kept both: *major* and *maximum*. |
| Chapter 3's *parvus*, "small." "Smaller," "smallest"? | ***Minor · minimus.*** Neuter ***minus***: English kept *minor*, *minus*, and *minimum*. |
| Chapter 2's answer word ***minimē***, "no, not at all." Which word is hiding inside it? | ***Minimus***: *minimē* is "least of all," so "**not in the least**." It's a how-word (an adverb), and adverbs are Chapter 34. |
| Rome's greatest god had a title: *Iuppiter Optimus Maximus*. And the chariot track was the *Circus Maximus*. Translate. | "**Jupiter, Best and Greatest**." · "**The Biggest Circle**" (racetrack). Altars were inscribed *I. O. M.* |
| Jesus on John the Baptist: *Nōn surrēxit inter nātōs mulierum maior Iōanne Baptistā.* (*Surgō, surrēxī*: rise. *Nātī*: those born. *Mulier*: woman, Chapter 13.) Find the "than." | ***Iōanne Baptistā***, ablative of comparison: "greater **than John** the Baptist." Your name, in the ablative (Chapter 13's *Iōanne*)! |
| The verse goes on: *quī autem minor est in rēgnō caelōrum, maior est illō.* (*Autem*: but. *Rēgnum*: kingdom.) | "But **the one who is smaller** in the kingdom of heaven is **greater than he**." *Minor, maior*, and *illō*, Chapter 19's *ille* as the "than" ablative. |

## 10. *Plūs*: more

:: Ē plūribus ūnum. || Out of many, one. (motto of the United States; after *color est ē plūribus ūnus*, *Moretum* 102)

Multus, plūs, plūrimus.

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Multus*, "much, many" (Chapter 18's *multōs cāseōs*). Read the blurb. Its comparative is ***plūs***. What English words did it give us? | *Plus* (more), *plural* (more than one), and *surplus*. |
| In the singular, ***plūs*** is a neuter **noun**, "more," "a larger amount," and the stuff goes in the genitive (Chapter 22's Law of the Part, like *satis pecūniae*). "More cheese!" | ***Plūs cāseī!*** Word for word "more **of** cheese." (*Plūs cāseus* is wrong.) |
| In the plural, it's an adjective: ***plūrēs*** (m./f.), ***plūra*** (n.), "more, several." "More cheeses." "More apples." | ***Plūrēs cāseī. Plūra māla.*** These agree with the noun, like any adjective. |
| One surprise: the genitive plural is ***plūrium***, with an *i*, though the other comparatives say *altiōrum*. And the ablative plural? | ***Plūribus***, like *altiōribus*. *Plūs* is the one comparative that misbehaves a little. |
| The superlative is ***plūrimus***, "most, very many." "Grandpa eats very much cheese." | ***Avus plūrimum cāseum edit.*** |
| The motto. *Ē plūribus ūnum*, on the Great Seal of the United States. It echoes a little Latin poem once credited to Vergil, the *Moretum*, where a farmer pounds garlic, herbs, and **cheese** into one green paste: *color est ē plūribus ūnus.* Translate the poem's line. | "**Out of many, there is one color.**" *Ē* + ablative *plūribus* (Chapter 7). The Great Seal's motto came, in part, from a cheese recipe. |

!!! success "The Law of the Five Rebels"
    Five common adjectives use other roots. Their endings are still regular: the comparative goes like *altior*, the superlative like *bonus*.

    | plain | more | most |
    |---|---|---|
    | *bonus* good | *melior, melius* better | *optimus* best |
    | *malus* bad | *peior, peius* worse | *pessimus* worst |
    | *magnus* big | *maior, maius* bigger | *maximus* biggest |
    | *parvus* small | *minor, minus* smaller | *minimus* smallest |
    | *multus* much | *plūs* (*plūrēs, plūra*) more | *plūrimus* most |

## 11. "Very," "rather," "of all"

:: Maior autem hōrum est cāritās. || But the greatest of these is love. (1 Corinthians 13:13)

Fessus. Fessior? Optimus omnium!

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Sometimes a comparative has **no** "than." *Avus fessior est.* Avus is "more tired" than… what? | Than usual: "**rather** tired," "**too** tired," "quite tired." A comparative with no *quam* and no ablative means "rather, too, more than normal." |
| Sort the meanings: *Cāseus dulcissimus est* · *Cāseus dulcissimus omnium est*. | "The cheese is **very** sweet." · "The cheese is the **sweetest** of all." The genitive "of all" makes it "-est." |
| Saint Paul, on faith, hope, and love (Chapter 23's *fidēs* and *spēs*): *Nunc autem manent fidēs, spēs, cāritās, tria haec: maior autem hōrum est cāritās.* (*Maneō*: remain. *Cāritās*: love.) English says "the **greatest** of these." What does the Latin say? | ***Maior***: "the **greater** of these," a comparative with the Law of the Part (*hōrum*). Saint Jerome copied the Greek word for word; with three things, Latin usually says *maxima*. |
| English has a crowd of Latin comparatives: *senior, junior, superior, inferior, interior, exterior, prior*. *Senior* is from *senex*, "old man"; *junior* from *iuvenis*, "young man." What do they mean? | ***Senior***: "older." ***Junior***: "younger." The others are "higher, lower, inner, outer, earlier." All *-ior*, all comparatives. |
| How much bigger? ***Multō maior***: "**much** bigger," "bigger by much." (*Multō*: ablative of *multum*.) "Grandpa's cheese is much smaller." | ***Cāseus Avī multō minor est.*** Just a chunk for now; Chapter 35 explains this ablative. |
| The Olympic motto: *Citius, altius, fortius.* You know *altius* and *fortius* as neuter comparatives. Here they mean "faster, higher, stronger" as **how-words**. | Run "faster," jump "higher," be "stronger": the neuter comparative also works as an **adverb**, a how-word. That's Chapter 34's whole job. (*Citius* is from *citus*, "quick.") |

## 12. Look-alikes

:: Mālum maius, mālum melius. || A bigger apple, a better apple. (teaching line; *mālum* the apple, not *malum*!)

Melior an melius? Minor an minus? Plūs an plūrēs?

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Cāseus melior* · *mālum melius*. | Both "better." ***Melior*** for the masculine *cāseus*, ***melius*** for the neuter *mālum*. |
| *Maior* · *maius* · *magis*. | ***Maior***: bigger (m./f.). ***Maius***: bigger (n.). ***Magis***: "more" (Chapter 18), the how-word that goes with another adjective (*magis caeruleus*). |
| *Altiōre* · *altī* · *fortī*. Which is "with the taller"? | ***Altiōre*** (comparative, ablative, *-e*). *Altī* is "of the tall" (genitive of *altus*). *Fortī* is "with the brave" (Chapter 20's i-stem *-ī*). |
| *Plūs cāseī* · *plūrēs cāseī*. | "**More cheese**" (an amount: *plūs* + genitive). · "**More cheeses**" (several of them: *plūrēs* agreeing with *cāseī*). |
| Three *quam*s: *puella quam videō* (Chapter 28) · *Quam* … in Chapter 32's *mālō… quam* · *altior quam Marcus*. | The first is the relative "whom." The second and third are both "**than**": after *mālō*, "rather than"; after a comparative, "than." |
| Translate, naming each degree: *Mel dulce est. Mel dulcius est quam cāseus. Mel dulcissimum est.* | "Honey is sweet (**positive**). Honey is sweeter than cheese (**comparative**). Honey is very sweet / the sweetest (**superlative**)." |

## 13. Fix it

:: Errāre hūmānum est; corrigere optimum. || To err is human; to correct is best. (teaching line, after Chapter 32's proverb)

Bonior? Magnior? Fortissimus?

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Grandpa's cheese is better": *Cāseus Avī bonior est.* | ***Cāseus Avī melior est.*** *Bonus* is a rebel: *melior*, never *bonior*. |
| "Marcus is bigger than Quīntus": *Marcus magnior est quam Quīntō.* | ***Marcus maior est quam Quīntus*** (same case after *quam*), or ***Marcus Quīntō maior est*** (ablative, no *quam*). Never mix the two. |
| "The sharpest cheese": *cāseus ācrissimus.* | ***Cāseus ācerrimus.*** *-er* adjectives add *-rimus* to the naming form. |
| "The easiest book": *liber facilissimus.* | ***Liber facillimus.*** One of the six *-lis* words. |
| "With a taller boy": *cum puerō altiōrī.* | ***Cum puerō altiōre.*** Comparatives take *-e*, not *-ī* (Law of the Comparative). |
| "More cheese": *Plūs cāseus.* | ***Plūs cāseī.*** Singular *plūs* is a noun: the stuff goes in the genitive. |

## 14. Lectiō: *Quis cāseus optimus?*

:: Quis cāseus optimus est? || Which cheese is the best? (teaching line)

Maior, melior, optimus. Et canis?

{#g-14}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| John: *Meus maior est quam tuus.* Grandpa: *Maior, sed nōn melior! Meus multō melior est.* Name the forms. | ***Maior***: comparative of *magnus*, "bigger." ***Melior***: comparative of *bonus*, "better." ***Multō***: "much," the "by how much" chunk. *Meus* and *tuus* stand alone for "my cheese" and "your cheese." |
| Marcus: *Melle dulcior!* Quīntus: *Crustula meliōra sunt quam cāseī.* Which way does each say "than"? | Marcus: the **ablative** *melle*, no *quam*. Quīntus: ***quam*** + the same case (*crustula* and *cāseī*, both nominative). *Meliōra* is neuter plural for *crustula*. |
| *Cāseus meus fortissimus omnium est.* Why *omnium*? | Genitive plural of *omnis*: "**of all**." The Law of the Part turns the superlative into "the strongest of all." |
| Grandpa: *Optimus Maximus!* Grandma: *…et stomachus maximus!* What's the joke? | Grandpa crowns himself with Jupiter's title, "Best and Greatest." Grandma agrees about "greatest," but only about his **stomach**. |
| Grandma: *Canis tē sapientior est.* How else could she say it? | ***Canis sapientior est quam tū.*** *Tē* is the ablative of *tū* (Chapter 21), the "than" ablative: "wiser than you." |
| Who won the contest in the end? | The dog ate the **biggest** cheese (John's: *in stomachō… Cāseī*), so Grandpa's "best" cheese ends up in the biggest stomach of all, **his own**. |

## Vocābula: Words of this chapter

:: Verba plūra, verba meliōra. || More words, better words. (teaching line)

New forms:

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| *-ior, -ius* | the comparative ending: "more …," "…er" (m./f., n.) |
| *altior, altius (altiōris)* | higher, taller, deeper (a comparative, declined like *rēx* and *corpus*) |
| *-issimus, -issima, -issimum* | the superlative ending: "most …," "very …" |
| *-errimus* | the superlative of *-er* adjectives: *ācerrimus, celerrimus, pulcherrimus* |
| *-illimus* | the superlative of six *-ilis* adjectives: *facillimus, difficillimus, simillimus* |
| *bonus, melior, optimus* | good, better, best |
| *malus, peior, pessimus* | bad, worse, worst |
| *magnus, maior, maximus* | big, bigger, biggest |
| *parvus, minor, minimus* | small, smaller, smallest |
| *multus, plūs, plūrimus* | much, more, most |
| *plūs, plūris* (n.) | more, a larger amount (+ genitive: *plūs cāseī*) |
| *plūrēs, plūra* | more, several (plural adjective: *plūrēs cāseī*) |

New words:

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| *quam* (after a comparative) | than |
| *altus, alta, altum* | high, tall, deep |
| *pulcher, pulchra, pulchrum* | beautiful, handsome |
| *clārus, clāra, clārum* | clear, bright; famous |
| *difficilis, difficile* | hard, difficult |
| *similis, simile* | like, similar |
| *multō maior* | much bigger (chunk; Chapter 35) |
| *positive* | the plain form: *altus*, "tall" |
| *comparative* | the "more" form: *altior*, "taller" |
| *superlative* | the "most / very" form: *altissimus*, "tallest, very tall" |
| *ablative of comparison* | the "than" ablative, no *quam*: *melle dulcior*, "sweeter than honey" |

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīc, et dīc melius! || Say it, and say it better! (teaching line)

1. Chant *altus, altior, altissimus*, then decline *altior* (m./f. and n.) in all cases.
2. Chant the five rebels: *bonus, melior, optimus · malus, peior, pessimus · magnus, maior, maximus · parvus, minor, minimus · multus, plūs, plūrimus*.
3. Say the four Laws in your own words: the Comparative, *Quam* and the Ablative, the Superlative, the Five Rebels.
4. Compare your family: "X is taller than Y" three times with *quam*, then the same three with the ablative.
5. Say who is "the best / the oldest / the wisest of all" in your house (*… omnium*).
6. Ask for more: "More cheese, please! More cookies! More apples!" (*Plūs cāseī, quaesō! Plūra crustula! Plūra māla!*)
7. The real lines: *Exēgī monumentum aere perennius · melle dulcior · Lūce sunt clāriōra nōbīs tua cōnsilia omnia · Hōrum omnium fortissimī sunt Belgae · maior Iōanne Baptistā · maior autem hōrum est cāritās · Ē plūribus ūnum.*
8. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part (the dog too). Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
