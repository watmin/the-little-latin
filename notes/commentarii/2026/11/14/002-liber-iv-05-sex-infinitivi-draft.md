# Caput XLI: Sex Īnfīnītīvī

*Liber IV, Verba Agentia · Chapter 41*

*In which "to" learns to tell time. Since Chapter 6 you've had one infinitive, the "to do it" form:* laudāre, *"to praise." Chapter 30 gave it a passive twin,* laudārī, *"to be praised." But Latin has six, and you already own the pieces for the other four: Chapter 25's keys, Chapter 31's* laudātus est, *Chapter 39's* laudātūrus. *"To have praised." "To have been praised." "To be about to praise." Today they line up in one table, and they finally pay off two old promises: Chapter 30's "it seems" and Chapter 32's "the story goes." Along the way: Grandpa's reputation all over town, a goddess who loved Carthage, old Cato warmed by wine, the Cato who would rather be good than look good, a state motto, and why to err is human. Speaking goal: say what someone seems to be doing, seems to have done, and seems about to do, and what they're said to have done.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Avus dormīre vidētur · dormīvisse vidētur · dormītūrus esse vidētur* · *Avus cāseum ēdisse dīcitur* · *Esse quam vidērī* · *Errāre hūmānum est*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** infinitive (recalled: the "to" form) · perfect active infinitive (the "to have ___ed" form) · perfect passive infinitive (the "to have been ___ed" form) · future active infinitive (the "to be about to" form)
    - **Forms:** *laudāre, laudārī* (Chapters 6, 30) · *laudāvisse* · *laudātus esse* · *laudātūrus esse* · *laudātum īrī* (recognize only) · *fuisse, īsse, tulisse, dedisse, potuisse* · *fore*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Six · the Law of the Borrowed Clock · the Law of the Said and the Seen
    - **Words:** *fore, fertur, dīcitur, errō, hūmānus, colō, vīcīnus, fāma, esse quam vidērī*
    - **Reading:** *Fāma Volat* (Grandpa's reputation reaches the cheese shop; the new cheese vanishes overnight, and everyone seems innocent)
    - **Speaking:** say what someone seems to be doing, to have done, and to be about to do; say what someone is said to have done

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep three old friends close: Chapter 25's four keys (*laudō, laudāre, laudāvī, laudātum*), Chapter 31's *laudātus est*, and Chapter 39's *laudātūrus sum*.

## 1. Six ways to say "to"

:: Sex īnfīnītīvī, ūnum verbum. || Six infinitives, one verb. (teaching line)

Laudāre. Laudārī. Et quattuor aliī.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 6 named the "to do it" form. What's it called, and why? | The **infinitive**, from *īnfīnītus*, "unlimited, unbounded": it doesn't say **who** does it. No person, no number. *Laudāre* is just "to praise." |
| Chapter 30 gave you a second one. *Laudāre* and *laudārī*: what's the difference? | ***Laudāre***: "to praise" (active). ***Laudārī***: "to **be** praised" (passive). Same time: both are **present** infinitives. |
| The title: *Sex Īnfīnītīvī.* (*Sex*: Chapter 22.) Why six? | **Three times × two voices.** Present, perfect, future (the same three as Chapters 37–39's participles), each active and passive: 3 × 2 = 6. |
| In English: "to praise," "to be praised," "to have praised," "to have been praised," "to be about to praise," "to be about to be praised." Which two do you already know in Latin? | The first two: ***laudāre*** and ***laudārī***. Four to go. |
| Where will the other four come from? | From the **keys** you already have: the third key (*laudāvī*) for one, the fourth key (*laudātum*) for the other three. Only three little pieces are new: *-isse*, *esse* after a participle, and *īrī*. |

## 2. The present pair

:: Amāre et amārī dulce mihi erat. || To love and to be loved was sweet to me. (Augustine, *Confessions* 3.1.1; Chapter 30)

Laudāre, laudārī. Legere, legī.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Warm-up from Chapter 30. Active and passive: *habēre*, *audīre*. | ***Habēre, habērī*** · ***audīre, audīrī***. Final *-e* becomes long *-ī*. |
| And the short ones: *legere*, *capere*, *edere*. | ***Legī, capī, edī***: just *-ī* on the stem. *Cāseus edī nōn vult!* (Chapter 30.) |
| The rule-breakers' present infinitives: *sum, possum, volō, nōlō, mālō, ferō, eō, fīō*? | ***Esse, posse, velle, nōlle, mālle, ferre, īre, fierī*** (Chapters 18 and 32). |
| Which of those have a passive infinitive? | ***Ferrī***, "to be carried" (Chapter 32). *Esse, posse, velle, nōlle, mālle* have none. And *fierī* already works as the passive of *facere*: "to be made, to become." |
| Chapter 30's trap: *legī* vs *lēgī*. | ***Legī*** (short *e*): "to be read," present passive infinitive. ***Lēgī*** (long *ē*): "I read, I have read," the perfect. Keep both in mind: the next section uses *lēg-*. |

## 3. To have done it: *-isse*

:: Vēnisse, vīdisse, vīcisse. || To have come, to have seen, to have conquered. (teaching line, after Chapter 25's Caesar)

Laudāvī → laudāvisse.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 25's third key: *laudāvī*, "I praised." Drop the *-ī*, add ***-isse***. | ***Laudāvisse***: "**to have praised**." The perfect stem (Chapter 25) + *-isse*. |
| Grammar books call it the **perfect active infinitive**, or just the "perfect infinitive." A plain-words name? | "The **'to have ___ed' form**." Perfect = done before; active = the subject does it. |
| Same trick on *habuī, lēgī, audīvī, cēpī, ēdī*. | ***Habuisse, lēgisse, audīvisse, cēpisse, ēdisse.*** "To have had, read, heard, taken, eaten." *Lēgisse* and *ēdisse* keep the perfect's long *ē*. |
| Does the family matter? | **No.** Like Chapter 39's *-ūrus*, *-isse* goes on any third key. Find the key, and the rest is automatic. |
| The rule-breakers' third keys: *fuī, tulī, dedī, potuī, voluī.* | ***Fuisse, tulisse, dedisse, potuisse, voluisse.*** "To have been, carried, given, been able, wanted." Even the wild verbs are tame in the perfect (Chapter 25). |
| *Eō*'s third key is *iī* (or *īvī*). So "to have gone"? | ***Iisse***, usually squeezed to ***īsse*** (one long *ī*); *īvisse* also occurs. *Abīsse, redīsse, exīsse*: "to have gone away, come back, gone out." |
| Poets shorten *-āvisse* and *-īvisse* too: *amāsse, audīsse.* Do you need to make these? | **No, just recognize them**: *amāsse* = *amāvisse*, *audīsse* = *audīvisse*. Like the short *amāstī* for *amāvistī*. |

## 4. To have been done: two pieces

:: Cāseus ēsus esse vidētur. || The cheese seems to have been eaten. (teaching line)

Laudātus est → laudātus esse.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 31: *laudātus est*, "he has been praised" (two pieces). Swap *est* for the infinitive *esse*. | ***Laudātus esse***: "**to have been praised**." The perfect passive infinitive: fourth key as a participle + *esse*. |
| Plain-words name for *laudātus esse*? | "The **'to have been ___ed' form**." Perfect (before) + passive (it happened to the subject). |
| Chapter 31's Law of the Two Pieces said the participle agrees. Does it still? Cheese, box, wings: *cāseus ēsus…, capsa aperta…, ālae factae…* | **Yes**: ***cāseus ēsus esse*** · ***capsa aperta esse*** · ***ālae factae esse***. The participle agrees with whatever "has been" done to. *Esse* never changes. |
| *Laudātus est* or *laudātus esse*: which is a whole sentence? | ***Laudātus est***: "He has been praised." A full statement with a person (*est*). *Laudātus esse* is only "to have been praised": it needs a main verb. |
| *Factus esse.* Two meanings, from Chapter 32's *fīō, fierī, factus sum*? | "To have been **made**" or "to have **become**." *Fīō* uses *factus* for its perfect. |

## 5. About to: *-ūrus esse*

:: Quid factūrus esse vidēris? || What do you seem to be about to do? (teaching line)

Laudātūrus sum → laudātūrus esse.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 39: *laudātūrus sum*, "I am about to praise." Swap *sum* for *esse*. | ***Laudātūrus esse***: "**to be about to praise**," "to be going to praise." |
| Grammar books call it the **future active infinitive** (or just "future infinitive"). Plain words? | "The **'to be about to' form**." Future = after the main verb; active = the subject does it. |
| Does *-ūrus* still agree? Grandma, about to sing; the kids, about to play. | **Yes**: ***Avia cantātūra esse…*** · ***līberī lūsūrī esse…*** Declined like *magnus* (Chapter 39). |
| Chapter 39's *futūrus*. "To be about to be"? | ***Futūrus esse***, or one short word: ***fore*** (FO-re). *Fore* never changes for gender or number. |
| *Itūrus esse, ēsūrus esse, factūrus esse.* And for *possum*? | "To be about to go, eat, do." *Possum* has **no** future participle (Chapter 39), so no future infinitive either. |

## 6. The rare sixth

:: Rāra avis. || A rare bird. (Juvenal 6.165; Chapter 15)

Laudātum īrī.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The sixth one, "to be about to be praised": ***laudātum īrī***. Where have you seen *īrī*'s cousin? | Chapter 32's ***Ītur***, "there's a going": *eō* in the passive. ***Īrī*** is *eō*'s present passive infinitive, "to be gone." Literally *laudātum īrī* is "there's a going to praise." |
| Does *laudātum* agree like the other participles? | **No.** It's a frozen form of the fourth key that **never changes** (grammar books name it later). *Laudātum īrī* is the same for anyone or anything. |
| How often will you meet it? | **Rarely.** Even Romans avoided it. Recognize it; you won't need to make it. |
| So the full set for *laudō*, active and passive? | Present ***laudāre / laudārī*** · perfect ***laudāvisse / laudātus esse*** · future ***laudātūrus esse / laudātum īrī***. |

!!! success "The Law of the Six"
    Every verb has **six infinitives**: three times × two voices. You build them from the keys you already know:

    | | active | passive |
    |---|---|---|
    | **present** | *laudāre* (key 2) | *laudārī* (Chapter 30) |
    | **perfect** | *laudāvisse* (key 3 stem + *-isse*) | *laudātus esse* (key 4 + *esse*) |
    | **future** | *laudātūrus esse* (Chapter 39 + *esse*) | *laudātum īrī* (rare; never changes) |

    The **one-word** infinitives never change. The **two-piece** ones with *-us* agree like *magnus*. *Futūrus esse* also has a short form, ***fore***.

## 7. Before, same, after

:: Avus dormīvisse vidētur. || Grandpa seems to have slept. (teaching line)

Dormīre, dormīvisse, dormītūrus esse.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 30's *videor*, "seem." *Avus dormīre vidētur.* | "Grandpa **seems to be sleeping**." The sleeping and the seeming happen **at the same time**. |
| *Avus dormīvisse vidētur.* | "Grandpa **seems to have slept**." The sleeping came **before** the seeming. |
| *Avus dormītūrus esse vidētur.* | "Grandpa **seems about to sleep**." The sleeping comes **after**. |
| Now push the main verb into the past: *Avus dormīre vidēbātur.* | "Grandpa **seemed to be sleeping**." Still the same time, but now the same time as the **past** seeming. |
| *Avus dormīvisse vidēbātur · dormītūrus esse vidēbātur.* | "Grandpa **seemed to have slept**" (before the seeming) · "Grandpa **seemed about to sleep**" (after it). |
| Where have you seen this before? | **Chapter 39's Three Times**: *laudātus* before, *laudāns* same time, *laudātūrus* after. Infinitives tell time the same way: not by the clock, but **compared with the main verb**. |

!!! success "The Law of the Borrowed Clock"
    An infinitive's "time" is **relative to the main verb**: it borrows the main verb's clock.

    | infinitive | time | *vidētur* (seems) | *vidēbātur* (seemed) |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | present *dormīre* | **same time** | seems to be sleeping | seemed to be sleeping |
    | perfect *dormīvisse* | **before** | seems to have slept | seemed to have slept |
    | future *dormītūrus esse* | **after** | seems about to sleep | seemed about to sleep |

## 8. It seems

:: Possunt, quia posse videntur. || They can, because they seem to be able. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 5.231; Chapters 18, 30)

Vidēris fessus esse.

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 30's chunk: *Vidēris fessus, Ave.* The full version has an infinitive. Where? | ***Vidēris fessus esse***: "You seem **to be** tired." Latin often leaves the *esse* out. *Fessus* agrees with "you," the subject. |
| *Cāseus ēsus esse vidētur.* Why is *ēsus* nominative? | It agrees with the **subject**, *cāseus*. "The cheese seems to have been eaten." Two-piece infinitives agree with whoever the sentence is about. |
| "Grandma seems about to sing." | ***Avia cantātūra esse vidētur.*** Feminine *-a* for Grandma. |
| Chapter 30's other chunk, *Mihi vidētur…*, "it seems to me." Add a subject and an infinitive: "Grandpa seems to me to be sleeping." | ***Avus mihi dormīre vidētur.*** "Grandpa seems to me to be asleep": that is, "**I think** Grandpa is sleeping." A polite way to give an opinion. |
| "I seem to have seen the cheese" (I think I saw it). | ***Cāseum vīdisse videor.*** *Videor*, "I seem," + the perfect infinitive *vīdisse*. |
| The motto, Chapter 18's line, read once more: *Possunt, quia posse videntur.* Who is the subject of *videntur*, and what's *posse*? | The same "they" as *possunt* (Aeneas's racing crews). ***Posse*** is a present infinitive after *videntur*: "they seem **to be able**." So they can. |

## 9. The story goes

:: Fāma volat. || Rumor flies. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 3.121)

Avus cāseum ēdisse fertur.

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 32 said *fertur* means "it is said, the story goes," and promised "the full 'he is said to…' with an infinitive: Chapter 41." *Avus cāseum ēdisse fertur.* | "Grandpa **is said to have eaten** the cheese." *Fertur* (he is carried around, talked about) + the perfect infinitive *ēdisse*. |
| *Dīcitur* (from *dīcō*) works the same way. *Canis fūr esse dīcitur.* Why *fūr*, not *fūrem*? | "The dog is said **to be** a thief." *Fūr* is nominative because it describes the subject, *canis*, like *Canis fūr est* (Chapter 13). |
| English likes "**It** is said that Grandpa ate the cheese." What does Latin do with "it"? | Latin makes **Grandpa** the subject: *Avus … dīcitur*, "Grandpa is said…" English can do it too: "Grandpa is said to have eaten it." (English's "they say that…" has a Latin twin with a different case: Chapter 42.) |
| Plural: "The kids are said to have eaten the cheese." | ***Līberī cāseum ēdisse dīcuntur.*** The main verb is plural to match *līberī*. *Ēdisse* never changes. |
| Vergil, *Aeneid* 1.15–16, about Carthage (Chapter 26's *Urbs antīqua fuit*): *quam Iūnō fertur terrīs magis omnibus ūnam / posthabitā coluisse Samō.* (*Colō … coluī*: cherish, tend; *Samos*: Juno's own island; *posthabeō*: rank second.) | "which **Juno is said to have cherished** more than all lands, alone, with **Samos ranked second**." *Fertur* + *coluisse* (perfect active infinitive), and Chapter 40's ablative absolute *posthabitā Samō*. |
| Horace, *Odes* 3.21.11–12, to a wine jar: *narrātur et prīscī Catōnis / saepe merō caluisse virtūs.* (*Narrō*: tell, Chapter 26; *prīscus*: of old; *merum*: unmixed wine; *caleō … caluī*: be warm.) | "**Even old Cato's virtue is said** often **to have grown warm** with wine." *Narrātur* works just like *fertur*. (The jar, by the way, was "born with me in Manlius's consulship": *Ō nāta mēcum cōnsule Mānliō*, 3.21.1. Chapter 40!) |

!!! success "The Law of the Said and the Seen"
    With ***vidētur*** (seems), ***dīcitur*** and ***fertur*** (is said), Latin makes the **person** the subject, in the nominative, and adds an infinitive:

    | Latin | English |
    |---|---|
    | *Avus dormīre vidētur.* | Grandpa seems to be sleeping. |
    | *Avus cāseum ēdisse dīcitur.* | Grandpa is said to have eaten the cheese. |
    | *Cāseus ēsus esse vidētur.* | The cheese seems to have been eaten. |

    Any participle piece (*ēsus, cantātūra*) and any describing noun or adjective (*fūr, fessus*) agrees with that **nominative** subject.

## 10. "To err" as a subject

:: Errāre hūmānum est. || To err is human. (a later proverb; Cicero says it first, *Philippics* 12.5)

Errāre est…

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 32's motto, seen again in Chapters 33 and 35: *Errāre hūmānum est.* (*Errō*: err, wander; *hūmānus*: human.) What's the subject of *est*? | ***Errāre***: "**To err** is human." An infinitive can be a subject, like a noun: "erring." |
| Why *hūmānum*, neuter? | An infinitive counts as a **neuter singular** noun. So the adjective is neuter: *hūmānum*, like Chapter 20's *Dulce et decōrum est prō patriā morī* and Chapter 38's *Cantāre iūcundum est*. |
| The proverb's source, Cicero, *Philippics* 12.5: *Cuiusvīs hominis est errāre, nūllīus nisi īnsipientis in errōre persevērāre.* (*Cuiusvīs*: of anyone at all; *īnsipiēns*: fool; *persevērō*: persist.) | "**To err** is (the way) of any human being; **to persist** in error is (the way) of no one but a fool." Two infinitives as subjects. The famous short form came much later. |
| Chapter 16's motto: *Forsan et haec ōlim meminisse iuvābit.* What looks familiar now in *meminisse*? | The ***-isse***! It looks like a perfect infinitive, and it is one, but *meminī* means "I remember" (a verb with only perfect forms, Chapter 46). *Meminisse* is the **subject** of *iuvābit*: "Maybe someday **to remember** even this will be a pleasure." |
| Can a perfect infinitive be a subject too? "To have eaten the cheese was sweet." | ***Cāseum ēdisse dulce erat.*** Yes. Grandpa would agree. |

## 11. To be, not to seem

:: Esse quam vidērī. || To be, rather than to seem. (North Carolina's state motto, after Sallust)

Esse an vidērī?

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Sallust, *Catiline* 54.6, on Cato (the younger one, Caesar's rival): *esse quam vidērī bonus mālēbat.* (*Mālō … quam*: Chapter 32.) | "He preferred **to be** good **rather than to seem** good." *Esse* active, *vidērī* passive ("to be seen," so "to seem," Chapter 30). |
| Why is *bonus* nominative? | It describes the **subject** of *mālēbat*, Cato himself (as in *Vidēris fessus*). Chapter 18's complementary infinitive keeps the subject in the nominative. |
| North Carolina has *Esse quam vidērī* as its state motto. Which word is missing? | The adjective (*bonus*): "To be (good) rather than to seem (good)." The infinitives carry the idea. |
| Chapter 18's *possum, volō, nōlō, mālō* take which infinitive, almost always? | The **present**: *Possum legere · Volō laudārī · Mālō esse quam vidērī.* "Can," "want," "prefer" look ahead from now, so the present does the job. |
| *Avus laudāre vult* vs *Avus laudārī vult.* | "Grandpa wants **to praise**" (someone else) vs "Grandpa wants **to be praised**" (Chapter 30). Which sounds more like Grandpa? |

## 12. Look-alikes and traps

:: Nōn "esse," sed "ēdisse." || Not "to be," but "to have eaten." (teaching line)

Laudāvisse? Laudāvistī?

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Laudāvisse* · *laudāvistī* · *laudāvistis.* | ***Laudāvisse***: "to have praised" (infinitive). ***Laudāvistī***: "you praised." ***Laudāvistis***: "y'all praised" (Chapter 25). Watch the ending after *-vis-*. |
| *Esse* · *ēdisse.* | ***Esse***: "to be." ***Ēdisse***: "to have eaten" (*ēdī* + *-isse*). The long *ē* and the *-d-* tell you it's *edō*. |
| *Laudātūrus est* · *laudātūrus esse.* | ***Laudātūrus est***: "he is about to praise" (Chapter 39, a whole sentence). ***Laudātūrus esse***: "to be about to praise" (needs a main verb). |
| *Legī* · *lēgī* · *lēgisse.* | ***Legī***: "to be read." ***Lēgī***: "I read." ***Lēgisse***: "to have read." One macron, three meanings. |
| "The cheese seems to have been eaten": *Cāseus ēsum esse vidētur*? | ***Cāseus ēsus esse vidētur.*** The participle agrees with the subject: *ēsus*. (Only the rare *laudātum īrī* never changes.) |

## 13. Fix it

:: Errāre hūmānum est; corrigere optimum. || To err is human; to correct is best. (teaching line; Chapter 33)

Laudāvesse? Laudāvisse.

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "To have praised": *laudāvesse.* | ***Laudāvisse.*** The ending is ***-isse***, on the perfect stem *laudāv-*. |
| "Grandma seems about to sing": *Avia cantātūrus esse vidētur.* | ***Avia cantātūra esse vidētur.*** Feminine, to agree with *Avia*. |
| "Grandpa is said to have eaten the cheese": *Avus cāseum edere dīcitur.* | ***Avus cāseum ēdisse dīcitur.*** *Edere* is "to be eating" (same time); "to have eaten" needs the perfect, *ēdisse*. |
| "The dog is said to be a thief": *Canis fūrem esse dīcitur.* | ***Canis fūr esse dīcitur.*** *Fūr* describes the subject, so it's nominative. |
| "To have read": *legisse.* | ***Lēgisse.*** The perfect's long *ē* stays (*lēgī*). |

## 14. Lectiō: *Fāma Volat*

:: Esse quam vidērī bona mālō. || I'd rather be good than seem good. (Grandma, in today's reading, after Sallust)

Avus cāseum omnium ēdisse dīcitur!

Diē nōnā: the morning after Chapter 40, when the dog walked into the pillow city and ate the dinner cheese. John promised a new one "tomorrow, God willing." Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. As you read, hunt the infinitives and ask: before, same time, or after? New in the story: *vīcīnus*, neighbor; *fāma*, rumor, reputation; *armārium*, cupboard (Chapter 36); *ēsuriō*, be hungry (Chapter 39); *mīca*, crumb (Chapter 38).

::: dialogue lectio
Iōannēs | Ecce cāseus novus, ā mē emptus! || John | Look, a new cheese, bought by me!
Marcus | Sed tabernārius eum nōbīs vendere nōlēbat. || Marcus | But the shopkeeper didn't want to sell it to us.
Avia | Quā dē causā? || Grandma | Why?
Marcus | Avus noster in tōtā urbe cāseum omnium ēdisse dīcitur! || Marcus | Our grandpa is said to have eaten everyone's cheese in the whole town!
Iōannēs | Et canis noster fūr esse dīcitur. Fāma volat! || John | And our dog is said to be a thief. Rumor flies!
Avus | Ego? Ego bonus esse videor! || Grandpa | Me? I seem good!
Avia | Vidērī? Esse quam vidērī bona mālō, Ave. Et tū? || Grandma | Seem? I'd rather be good than seem good, Grandpa. And you?
Avus | Mē puerō, cāseum vīcīnī ēdisse feror… sed nunc bonus sum! || Grandpa | When I was a boy, I'm said to have eaten a neighbor's cheese… but now I'm good!
Cornēlia | Ave, cāseum ēsūrus esse vidēris. Oculī tuī… || Cornelia | Grandpa, you seem about to eat the cheese. Your eyes…
Avus | Minimē! Dormītūrus sum. || Grandpa | Not at all! I'm about to go to sleep.
Iōannēs | Cāseum in armāriō altissimō pōnō. Ibi tūtus esse vidētur. || John | I put the cheese in the highest cupboard. It seems safe there.
Iōannēs | Nox venit. Omnēs dormīre videntur. || John | Night comes. Everyone seems to be asleep.
Iōannēs | Māne capsa vacua est! Cāseus ēsus esse vidētur! || John | In the morning the box is empty! The cheese seems to have been eaten!
Omnēs | Ave! || Everyone | Grandpa!
Avus | Nōn ego! Nocte dormiēbam! || Grandpa | Not me! I was sleeping at night!
Avia | Vērum dīcit. Avus semper stertēbat; eum audiēbam. || Grandma | He's telling the truth. Grandpa was snoring the whole time; I could hear him.
Marcus | Canis ergō! Canis fūr esse dīcitur! || Marcus | The dog, then! The dog is said to be a thief!
Cornēlia | Nōn canis. Canis in hortō dormīvisse vidētur: pedēs eius sordidī sunt, et nāsus sine mīcīs. || Cornelia | Not the dog. The dog seems to have slept in the garden: his paws are dirty, and his nose has no crumbs.
Quīntus | Pater ergō! Pater semper cantāre vult… et edere! || Quīntus | Dad, then! Dad always wants to sing… and eat!
Pater | Ego? Cantātūrus eram, nōn ēsūrus! || Dad | Me? I was going to sing, not eat!
Omnēs | Nōlī, Pater! || Everyone | Don't, Dad!
Iōannēs | Quis ergō? Nēmō cāseum ēdisse vidētur… sed cāseus ēsus est. || John | Who, then? Nobody seems to have eaten the cheese… but the cheese has been eaten.
Māter | Ego… ego cāseum ēdisse videor. || Mom | I… I seem to have eaten the cheese.
Omnēs | Māter?! || Everyone | Mom?!
Māter | Nocte ēsuriēbam. Ūnam partem ēsūra eram… deinde aliam… deinde tōtum. || Mom | I was hungry in the night. I was going to eat one piece… then another… then the whole thing.
Omnēs | In stomachō… || Everyone | In the stomach of…
Avia | …Mātris! || Grandma | …Mom!
Avus | Tandem! Ego innocēns esse videor… et sum! || Grandpa | At last! I seem innocent… and I am!
Māter | Semper bona esse vidēbar. Errāre hūmānum est! Crās cāseum novum emam. || Mom | I always seemed good. To err is human! Tomorrow I'll buy a new cheese.
Iōannēs | Et nunc Māter in tōtā urbe cāseum ēdisse dīcētur! || John | And now Mom will be said, all over town, to have eaten the cheese!
Pater | Hōc cognitō, cantābō! || Dad | Now that we know this, I'll sing!
Omnēs | Nōlī, Pater! || Everyone | Don't, Dad!

{#g-14}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Marcus: *Avus … cāseum omnium ēdisse dīcitur.* Which infinitive, and when did the eating happen? | ***Ēdisse***, perfect active: **before** the saying. "Grandpa is said **to have eaten** everyone's cheese." |
| Grandma: *Esse quam vidērī bona mālō.* Whose line is she borrowing, and why *bona*? | **Sallust's**, about Cato (*esse quam vidērī bonus mālēbat*). *Bona* is feminine because it describes Grandma, the subject of *mālō*. |
| Cornelia: *cāseum ēsūrus esse vidēris.* What time, and why *ēsūrus*? | **After**: "you seem **about to eat**." *Ēsūrus* is masculine nominative, agreeing with "you," Grandpa. |
| *Cāseus ēsus esse vidētur* vs *cāseus ēsus est.* What's the difference in John's two lines? | *Ēsus esse vidētur*: it **seems** to have been eaten (we don't know yet). *Ēsus est*: it **has been** eaten (a fact). The infinitive hedges; *est* states. |
| Cornelia: *Canis in hortō dormīvisse vidētur.* Before, same, or after? | **Before**: "The dog **seems to have slept** in the garden," all night, before the seeming this morning. Dirty paws, no crumbs. |
| Mom: *Ego cāseum ēdisse videor.* Why is that funny? | She doesn't say "I ate it" (*ēdī*). She says "I **seem** to have eaten it": a hedged confession. Then she admits it all. *In stomachō… Mātris!*: for the first time, Mom. |

## Vocābula: Words of this chapter

New forms:

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| *laudāvisse* | to have praised (perfect active infinitive: perfect stem + *-isse*) |
| *laudātus esse* | to have been praised (perfect passive infinitive: participle + *esse*; the participle agrees) |
| *laudātūrus esse* | to be about to praise (future active infinitive: *-ūrus* + *esse*; agrees) |
| *laudātum īrī* | to be about to be praised (future passive infinitive; rare; never changes) |

New words:

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| *fore* | = *futūrus esse*, to be about to be, to be going to be (never changes) |
| *fertur* | is said, the story goes; + infinitive: *Avus cāseum ēdisse fertur*, Grandpa is said to have eaten the cheese |
| *dīcitur* | is said; + infinitive: *Canis fūr esse dīcitur*, the dog is said to be a thief |
| *errō, errāre, errāvī, errātum* | make a mistake, err; wander |
| *hūmānus, hūmāna, hūmānum* | human; *Errāre hūmānum est*, to err is human |

| *fāma, fāmae* | f., rumor, report, reputation; *Fāma volat*, rumor flies |
| *esse quam vidērī* | to be rather than to seem (after Sallust) |
| *perfect active infinitive* | the "to have ___ed" form: *laudāvisse* |

## Speak it aloud

:: Librō clausō, dīcere vidēberis. || Book closed, you'll seem to be speaking. (teaching line)

1. Say all six infinitives of *laudō*, then of *audiō* and *capiō*: present, perfect, future, active then passive.
2. Make the perfect infinitive of ten verbs from their third keys: *amāvī, habuī, lēgī, audīvī, cēpī, ēdī, fuī, tulī, dedī, iī.*
3. The three Laws in your own words: the Six, the Borrowed Clock, the Said and the Seen.
4. Say what three family members **seem** to be doing, to have done, and to be about to do: *Avus dormīre / dormīvisse / dormītūrus esse vidētur.*
5. Say what three people **are said** to have done: *… cāseum ēdisse dīcitur.*
6. Give one opinion with *Mihi vidētur…* and one with *videor*: *Cāseum vīdisse videor.*
7. The real lines: *quam Iūnō fertur … coluisse · narrātur et prīscī Catōnis … caluisse virtūs · esse quam vidērī bonus mālēbat · Cuiusvīs hominis est errāre.*
8. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part. Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
