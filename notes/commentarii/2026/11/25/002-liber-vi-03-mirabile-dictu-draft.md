# Caput LXIII: Mīrābile Dictū

*Liber VI, Ad Auctōrēs · Chapter 63*

*In which the fourth key finally gets its name. Back in Chapter 25 every verb came with four keys, and the fourth (*amātum, dictum, emptum*) was "for later." Chapters 37–39 built participles on it, and Chapter 41's odd* laudātum īrī *was "a frozen form of the fourth key that never changes (grammar books name it later)." Later is now: it's the **supine**, Latin's last verb-noun, and it has only two forms. With* -um *it goes somewhere to do something (*Eō cubitum*, "I'm going to bed"). With* -ū *it sits after an adjective (*mīrābile dictū*, "wonderful to tell"), and it explains Chapter 35's* nātū maior. *The real lines: Caesar's Gauls sending envoys to ask for help, Ovid's theater crowd, Horace and Vergil going off to sleep, Terence losing his audience to the gladiators, Cicero, Sallust, Livy, Vergil's four wonders, and Nepos's Hannibal running home from Zama. At home it's the thirty-first day: Grandpa goes to the forum to buy cheese, as promised, with two guards. Speaking goal: say where you're going and what for, and say what's easy, hard, or wonderful to do, see, and tell.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Mīrābile dictū! · Eō cubitum · Cubitum eāmus! · Avus it cāseum emptum · Spectātum veniunt · facile dictū, difficile factū · Nefās est dictū*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** supine (the "fourth-key verb-noun"; old-school *supīnum*) · supine in *-um* (the "going-to supine": after going, coming, sending; old-school also "accusative supine," "first supine") · supine in *-ū* (the "in-the-___ing supine": after an adjective; old-school also "ablative supine," "second supine")
    - **Forms:** *dictum / dictū, factum / factū, vīsum / vīsū, audītum / audītū* · *cubitum, dormītum, lūsum, spectātum, rogātum, grātulātum* · *laudātum īrī* taken apart
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Supine · the Law of the Going-To · the Law of the Five Ways · the Law of *Īrī* · the Law of the *-ū* · the Law of the Many "To"s
    - **Words:** *mīrābilis* · *incrēdibilis* · *cubō* · *fās, nefās* · *grātulor* · *mercātor* · *supine*
    - **Reading:** *Cāseum emptum* (day thirty-one: Grandpa goes shopping with guards) and Nepos on Hannibal's run from Zama
    - **Speaking:** say where you're going and what for (*Eō … -um*), and what's easy, hard, or wonderful to say, do, see, hear

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. When you meet a word that looks like the fourth key but **agrees with nothing**, ask one question: is there a verb of **going** nearby (then it's *-um*, "to ___"), or an **adjective** (then it's *-ū*, "to ___" in the sense "in the ___ing")?

## 1. *Mīrābile dictū*

:: Mīrābile dictū! || Wonderful to tell! (Vergil, four times; see section 9)

Mīrābile… dictū…

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Today's title: *Mīrābile dictū!* (*Mīrābilis, -e*: wonderful, amazing, from *mīror*, Chapter 62's *Mīranda*; *dīcō, dīcere, dīxī, dictum*: say, tell.) Translate. | "**Wonderful to tell!**" "Amazing to say!" English still uses it, Latin and all, for "believe it or not." |
| *Dictū* looks like one of *dīcō*'s keys. Which one, and what changed? | The **fourth key**, ***dictum***, with ***-ū*** in place of *-um*. |
| Is *dictū* a participle like Chapter 37's *dictus*? Does it agree with *mīrābile*? | **No.** *Mīrābile* is neuter; *dictū* has no gender and **never changes**. It isn't an adjective but a frozen verb-noun: "wonderful **in the telling**." |
| Vergil, *Aeneid* 1.439–440. Aeneas, wrapped in a cloud by his mother Venus, walks into Carthage (Chapter 62's city, still being built): *Īnfert sē saeptus nebulā (mīrābile dictū) / per mediōs, miscetque virīs, neque cernitur ūllī.* (*Īnferō sē*: go in; *saeptus*: wrapped; *nebula*: cloud, mist; *misceō*: mix; *cernō*: see; *ūllus*: anyone.) Translate. | "He goes in, wrapped in a cloud (**wonderful to tell**), through the middle of them, mixes with the men, and is seen **by no one**." *Ūllī*: a dative doer with a passive verb, a poet's version of Chapter 62's *mihi*. |
| Vergil drops *mīrābile dictū* into his lines four times (*Georgics* 2.30; *Aeneid* 1.439, 2.174, 4.182), always as an aside. What does it do there? | It turns to the listener, like English "believe it or not." It isn't part of the sentence's grammar at all: take it out and the line still works. |

## 2. The fourth key gets a name

:: Amō, amāre, amāvī, amātum. || I love, to love, I loved, (the supine). (Chapter 25)

Quārta clāvis… supīnum…

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 25: *amō, amāre, amāvī, amātum.* What did the book say the fourth key was for? | "**You'll need it for passives later**; learn it now as the dictionary's last form." Chapter 31 built *amātus est* on it, Chapter 37 *amātus*, Chapter 39 *amātūrus*. |
| Chapter 41: *laudātum īrī* has "a frozen form of the fourth key that **never changes** (grammar books name it later)." Name it now. | Grammar books call it the **supine**. Plain words: **the fourth-key verb-noun.** Old-school name: *supīnum*, Latin for "lying on its back" (why it got that name is unclear). Many dictionaries print the supine itself as the fourth key: *amātum* **is** the supine. |
| Look at the endings, *-um* and *-ū*. Which noun family in Chapter 23 has accusative *-um* and ablative *-ū*? | **The fourth family**: *manus, manum, manū*. The supine is an old fourth-family verb-noun: ***dictum***, "to the telling," and ***dictū***, "in the telling." |
| Chapter 61's gerund had four cases. How many does the supine have? | **Two**: accusative ***-um*** and ablative ***-ū***. No nominative, no plural, no genders, no agreement. |
| Make the two supines of *faciō, videō, audiō*. (Fourth keys: *factum, vīsum, audītum*.) | ***factum / factū · vīsum / vīsū · audītum / audītū.*** Every verb with a fourth key has the *-um* one. Only a handful of *-ū* ones are in real use (section 8). |

!!! success "The Law of the Supine"
    The **supine** is the fourth key used as a verb-noun, in two cases only:  
    ***-um*** (accusative): *dictum, factum, vīsum*, after a verb of going: "**to** ___."  
    ***-ū*** (ablative, long *ū*): *dictū, factū, vīsū*, after an adjective: "**to** ___," meaning "in the ___ing."  
    It **never agrees** and never changes. The endings are the fourth family's: *manum, manū*.

## 3. Going to do it: the supine in *-um*

:: Eō cubitum. || I'm going to bed. (teaching line)

Quō īs? Cubitum… dormītum…

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 8: *Quō īs? Rōmam eō.* A bare accusative after *eō* is the **goal**. Now: *Avus it cāseum emptum.* (*Emō, emere, ēmī, emptum*: buy.) What's Grandpa's goal? | **The buying.** "Grandpa goes **to buy** cheese." ***Emptum*** is the goal, like *Rōmam*. And it still acts like a verb: ***cāseum*** is its object. |
| Grammar books call this the **supine in *-um*** (also the "accusative supine" or "first supine"). Plain words? | **The going-to supine.** After a verb of motion (*eō, veniō, currō, redeō, mittō*), it says **what you're going (or sending someone) to do**. |
| *Cubō, cubāre, cubuī, cubitum*: lie down. *Dormiō, dormīre, dormīvī, dormītum*: sleep. "I'm going to bed." "Let's go to bed." "I'm going (off) to sleep." | ***Eō cubitum.*** (Word for word, "I go to lie down.") ***Cubitum eāmus!*** (Chapter 49's "let's.") ***Dormītum eō.*** |
| Plautus (died about 184 BC), *Amphitruo* 807. A wife tells her husband about last night: *mēnsa ablāta est, cubitum hinc abiimus.* (*Mēnsa*: table; *auferō, ablātum*: take away; *abeō, abiī*: go off.) Translate. | "The table was cleared, **we went off to bed**." Romans have been saying *cubitum īre* for over two thousand years. |
| Horace, *Satires* 1.5.48, 37 BC. On a trip south with friends, at Capua: *Lūsum it Maecēnās, dormītum ego Vergiliusque.* (*Lūdō, lūsī, lūsum*: play; Maecenas: their rich friend and patron.) Translate. | "**Maecenas goes off to play** (ball), **Vergil and I to sleep**." Two supines, one *it*. Horace's excuse in the next line: ball games are bad for sore eyes and upset stomachs. Yes, that Vergil. |
| "Grandma goes to buy bread." "We're coming to watch." (*Spectō, spectātum*.) "Mom sent Marcus to call Grandpa." | ***Avia it pānem emptum. · Spectātum venīmus. · Māter Marcum mīsit Avum vocātum.*** *Mittō* counts as motion: Marcus is sent somewhere. |

## 4. Envoys, Gauls and Hannibal

:: Lēgātōs ad Caesarem mittunt rogātum auxilium. || They send envoys to Caesar to ask for help. (Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.11.2)

Mittunt… rogātum… grātulātum…

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.11.2, 58 BC. The Helvetians are plundering the land of the Aedui, Rome's allies: *Haeduī, cum sē suaque ab iīs dēfendere nōn possent, lēgātōs ad Caesarem mittunt rogātum auxilium.* (*Sua*: their things; *lēgātus*: envoy; *rogō, rogātum*: ask for; *auxilium*: help.) Translate. | "The Aedui, since they couldn't defend themselves and their property from them, **send envoys to Caesar to ask for help**." *Cum … possent*: Chapter 55's "since." ***Rogātum*** after *mittunt*, with its own object ***auxilium***. |
| Caesar 1.30.1, after the war: *tōtīus ferē Galliae lēgātī, prīncipēs cīvitātum, ad Caesarem grātulātum convēnērunt.* (*Ferē*: almost; *prīnceps*: leading man; *cīvitās*: tribe, state; *grātulor, grātulārī, grātulātus sum*: congratulate.) Translate. A deponent has a supine? | "Envoys from almost all Gaul, the leading men of the tribes, **came together to Caesar to congratulate him**." Yes: deponents have supines, and they're **active** in meaning, like everything else a deponent does except the gerundive (Chapter 62). |
| Nepos, *Hannibal* 6.1. After fifteen years in Italy: *Hinc invictus patriam dēfēnsum revocātus…* (*Invictus*: unbeaten; *patria*: homeland; *dēfendō, dēfēnsum*: defend; *revocō*: call back.) Translate. Where's the motion? | "From here, unbeaten, **called back to defend his homeland**…" The motion is in ***revocātus***: being called home is a journey, so ***dēfēnsum*** says what for, with ***patriam*** as its object. (Section 15 reads on.) |
| Ovid, *Art of Love* 1.99, about 2 BC, on Roman women at the theater: *Spectātum veniunt, veniunt spectentur ut ipsae.* Translate. Which two ways of saying "to" does Ovid use? | "**They come to watch**; they come **to be watched** themselves." First the supine ***spectātum***, then Chapter 51's goal clause ***ut spectentur***. |
| Why didn't Ovid write a supine both times? | The supine is **active**: *spectātum* = "to watch." For "to **be** watched" he needs a passive verb, so he switches to ***ut spectentur***. |
| Cicero, *On the Republic* 6.10, the start of Scipio's famous dream: *Deinde, ut cubitum discessimus, mē … artior, quam solēbat, somnus complexus est.* (*Ut* + indicative: when; *discēdō, discessī*: go off; *artus*: deep; *soleō*: be used to; *complector, complexus*: embrace.) Translate. | "Then, **when we had gone off to bed**, a deeper sleep than usual took hold of me." *Cubitum discēdere*: another going-to. (The "…" skips Scipio telling us how tired he was.) |

!!! success "The Law of the Going-To"
    After a verb of **going, coming, sending, calling** (*eō, veniō, currō, mittō, revocō…*), the supine in ***-um*** says **what for**: *Eō cubitum · Spectātum veniunt · Lēgātōs mittunt rogātum auxilium.*  
    It is still a verb: it takes an **object** in whatever case its verb wants (*auxilium rogātum*; *Iōannī grātulātum*, because *grātulor* takes the dative).  
    It is **active only**. For "to be ___ed," use *ut* + subjunctive.

## 5. Five ways to say "came to buy"

:: Vēnit ut emeret; vēnit emptum. || He came to buy; he came to buy. (teaching line)

Ut… quī… causā… ad… -um…

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Grandpa came to buy cheese." Chapter 51's way, with a goal clause. | ***Avus vēnit ut cāseum emeret.*** Past main verb, so the imperfect subjunctive (Chapter 54's matching rule). |
| Chapter 51's sending *quī*: "Grandma sent Grandpa to buy cheese." | ***Avia Avum mīsit quī cāseum emeret.*** "…who was to buy cheese." |
| Chapters 61 and 62: with *causā*, and with *ad*. | ***Avus vēnit cāseī emendī causā. · Avus vēnit ad cāseum emendum.*** Both with Chapter 62's swap: the gerundive agrees with the cheese. |
| And today's way? | ***Avus vēnit cāseum emptum.*** The shortest of the five. |
| "Grandpa wants to buy cheese": *Avus cāseum emptum vult*? | **No.** *Volō* isn't a verb of going; it takes the infinitive: ***Avus cāseum emere vult.*** *Cāseum emptum vult* would mean "he wants **the bought cheese**," the participle (Chapter 32's Grandpa: *Cāseum emptum mālō!*). |

!!! success "The Law of the Five Ways"
    "Grandpa came **to buy** cheese," five ways, all good Latin:

    | Way | Latin | Chapter |
    |---|---|---|
    | goal clause | *vēnit **ut** cāseum **emeret*** | 51 |
    | sending *quī* | *(Avia Avum mīsit) **quī** cāseum **emeret*** | 51 |
    | *causā* | *vēnit cāseī **emendī causā*** | 61–62 |
    | *ad* | *vēnit **ad** cāseum **emendum*** | 61–62 |
    | supine | *vēnit cāseum **emptum*** | 63 |

    The supine works only after a verb of **motion**.

## 6. *Laudātum īrī* taken apart

:: Rūmor venit datum īrī gladiātōrēs. || A rumor comes that there's going to be a gladiator show. (Terence, *The Mother-in-Law* 39–40)

Laudātum… īrī…

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 41: *laudātum īrī*, "to be about to be praised," word for word "there's a going to praise." Take it apart now. | ***Laudātum***: the going-to supine, "to praise." ***Īrī***: *eō*'s passive infinitive, "to be gone" (Chapter 32's *ītur*, "there's a going"). Together: "**there is a going (off) to praise**," that is, someone is going to praise. |
| So why does *laudātum īrī* never change? "I think the letter will be written." (*Scrībō, scrīptum*.) | ***Putō epistulam scrīptum īrī***, not *scrīptam*. *Scrīptum* is a supine, and supines never agree. |
| Terence, *The Mother-in-Law*, second prologue (lines 39–40), 160 BC. The play had already failed twice: *prīmō āctū placeō; cum intereā rūmor venit / datum īrī gladiātōrēs, populus convolat.* (*Āctus*: act; *placeō*: please, go down well; *intereā*: meanwhile; *rūmor*: rumor; *dō, datum*: give, put on a show; *convolō*: rush together.) Translate. | "**The first act goes down well; then meanwhile a rumor comes that there's going to be a gladiator show** (word for word, 'that there's a going to give gladiators'), and the people rush off." Terence's own spellings *quom* and *populu'* are printed as *cum* and *populus*. |
| *Datum īrī gladiātōrēs*: why isn't it *datōs*? What is *gladiātōrēs* doing? | ***Gladiātōrēs*** is the **object of the supine** *datum*, "to give gladiators," not a subject it agrees with. That's the whole trick of *-um īrī*. It's rare: recognize it, don't make it. |

!!! success "The Law of *Īrī*"
    The future passive infinitive ***laudātum īrī*** = the going-to supine + ***īrī*** ("to be gone"): "**there's a going to praise**."  
    That's why it never agrees: *Putō epistulam **scrīptum** īrī.* Recognize it; you'll rarely need it.

## 7. "In the telling": the supine in *-ū*

:: Prōdigium vīsū ēventūque mīrābile. || An omen wonderful to see and in its outcome. (Livy, *From the Founding of the City* 1.39.1)

Dictū… vīsū… factū…

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Back to *mīrābile dictū*. Grammar books call *dictū* the **supine in *-ū*** (also the "ablative supine" or "second supine"). Plain words? | **The in-the-___ing supine.** After an adjective, it says **in what way** the adjective is true: *mīrābile dictū*, "wonderful **in the telling**," "wonderful **to tell**." |
| Chapter 35's ablative of respect: *nātū maior* (older "by birth"), *pede claudus* (lame "in the foot"). How is *dictū* the same? | It **is** an ablative of respect, of a verb-noun: "wonderful in respect of telling." And Chapter 35's ***nātū***, "an old noun used only in this form," is one of these too: grammar books list it with the *-ū* supines (from *nāscor, nātus*, be born). |
| "Easy to say, hard to do." (*Facilis*: easy; *difficilis*: hard.) | ***Facile dictū, difficile factū.*** Two adjectives, two *-ū* supines. |
| Livy, *From the Founding of the City* 1.39.1, about 27 BC. The palace of King Tarquin, and a sleeping boy named Servius: *Eō tempore in rēgiā prōdigium vīsū ēventūque mīrābile fuit.* (*Rēgia*: palace; *prōdigium*: omen, portent; *ēventus, -ūs*: outcome.) Translate. Which two words have *-ū*? | "At that time there was an omen in the palace, **wonderful to see** and in its outcome." ***Vīsū***: the supine. ***Ēventū***: a plain fourth-family noun in the same "in what respect" ablative. Livy pairs them because they work the same way. (The omen: the boy's head seemed to blaze with fire as he slept. He grew up to be King Servius Tullius.) |
| Cicero, *Philippics* 2.63, 44 BC, mocking Mark Antony, who threw up in public the morning after a wedding feast: *Ō rem nōn modo vīsū foedam, sed etiam audītū!* (*Ō* + accusative: what a…!; *foedus, -a, -um*: disgusting.) Translate. | "What a thing, **disgusting** not only **to see**, but even **to hear about**!" ***Vīsū*** and ***audītū*** both hang on ***foedam***. |
| Can an *-ū* supine take an object? "Easy to say this": *facile dictū hoc*? | **Never.** Make the thing the subject instead: ***Hoc facile dictū est***, "This is easy to say." The *-um* supine takes objects; the *-ū* one never does. |

!!! success "The Law of the *-ū*"
    After an **adjective** (*mīrābilis, facilis, difficilis, incrēdibilis, foedus, optimus…*) or ***fās / nefās***, the supine in ***-ū*** says "**to** ___" in the sense "in the ___ing": *mīrābile dictū · facile factū · foedum vīsū*.  
    It's an **ablative of respect** (Chapter 35), like *nātū maior*. The *ū* is **long**, like *manū*.  
    It **never takes an object**: *Hoc facile dictū est.*

## 8. The few, and *nefās*

:: Nefās esse dictū. || That it is a sin to say. (Cicero, *On Old Age* 13)

Fās… nefās… memorātū…

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 53's Horace: *scīre nefās*, "it's wrong to know." *Fās* and *nefās* (no other forms) mean "right" and "wrong" in the eyes of the gods. Cicero, *On Old Age* 13, 44 BC, old Cato speaking about his hero Fabius Maximus: *Vidētis nefās esse dictū miseram fuisse tālem senectūtem.* (*Senectūs, -ūtis*: old age.) Translate. | "You see that it **is a sin to say** that such an old age was wretched." Chapter 42's reported speech twice over: *nefās esse*, and *senectūtem … fuisse*. ***Dictū*** hangs on ***nefās***. |
| Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations* 5.38, comparing the human mind to God, adds: *sī hoc fās est dictū.* | "**If it is right to say this.**" Cicero's polite "if I may say so." |
| Which *-ū* supines will you really meet? | A short list: ***dictū, factū, vīsū, audītū, memorātū*** (*memorō*: tell, relate), ***inventū*** (*inveniō*: find), and ***nātū***. That's nearly all. Don't invent *emptū* or *ēsū*. |
| Sallust, *Catiline* 7.3, about 42 BC, on early Rome: *Sed cīvitās incrēdibile memorātū est adeptā lībertāte quantum brevī crēverit.* (*Incrēdibilis*: unbelievable; *adipīscor, adeptus*: win; *brevī*: in a short time; *crēscō, crēvī*: grow.) Translate. | "But it's **unbelievable to relate** how much the city grew in a short time once it had won its freedom." ***Quantum … crēverit***: Chapter 53's tucked-in question. ***Adeptā lībertāte***: Chapter 40's untied pair, with a deponent. |
| Sallust, *Catiline* 14.1: Catiline gathers every criminal in Rome around him, *id quod factū facillumum erat.* (*Facillumum*: old spelling of *facillimum*.) Translate. | "(Which was) **the easiest thing to do**." Sallust likes the old *-umus* spelling for *-imus*. |

## 9. Vergil's wonders

:: Mōnstrum horrendum, ingēns. || A monster dreadful, huge. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 4.181; Chapters 20 and 62)

Mīrābile dictū… iterum…

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Vergil, *Aeneid* 4.181–182, on Rumor (*Fāma*): *mōnstrum horrendum, ingēns, cui quot sunt corpore plūmae, / tot vigilēs oculī subter (mīrābile dictū).* (*Quot … tot*: as many … so many; *plūma*: feather; *vigil*: watchful; *subter*: underneath.) Translate. | "A monster dreadful, huge, who has as many watchful eyes underneath as there are feathers on her body (**wonderful to tell**)." *Cui sunt*: Chapter 11's "for whom there are," she has. The gerundive ***horrendum*** (Chapter 62) and the supine ***dictū*** in one sentence. |
| *Aeneid* 2.174–175. The Greeks have stolen Minerva's statue, and the goddess is angry: *terque ipsa solō (mīrābile dictū) / ēmicuit parmamque ferēns hastamque trementem.* (*Ter*: three times; *solum*: ground; *ēmicō, ēmicuī*: leap up; *parma*: shield; *hasta*: spear; *tremō*: quiver.) Translate. | "And three times the goddess herself (**wonderful to tell**) leapt up from the ground, carrying her shield and her quivering spear." |
| *Georgics* 2.30–31, Vergil's farming poem, on olive trees: *quīn et caudicibus sectīs (mīrābile dictū) / trūditur ē siccō rādīx oleāgina lignō.* (*Quīn et*: why, even; *caudex, -icis*: trunk; *secō, sectum*: cut; *trūdō*: push; *siccus*: dry; *rādīx*: root; *oleāginus*: of the olive; *lignum*: wood.) Translate. | "Why, even when the trunks are cut up (**wonderful to tell**), an olive root pushes out of the dry wood." *Caudicibus sectīs*: Chapter 40's untied pair. A farmer's wonder, and a true one: olive wood does sprout. |
| *Aeneid* 3.621, the Cyclops of Chapter 20: *nec vīsū facilis nec dictū adfābilis ūllī.* (*Adfābilis*: easy to talk to; *ūllī*: for anyone.) Translate. | "**Not easy for anyone to look at, nor to speak to.**" Two *-ū* supines, each after its own adjective. |

## 10. The many "to"s

:: Facile dictū, difficile factū. || Easy to say, hard to do. (teaching line)

Legere… legendī… legendus… lēctum…

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| With the supine, you have every verb-noun and verb-adjective Latin makes. Line up *legō, legere, lēgī, lēctum*. | Infinitive ***legere*** (Chapter 6) · gerund ***legendī*** (Chapter 61) · gerundive ***legendus*** (Chapter 62) · supine ***lēctum / lēctū*** (today) · participles ***legēns, lēctus, lēctūrus*** (Chapters 37–39). |
| English "to ___" hides several Latin forms. "I want to sleep." | ***Dormīre volō.*** After *volō*: the **infinitive**. |
| "I'm going to sleep" (off to bed). "The book must be read." | ***Dormītum eō*** (the going-to supine). ***Liber legendus est*** (the must-be gerundive). |
| "Wonderful to see." And Chapter 62's "a thing to be wondered at"? | ***Mīrābile vīsū*** (the *-ū* supine). ***Rēs mīranda*** (the gerundive). |
| With *facilis* and *difficilis*, Romans often used Chapter 61's *ad* + gerund instead of the *-ū*. "Easy to understand"? (*Intellegō*.) | ***Facilis ad intellegendum.*** And Vergil's *nec vīsū facilis* shows the supine. Both are Latin. |

!!! success "The Law of the Many “To”s"
    | English "to ___" | Latin | Example |
    |---|---|---|
    | after *volō, possum, cupiō* | infinitive | *Dormīre volō.* |
    | going **to**, sent **to** | supine *-um* (or the Five Ways) | *Dormītum eō.* |
    | easy / wonderful **to** | supine *-ū* | *Mīrābile dictū.* |
    | must be, **to be** ___ed | gerundive | *Liber legendus est.* |

## 13. Traps and fix-its

:: Errandō discitur. || By erring, one learns. (Chapter 23)

Mūtātur? Nōn mūtātur!

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Grandma goes to buy bread": *Avia it pānem empta.* | ***Avia it pānem emptum.*** The supine **never agrees**: not with Grandma, not with the bread. |
| "The girls come to watch": *Puellae spectātae veniunt.* | ***Puellae spectātum veniunt.*** *Spectātae* is the participle: "the girls, **having been watched**, come." |
| "I want to buy cheese": *Volō cāseum emptum.* | ***Volō cāseum emere.*** No going, no supine. *Volō cāseum emptum* = "I want **the bought cheese**." |
| "Easy to say this": *Facile dictū hoc.* | ***Hoc facile dictū est.*** The *-ū* supine never takes an object. |
| "Easy to say": *facile dictum.* | ***Facile dictū.*** *Dictum* is the going-to form or the participle ("said"); after an adjective you need ***-ū***. |
| "Wonderful to tell": *mīrābile dictu.* | ***Mīrābile dictū.*** The ablative *-ū* is **long**, as in *manū* (Chapter 23). |

## 14. Lectiō: *Cāseum emptum*

:: Cubitum eāmus! || Let's go to bed! (teaching line; Dad)

Ad forum… cāseum emptum…

Diē trīcēsimā prīmā: the day after Chapter 62. Yesterday the dog "destroyed" the cheese, and Grandpa promised to go to the forum to buy a new one. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. Find every supine and say which kind it is. New in passing: *trīcēsimus prīmus*, thirty-first; *togam induō*, put on the toga (Chapter 48); *custōdiō, custōdītum*, guard; *aliī … aliī*, some … others; *theātrum*, theater; *mercātor, -ōris*, merchant, seller; *senex*, old man, sir; *cavē*, careful!; *gustō*, taste; *vix*, hardly; *crēdibilis*, believable; *īnspiciō*, look over; *laudandus* (Chapter 62); *sērō*, late; *iter*, journey; *culīna*, kitchen; *quaerō, quaesītum*, look for.

::: dialogue lectio
Iōannēs | Diē trīcēsimā prīmā Avus māne surgit et togam induit. || John | On the thirty-first day Grandpa gets up early and puts on his toga.
Avia | Quō īs, Ave, tam māne? || Grandma | Where are you going so early, Grandpa?
Avus | Herī prōmīsī: eō ad forum cāseum emptum. || Grandpa | I promised yesterday: I'm going to the forum to buy cheese.
Avia | Cāseum emptum? An cāseum ēsum? || Grandma | To buy cheese? Or to eat cheese?
Avus | Emptum, Avia, emptum! Nōn ēsum. || Grandpa | To buy, Grandma, to buy! Not to eat.
Marcus | Iōannēs et ego tēcum venīmus, tē custōdītum. || Marcus | John and I are coming with you, to guard you.
Quīntus | Et ego veniō spectātum! || Quintus | And I'm coming to watch!
Cornēlia | Ego domī maneō. Mihi epistula scrībenda est. || Cornelia | I'm staying home. I have a letter to write.
Iōannēs | In forō multī hominēs ambulant. Aliī emptum veniunt, aliī spectātum. || John | In the forum lots of people are walking around. Some come to buy, others to watch.
Avus | Ut Ovidius dē theātrō: "Spectātum veniunt, veniunt spectentur ut ipsae." || Grandpa | As Ovid says of the theater: "They come to watch; they come to be watched themselves."
Mercātor | Salvēte! Quid quaeritis? Ecce cāseus maximus, mīrābilis vīsū! || Seller | Hello, y'all! What are you looking for? Look, a huge cheese, wonderful to see!
Avus | Uxor mē mīsit cāseum emptum. Hunc emō. || Grandpa | My wife sent me to buy cheese. I'll buy this one.
Mercātor | Vīsne frūstum gustāre, senex? || Seller | Would you like to taste a piece, sir?
Marcus | Cavē, Ave! Nōs tē custōdītum vēnimus! || Marcus | Careful, Grandpa! We came to guard you!
Avus | Facile dictū, difficile factū… sed nōn gustābō. || Grandpa | Easy to say, hard to do… but I won't taste it.
Iōannēs | Et, mīrābile dictū, Avus nūllum frūstum gustat. Cāseum domum portat. || John | And, wonderful to tell, Grandpa doesn't taste a single piece. He carries the cheese home.
Quīntus | Incrēdibile vīsū! Avus cāseum portat nec edit! || Quintus | Unbelievable to see! Grandpa is carrying cheese and not eating it!
Iōannēs | Domī Avia cāseum īnspicit. Tōtus est. || John | At home Grandma looks the cheese over. It's whole.
Avia | Nihil ēdistī, Ave? Vix crēdibile audītū! || Grandma | You ate nothing, Grandpa? Hardly believable to hear!
Avus | Nefās est dictū, sed gustāre voluī. Nōn tamen gustāvī. || Grandpa | It's a sin to say, but I wanted to taste it. I didn't, though.
Avia | Laudandus es, Ave! Ad cēnam tibi prīmum frūstum dabō. || Grandma | You deserve praise, Grandpa! At dinner I'll give you the first piece.
Avia | Nunc cēnam parātum eō. || Grandma | Now I'm going to get dinner ready.
Iōannēs | Post cēnam, cāseō ēsō, Pater surgit. || John | After dinner, when the cheese has been eaten, Dad gets up.
Pater | Sērō est. Cubitum eāmus! || Dad | It's late. Let's go to bed!
Avus | Ut Horātius in itinere: "Lūsum it Maecēnās, dormītum ego Vergiliusque." || Grandpa | As Horace said on his journey: "Maecenas goes off to play, Vergil and I to sleep."
Marcus | Nōs nōn lūsum īmus? || Marcus | We're not going off to play?
Avia | Nōn! Omnēs dormītum! || Grandma | No! Everyone off to sleep!
Iōannēs | Et canis? Canis in culīnam it… cāseum quaesītum. || John | And the dog? The dog goes into the kitchen… to look for cheese.
Avus | Crās vōbīs nārrābō quid mercātor mihi dīxerit. Mīrābile audītū erit! || Grandpa | Tomorrow I'll tell y'all what the seller said to me. It'll be wonderful to hear!

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Find every going-to supine and the verb of motion it goes with. | ***eō … emptum*** (and Grandma's ***emptum? … ēsum?***, with *īs* understood) · ***custōdītum*** (with *venīmus*, then *vēnimus*) · ***veniō spectātum · emptum veniunt, spectātum*** (*veniunt*) · ***Spectātum veniunt*** (Ovid) · ***mīsit … emptum · parātum eō · Cubitum eāmus · Lūsum it, dormītum*** (Horace) · ***lūsum īmus · dormītum*** (go understood) · ***it … quaesītum***. |
| Find every *-ū* supine and what it hangs on. | ***mīrābilis vīsū · Facile dictū, difficile factū · mīrābile dictū · Incrēdibile vīsū · crēdibile audītū · Nefās est dictū · Mīrābile audītū.*** Adjectives, plus *nefās*. |
| *Cāseum emptum? An cāseum ēsum?* What's Grandma's joke? | Same going-to supine, two different fourth keys: *emō → **emptum*** (to buy) and *edō → **ēsum*** (to eat, Chapter 31). Is he going **to buy** the cheese, or **to eat** it? |
| *Post cēnam, cāseō ēsō…* Supine? | **No.** ***Cāseō ēsō*** is Chapter 40's untied pair: noun + participle, both ablative, "the cheese having been eaten." The participle **agrees** (*ēsō* with *cāseō*); a supine never would. |
| *Crās vōbīs nārrābō quid mercātor mihi dīxerit.* Why *dīxerit*? | Chapter 53's **tucked-in question** with a time before the main verb: perfect subjunctive. What the seller actually said comes tomorrow, in Chapter 64, as reported speech. |

## 15. Auctor: Hannibal runs home

:: Pulsus, incrēdibile dictū… || Beaten, unbelievable to tell… (Nepos, *Hannibal* 6.3)

Zama… Hadrūmētum… incrēdibile…

Grandpa promised more Nepos. Here is *Hannibal* 6.1, 6.3 and 6.4 in Nepos's own words (the Latin Library text, with macrons added; 6.2, on failed peace talks, is left out). In 203 BC Carthage called Hannibal home from Italy; in 202 the young Publius Scipio (later called Africanus) beat him at Zama, in Africa. *Fugārat* is short for *fugāverat* (*fugō*: put to flight). New in passing: *hinc*, from here; *invictus*, unbeaten; *patria*, homeland; *dēfendō, dēfēnsum*, defend; *revocō*, call back; *gerō bellum*, wage war; *adversus* + acc., against; *Rhodanus*, the Rhone; *Padus*, the Po; *Trebia*, a river in north Italy; *factum*, deed, event; *cōnflīgō, cōnflīxī*, clash; *pellō, pulsum*, beat, drive back; *bīduum*, two days; *Hadrūmētum*, a port town in Africa; *absum*, be away; *circiter*, about; *passus*, pace (*mīlia passuum*, miles); *trecentī*, three hundred; *Numidae*, Numidians (African horsemen); *aciēs*, battle line; *excēdō, excessī*, leave; *īnsidior* + dat., lay a trap for; *effugiō, effūgī*, escape; *opprimō, oppressī*, crush.

::: dialogue auctor
Nepōs | Hinc invictus patriam dēfēnsum revocātus || Nepos | From here, unbeaten, called back to defend his homeland,
Nepōs | bellum gessit adversus P. Scīpiōnem, fīlium eius, || Nepos | he waged war against Publius Scipio, the son of the man
Nepōs | quem ipse prīmō apud Rhodanum, iterum apud Padum, tertiō apud Trebiam fugārat. || Nepos | whom he himself had put to flight first at the Rhone, again at the Po, a third time at the Trebia.
Nepōs | Post id factum paucīs diēbus apud Zamam cum eōdem cōnflīxit: || Nepos | A few days after that, he clashed with the same man at Zama:
Nepōs | pulsus – incrēdibile dictū – bīduō et duābus noctibus Hadrūmētum pervēnit, || Nepos | beaten, he reached Hadrumetum (unbelievable to tell) in two days and two nights,
Nepōs | quod abest ab Zamā circiter mīlia passuum trecenta. || Nepos | which is about three hundred miles away from Zama.
Nepōs | In hāc fugā Numidae, quī simul cum eō ex aciē excesserant, īnsidiātī sunt eī; || Nepos | In this flight some Numidians, who had left the battle line together with him, laid a trap for him;
Nepōs | quōs nōn sōlum effūgit, sed etiam ipsōs oppressit. || Nepos | he not only escaped them, but crushed them as well.
:::

{#g-15}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Find the two supines. Which kind is each? | ***dēfēnsum***: going-to, after ***revocātus*** (being called home is motion), with the object ***patriam***. ***incrēdibile dictū***: the *-ū*, Nepos's aside, like Vergil's *mīrābile dictū*. |
| *P. Scīpiōnem, fīlium eius, quem…* Whose son? Whom had Hannibal beaten? | Scipio is the son of ***eius***, "that man," and ***quem*** goes back to the father: the elder Publius Scipio, whom (as Nepos tells it) Hannibal routed three times in his first year in Italy. Now he faced the son. |
| *Hadrūmētum pervēnit*: why no *ad*? | It's a **town**: Chapter 45's bare accusative of the goal, *Rōmam eō*. The same goal accusative the going-to supine comes from. |
| *Post id factum paucīs diēbus*: how many days after what? | "**A few days after** that event" (the failed talks of 6.2). ***Paucīs diēbus***: Chapter 35's "by how much" ablative, like *paulō post*. |
| What's *incrēdibile* about it? | **Three hundred Roman miles in two days and two nights**, on the run, after losing a battle. (Modern estimates of the distance are much shorter; Nepos's number makes the wonder bigger.) And he still had energy to crush an ambush. |

!!! success "Mīrābile dictū: tuum est!"
    You now have every verb-noun Latin owns: the **infinitive** (*legere*), the **gerund** (*legendī*), and the **supine**, the fourth key at work, *-um* after going (*Eō cubitum · Spectātum veniunt*) and *-ū* after an adjective (*mīrābile dictū · facile factū*). With the gerundive and the participles, that's the whole family of verbs that act like nouns and adjectives. Next, Chapter 64 *Ōrātiō Oblīqua*: what happens to *because*, *when* and *who* clauses inside reported speech, so Grandpa can tell you what the cheese seller said.
