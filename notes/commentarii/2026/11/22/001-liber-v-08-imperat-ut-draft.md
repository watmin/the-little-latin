# Caput LVI: Imperat Ut

*Liber V, Modus Subiūnctīvus · Chapter 56*

*In which Latin learns to pass on orders. Chapter 42 taught you to report what someone says ("Marcus says that Grandpa is sleeping"), and Chapter 53 to tuck a question inside a sentence ("I ask what you're doing"). One kind of speech is left: the command. "Come!" becomes "Grandma orders me to come." English uses "to"; Latin uses Chapter 51's "ut" and "nē" with the subjunctive, and Chapter 54's matching rule picks the tense. Chapter 50 taught "Velim mihi scrībās" as a chunk and said that Chapter 56 would explain it. Here it is. Along the way: Cicero ordering Catiline out of Rome and a client asking his partner to keep his word, Caesar's Orgetorix persuading a whole people to leave home, the German king Ariovistus making a demand, Jesus warning his disciples to tell no one, a tempter in the desert, and Cicero telling his sick secretary Tiro to spare no expense. Speaking goal: ask, order, beg, warn and persuade, and report what others asked of you.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Avia mihi imperat ut veniam · Tē rogō ut veniās · Moneō tē nē tangās · Avia mē iubet venīre · Cūrā ut valeās*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** indirect command (the "tucked-in command": an order reported inside a sentence; old-school "jussive noun clause")
    - **Forms:** nothing new to build! Chapter 49's present subjunctive and Chapter 52's imperfect subjunctive, picked by Chapter 54's matching rule.
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Person Asked · the Law of the Tucked-In Command · the Law of *Iubeō* and *Vetō* · the Law of "That" and "To"
    - **Words:** *petō … ab* (ask from) · *postulō* (demand) · *vetō* (forbid) · *suādeō* (advise) · *cūrō* (see to it) · *praecipiō* (instruct)
    - **Reading:** *Parva pars* (day twenty-four: Grandma gives an order about the last piece of cheese, Grandpa asks, begs, orders and persuades everyone in the house, and finally writes a letter)
    - **Speaking:** ask, beg, urge, warn, order and persuade; say what someone asked or ordered you to do

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep Chapter 54's two teams close: every tucked-in command picks its tense by them.

## 1. Three ways to pass it on

:: Imperat ut veniam. || She orders me to come. (teaching line; the chapter title)

Venī!… Avia imperat ut veniam.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Grandma calls: *Venī, Iōannēs!* What kind of verb is *venī*? | The **imperative**, the "command" mood (Chapter 6). Her exact words. |
| Now pass it on: "Grandma orders me to come." (*Imperō* + dative, Chapter 44.) | ***Avia mihi imperat ut veniam.*** *Mihi*: the person given the order, dative. ***Ut veniam***: "that I come," English "**to come**." |
| Which mood is *veniam*, and why? | The **subjunctive** (Chapter 49's *veniam, veniās, veniat*). An order isn't a fact yet: it's what someone **wants** to happen. It's Chapter 49's "let him" subjunctive, tucked inside a sentence. |
| Now you have three ways to pass on what someone said. Chapter 42: *Marcus dīcit Avum dormīre.* Chapter 53: *Rogō quid faciās.* Today: *Avia imperat ut veniam.* Which is which? | A **statement** (accusative + infinitive), a **question** (question word + subjunctive), a **command** (*ut* + subjunctive). |
| Grammar books call today's clause an **indirect command**. Plain words? | **The tucked-in command**, like Chapter 53's tucked-in question: an order reported inside a sentence. Some old school books call it a "jussive noun clause" (*iubeō*, order, as in Chapter 49's jussive). |
| Chapter 51: *Ad forum eō ut cāseum emam.* Today: *Avia mē rogat ut cāseum emam.* Same *ut*, same subjunctive. What's different? | The **job**. Chapter 51's *ut* tells **why** I go: a goal. Today's tells **what** Grandma asks: it's the **thing asked**. Test it: "What does she ask?" "That I buy cheese." |

## 2. Who gets the order?

:: Persuādē eī! || Persuade him! (teaching line; Chapter 44)

Mihi imperat… mē rogat… ā mē petit…

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 44: *imperō* and *persuādeō* take the dative. "I order **you** to sing." | ***Tibi imperō ut cantēs.*** |
| "I persuaded **Grandpa** to sleep." (*Persuādeō, persuāsī*.) | ***Avō persuāsī ut dormīret.*** A story, so the past team: imperfect *dormīret* (Chapter 54). |
| *Rogō* (ask, Chapter 29), *ōrō* (beg, Chapter 49), *hortor* (urge, Chapter 43) and *moneō* (warn, Chapter 36) take the accusative. "I ask **you** to come." | ***Tē rogō ut veniās.*** |
| "Grandpa urges **us** to eat." | ***Avus nōs hortātur ut edāmus.*** A deponent: passive look, active meaning (Chapter 43). |
| "I beg y'all to sing." | ***Vōs ōrō ut cantētis.*** |
| *Petō* ("ask for"; Chapter 34's "chase") and *postulō* ("demand"; Chapter 54's "ask for") ask **from** someone: *ā / ab* + ablative. "I ask Grandma to give me cheese." | ***Ab Aviā petō ut mihi cāseum det.*** Word for word: "I seek **from** Grandma that she give me cheese." |
| So how do you know the case? | By the verb. ***Imperō, persuādeō*** give the order **to** someone (dative). ***Rogō, ōrō, hortor, moneō*** ask, beg, urge or warn someone (accusative). ***Petō, postulō*** seek or demand **from** someone (*ā* + ablative). |

!!! success "The Law of the Person Asked"
    The **person** who gets the order has the case the **verb** wants:

    | Case | Verbs | Example |
    |---|---|---|
    | dative ("to") | *imperō, persuādeō* | *Tibi imperō ut cantēs.* |
    | accusative | *rogō, ōrō, hortor, moneō* | *Tē rogō ut veniās.* |
    | *ā / ab* + ablative ("from") | *petō, postulō* | *Ab Aviā petō ut veniat.* |

## 3. "Not to" and "nobody"

:: Imperō nē quis tangat. || I order that nobody touch it. (teaching line)

Nē… nē quis… nē quid…

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Grandma warns me not to touch the cheese." | ***Avia mē monet nē cāseum tangam.*** ***Nē***: "**not to**." |
| Why not *ut nōn tangam*? | Chapter 52's Law of the Two Nots: a thing **wanted** takes *nē*; *ut nōn* is for **results**. An order is wanted, so: ***nē***. |
| "Grandma orders that nobody touch the cheese." | ***Avia imperat nē quis cāseum tangat.*** Chapter 51's ***nē quis***: after *nē*, *aliquis* drops its *ali-*. |
| "Grandpa begs me not to say anything." (After *nē*, *quid* = "anything.") | ***Avus mē ōrat nē quid dīcam.*** |
| Two orders, one "do" and one "don't": "I order y'all to sit and not to eat." Latin joins them with ***nēve*** ("and not"). | ***Vōbīs imperō ut sedeātis nēve edātis.*** *Nēve* (or *neu*) is the "and not" of orders and goals. |

## 4. Tell no one

:: Comminātus est eīs, nē cui dīcerent dē illō. || He warned them sternly not to tell anyone about him. (Mark 8:30, Nova Vulgata)

Nē cui… nē quid… nē quem…

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Mark 8:29–30 (Nova Vulgata). Peter has just said to Jesus, *Tū es Christus.* Then: *Et comminātus est eīs, nē cui dīcerent dē illō.* (*Comminor*: warn sternly, a deponent, + dative.) Translate. | "And he warned them sternly **not to tell anyone** about him." |
| *Cui*: what's it short for? | ***Alicui***, "to anyone," the dative of *aliquis*, with *ali-* dropped after *nē*. *Nē cui* = "not to anyone" = "to no one." |
| Mark 6:8 (Nova Vulgata), Jesus sending out the Twelve: *et praecēpit eīs, nē quid tollerent in viā…* (*Praecipiō*: instruct, + dative; *tollō*: lift, take; Chapter 45's "raise.") Translate. | "And he instructed them **not to take anything** for the road…" (Mark goes on: except a staff.) ***Nē quid*** = "not anything." |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.42, 58 BC. Before a meeting with Caesar, the German king Ariovistus makes a demand: *Ariovistus postulāvit nē quem peditem ad conloquium Caesar addūceret.* (*Pedes, peditis*: foot soldier; *conloquium*: a meeting, a talk, from Chapter 43's *loquor*.) Translate. | "Ariovistus **demanded** that Caesar **bring no** foot soldier to the meeting." ***Nē quem*** = "not anyone" (accusative). Both sides were to come with horsemen only. |
| *Nē cui, nē quid, nē quem*: what's the pattern? | *Aliquis* loses its *ali-* after *nē* in every case: ***nē quis, nē cuius, nē cui, nē quem, nē quō***, and ***nē quid***. In an order, as in a goal, Romans prefer this to *ut nēmō*. |

## 5. The matching rule, orders edition

:: Imperāvit ut venīrent. || He ordered them to come. (teaching line)

Imperat ut veniant… imperāvit ut venīrent.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Grandma orders the boys to come." Then: "Grandma ordered the boys to come." | ***Avia puerīs imperat ut veniant · Avia puerīs imperāvit ut venīrent.*** Chapter 54's two teams: present after a now verb, imperfect after a past one. |
| Could an order ever take the perfect or pluperfect subjunctive: *imperāvit ut vēnissent*? | **No.** An order always points **forward**: nobody orders what's already done. So a tucked-in command uses just two tenses: the **present** (now team) and the **imperfect** (past team). |
| And Chapter 54's "later" column, *-ūrus sim*? | Not needed. The command is already about later, so the plain present or imperfect does the job. |
| "I'm asking you to come" · "I asked you (yesterday) to come" · "I have (just now) asked you to come." | ***Tē rogō ut veniās · Heri tē rogāvī ut venīrēs · Tē rogāvī ut veniās*** (Chapter 54's "have done" perfect, now team). |
| A passive order: "Grandma orders that the cheese be carried home." (*Portō*.) Now, then past. | ***Avia imperat ut cāseus domum portētur · Avia imperāvit ut cāseus domum portārētur.*** |

!!! success "The Law of the Tucked-In Command"
    A verb of **asking, begging, urging, warning, ordering** or **persuading** + ***ut*** ("to") or ***nē*** ("not to") + the **subjunctive** reports a command. English says "to"; Latin says *ut*. Orders look forward, so only two tenses: **present** on the now team, **imperfect** on the past team.

    | Straight out | Tucked in |
    |---|---|
    | *Venī!* Come! | *Avia mihi imperat ut veniam.* Grandma orders me to come. |
    | *Nē tetigerīs!* Don't touch! | *Avia mē monuit nē tangerem.* Grandma warned me not to touch. |
    | *Nēmō tangat!* Let nobody touch! | *Avia imperat nē quis tangat.* Grandma orders that nobody touch. |
    | *Edāmus!* Let's eat! | *Avus nōs hortātur ut edāmus.* Grandpa urges us to eat. |

## 6. *Iubeō* and *vetō*

:: Exīre ex urbe iubet cōnsul hostem. || The consul orders the enemy to leave the city. (Cicero, *Against Catiline* 1.13)

Iubeō tē venīre.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 19's *iubeō*, "order," and Chapter 48's envoys: *Lēgātī eum togam induere iussērunt.* What follows *iussērunt*? | An **accusative** (*eum*) and an **infinitive** (*induere*): "The envoys **ordered him to put on** his toga." No *ut*! |
| So: "Grandma orders me to come," two ways. | ***Avia mē iubet venīre*** (*iubeō* + accusative + infinitive) · ***Avia mihi imperat ut veniam*** (*imperō* + dative + *ut*). Same meaning, two patterns. |
| Cicero to Catiline (*Against Catiline* 1.13): *Exīre ex urbe iubet cōnsul hostem.* (*Exeō*: go out, leave.) Who orders whom? | The **consul** (Cicero himself) orders the **enemy** (Catiline) **to leave** the city. The words are shuffled for punch: the infinitive first, *hostem* last. |
| Catiline asks, "Into exile?" Cicero: *nōn iubeō, sed … suādeō.* (*Suādeō*: advise.) | "I don't **order** it, but … I **advise** it." (Cicero's words in the gap, "if you ask me," are a *sī* clause: Chapter 57.) |
| Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.7.2, at Geneva: *pontem, quī erat ad Genavam, iubet rescindī.* (*Pōns, pontis*: bridge; *Genava*: Geneva; *rescindō*: cut down.) What kind of infinitive is *rescindī*? | The **passive** infinitive (Chapter 30): "he orders the bridge which was at Geneva **to be cut down**." With *iubeō*, a passive infinitive is common. |
| ***Vetō***, "forbid," works like *iubeō*. Chapter 38's Horace: *rīdentem dīcere vērum quid vetat?* And "Grandma forbids us to touch the cheese." | "What **forbids** someone laughing **to tell** the truth?" · ***Avia nōs vetat cāseum tangere*** = *Avia nōbīs imperat nē cāseum tangāmus.* |

!!! success "The Law of *Iubeō* and *Vetō*"
    ***Iubeō*** (order) and ***vetō*** (forbid) normally take no *ut*: they take an **accusative + infinitive**, like Chapter 42's reported speech. The other ordering verbs take *ut / nē* + subjunctive.

    | *iubeō, vetō* | the others |
    |---|---|
    | *Avia mē iubet venīre.* | *Avia mihi imperat ut veniam.* |
    | *Avia nōs vetat tangere.* | *Avia nōbīs imperat nē tangāmus.* |

## 7. Caesar's persuaders

:: Cīvitātī persuāsit ut dē fīnibus suīs … exīrent. || He persuaded the community to leave their lands… (Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.2.1)

Persuāsit ut… persuādet ut…

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| 61 BC: the story before the *Gallic War*. Orgetorix, the richest man among the Helvetians, plots to rule all Gaul (1.2.1): *cīvitātī persuāsit ut dē fīnibus suīs cum omnibus cōpiīs exīrent.* (*Cīvitās*: community, state, Chapter 54; *fīnēs*: lands, territory; *cōpiae*: forces.) Translate. | "He **persuaded** the community **to go out** of their territory with all their forces." |
| *Cīvitātī* is singular. Why plural *exīrent*? | Caesar thinks of the **people** in it: "the community … that **they** go out." Latin often follows the sense. And imperfect, past team: *persuāsit* is a story perfect. |
| Next (1.3) Orgetorix visits the neighbors, and Caesar switches to his historic present: *persuādet Casticō … ut rēgnum in cīvitāte suā occupāret.* (*Casticus*: a Sequanian noble; *rēgnum*: royal power; *occupō*: seize.) Translate. | "He **persuades** Casticus **to seize** royal power in his own state." |
| *Persuādet* is present. Which team is *occupāret*? | The **past team**. Chapter 54: after Caesar's historic present, a subjunctive may take either team. The next line does the same: *Dumnorīgī … ut idem cōnārētur persuādet*, "he persuades Dumnorix to try the same thing." |
| 1.20, in 58 BC. Caesar spares the troublemaker Dumnorix for his brother's sake, calls him in, and *monet ut in reliquum tempus omnēs suspīciōnēs vītet.* (*In reliquum tempus*: for the future; *suspīciō*: suspicion; *vītō*: avoid.) Translate, and name the team. | "He **warns** him **to avoid** all suspicion in the future." Present *vītet*: the **now team** this time. Same author, same historic present, either team. |

## 8. Asking in the Gospels

:: Rogāte ergō Dominum messis, ut mittat operāriōs in messem suam. || So ask the Lord of the harvest to send workers into his harvest. (Matthew 9:38, Nova Vulgata)

Rogāte ut… dēprecābātur nē…

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Matthew 9:37–38 (Nova Vulgata). "The harvest is great, but the workers are few": *rogāte ergō Dominum messis, ut mittat operāriōs in messem suam.* (*Messis*: harvest; *operārius*: worker; *ergō*: so.) Translate. | "**So ask** the Lord of the harvest **to send** workers into his harvest." |
| *Rogāte* is an imperative. Which team is *mittat*? | The **now team**: a command is a now verb (Chapter 54). |
| Mark 5:10 (Nova Vulgata). A man possessed by a crowd of demons meets Jesus. Asked his name, he answers "Legion, for we are many." *Et dēprecābātur eum multum, nē sē expelleret extrā regiōnem.* (*Dēprecor*: beg; *multum*: hard, a lot; *expellō*: drive out; *extrā regiōnem*: out of the region.) Translate. | "And he **begged** him hard **not to drive** them out of the region." |
| Whom does *sē* mean? | The **one begging** (Legion, who speaks as "we"), the subject of *dēprecābātur*, not Jesus. Chapter 42's Law of the Speaker's *Sē* works in tucked-in commands too: *sē* points back to the one asking. |
| Luke 8:31 (Nova Vulgata), the same story: *Et rogābant eum, nē imperāret illīs, ut in abyssum īrent.* (*Abyssus*: the abyss, the deep.) Untangle the two tucked-in commands. | "And they begged him **not to order** them **to go** into the abyss." *Rogābant … nē imperāret* (the asking, "not to"); inside it, *imperāret illīs ut … īrent* (the ordering, "to"). Both past team. |

## 9. "That" or "to"?

:: Dīc ut lapidēs istī pānēs fīant. || Tell these stones to become bread. (Matthew 4:3, Nova Vulgata)

Persuāsī eī… ut…

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Persuādeō* has two jobs. *Marcō persuāsī Avum dormīre* · *Marcō persuāsī ut dormīret.* | First: "I **convinced** Marcus **that** Grandpa was sleeping," a statement (accusative + infinitive, Chapter 42). Second: "I **persuaded** Marcus **to sleep**," a command (*ut* + subjunctive). |
| *Moneō* too: *Tē moneō canem mordēre* · *Tē moneō nē canem tangās.* (*Mordeō*: bite, Chapter 38.) | "I **warn** you **that** the dog bites" (a statement) · "I **warn** you **not to** touch the dog" (a command). |
| Even *dīcō*. Matthew 4:3 (Nova Vulgata): in the desert the tempter says to Jesus, "If you are the Son of God, *dīc ut lapidēs istī pānēs fīant*." (*Lapis, lapidis*: stone; *pānis*: bread, Chapter 11; *fīō*: become, Chapter 32.) Translate. | "**Tell** these stones **to become** bread." *Dīc ut*: "tell (them) to," a command. *Dīc lapidēs pānēs esse* would be "**say that** the stones are bread." |
| So how do you tell? | Look at what follows: an **accusative + infinitive** is "**that**" (a report of fact); ***ut / nē* + subjunctive** is "**to**" (an order). |

!!! success "The Law of “That” and “To”"
    Some verbs can report a **fact** or an **order**. The construction tells you which:

    | Verb | + accusative + infinitive: "that" | + *ut / nē* + subjunctive: "to" |
    |---|---|---|
    | *persuādeō* | convince that | persuade to |
    | *moneō* | warn, remind that | warn to, advise to |
    | *dīcō* | say that | tell to |

## 10. Polite letters

:: Cūrā ut valeās. || Take care that you stay well. (Cicero, *Letters to Friends* 16.7, to Tiro)

Velim… cūrā… petō ā tē…

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 50's chunk: *Velim mihi scrībās*, "I'd like you to write to me." Where's the *ut*? | There isn't one. After ***velim*** (and *volō, vellem*), Latin often leaves it out: *velim scrībās* says the same as *velim ut scrībās*. It's a tucked-in command without the *ut*. Chapter 50's promise, paid. |
| Rome, 81 BC, Cicero's earliest surviving speech, *Pro Quinctio*. His client Quinctius has a debt to pay, and his partner Naevius has promised the money. Quinctius says to him (19): *nunc hoc velim cūrēs … quod dīxistī.* (*Cūrō*: see to, take care of; Chapter 35's *cūra*, care.) Translate. | "Now I'd like **you to see to** this … what you said." Quinctius's own words, with Chapter 50's polite *velim*. |
| Just before (18), Cicero reports Quinctius's first request in his own words: *rogat ut cūret quod dīxisset.* Translate. | "He **asks** him **to see to** what he **had said**." The tucked-in version. Naevius refuses, so Quinctius asks again, straight out: the *velim* line. (*Quod dīxisset* goes subjunctive too: it's part of the request.) |
| *Rogat* is a historic present. Which team are *cūret* and *dīxisset*? | **Both!** *Cūret* is now team, *dīxisset* past team. Chapter 54 said a historic present may take either team; Cicero uses both in one line. Allen and Greenough's grammar quotes this very line to show it. |
| Cicero ends a letter to his secretary Tiro: *Cūrā ut valeās* (*Letters to Friends* 16.7). (*Valeō*: be well, Chapter 50's *Valeās*.) Translate word for word, then naturally. | "**Take care that you stay well**": "Take care of yourself." *Cūrā* is an imperative; *ut valeās* is the thing to take care of. |
| 50 BC: Tiro is sick in Greece. Cicero writes (*Letters to Friends* 16.4): *Illud, mī Tīrō, tē rogō, sūmptū nē parcās ūllā in rē.* (*Sūmptus*: expense, *sūmptū* an old dative; *parcō* + dative, spare, Chapter 44.) Translate. | "This, my Tiro, I ask of you: **don't spare** expense in anything." No *ut* needed: *nē* starts the order. |
| Cicero's son Marcus, a student in Athens, writes to Tiro (16.21): *petō ā tē ut quam celerrimē mihi librārius mittātur.* (*Librārius*: secretary, copyist; *quam celerrimē*: as fast as possible, Chapter 34.) Translate. | "I **ask** you **that** a secretary **be sent** to me as fast as possible." *Petō ā tē*: "I ask **from** you." A passive order, now team. |

## 11. Which *ut*?

:: Cūr? An quid? || Why? Or what? (teaching line)

Ut… ut… ut…

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Ad forum eō ut cāseum emam.* | A **goal** (Chapter 51): **why** I go. "I go to the forum **to buy** cheese." |
| *Avia mē rogat ut cāseum emam.* | A **tucked-in command**: **what** she asks. "Grandma asks me **to buy** cheese." |
| *Avus tam fessus erat ut dormīret.* | A **result** (Chapter 52): the signpost *tam*. "Grandpa was **so** tired **that** he slept." |
| *Avia mē rogat quid emam.* | A **tucked-in question** (Chapter 53): a question word, no *ut*. "Grandma asks me **what** I'm buying." |
| *Ut Avia dīxit, cāseum ēmī.* | Fact mood: ***ut*** = "**as**" (Chapter 51). "As Grandma said, I bought the cheese." |
| *Avia dīcit mē cāseum ēmisse.* | A **report** (Chapter 42): accusative + infinitive. "Grandma says **that** I bought the cheese." |

## 14. Lectiō: *Parva pars*

:: Hoc volō, sīc iubeō. || This I want; so I order. (Juvenal, *Satires* 6.223; Chapter 19)

Parva pars cāseī… magna rogātiō.

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Find the orders with *nē*. Who gives each, and which team? | Grandma: ***nē quis hanc partem tangat*** (now team, *imperō*). John retells it: ***nē quis cāseum tangeret*** (past team, *imperāvit*). |
| Find the orders with *ut*, and the person asked. What case is each? | ***tē … moneō ut … sedeās · tē rogō ut … dēs · Marcum ōrāvit ut … daret · mē ōrāvit ut … ferrem · tē hortor ut … fugēs · tē ōrō ut … mittās*** (accusative); ***canī persuādēre … ut … ferret · tibi imperō ut … ferās · tibi imperō ut … dēs*** (dative); ***Petis ā mē ut … dem*** (*ā* + ablative); ***rogāvit ut … custōdīrem***, with no person named; and the letters' ***Cūrā ut valeās***, twice. |
| Which orders have **no** *ut*? | ***Avia mē iussit cāseum custōdīre*** and ***eum mūrem fugāre iubeō*** (*iubeō* + infinitive), and Grandpa's polite ***Velim … dēs*** (no *ut* after *velim*). |
| Grandpa's last request has two clauses: *ut Iōannem mēcum mittās* and *nē cāseum in viā edam*. Which is which? | ***Ut … mittās***: a **tucked-in command**, what he begs. ***Nē … edam***: a **goal**, why he wants it. Grandma's ***ut novum cāseum emās*** is a goal too (Chapter 51's *mittō … ut*). |
| *Ut Avia imperāverat*: which *ut*? And whose words does John borrow at the end? | Fact mood: ***ut*** = "**as**" (Chapter 51). John borrows **Juvenal** (Chapter 29): *Quis custōdiet ipsōs custōdēs?* |
