# Caput L: Utinam!

*Liber V, Modus Subiūnctīvus · Chapter 50*

*In which the verb learns to wish. Chapter 49 gave the new mood three jobs: "let's," "let him," and "what am I to do?" It also left two promises. The "let…" of a prayer (*fīat voluntās tua*) was "really a wish," and* velim *was "a famous polite 'I'd like…'" with "Chapter 50's job." Here are both. Today: the subjunctive of wishing (with and without the little word* utinam, *"if only"), the subjunctive of "would" and "might," the polite* velim, *and one chunk,* vellem, *for a wish that can't come true. Along the way: Cicero wishing Catiline would leave town, a Roman comedy greeting, a tombstone, the election of Rome's second king, Vergil's floating islands, and Nero refusing to sign. Speaking goal: make a wish, soften a request, and say what someone might say.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Utinam veniat! · Utinam nē pluat! · Dī tē ament! · Valeās! · Vīvat! · Velim cāseum. · Dā mihi, sīs… · Dīcat aliquis… · Crēdās… · Vellem…*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** optative subjunctive (the "if only / may it" subjunctive) · potential subjunctive (the "would / might" subjunctive)
    - **Forms:** no new forms: Chapter 49's present subjunctive does all the work; *vellem* only as a chunk
    - **Laws:** the Law of If Only · the Law of Would and Might · the Law of the Polite Ask
    - **Words:** *utinam, pluit, valeō* (*valeās*), *requiēscō, levis, faustus, deus* (*dī*), *sīs* (= *sī vīs*), *cōmiter, vellem*
    - **Reading:** *Utinam nē pluat!* (a rainy day eighteen: Grandpa learns to ask nicely, and the cheese survives the day)
    - **Speaking:** make five wishes; ask for things three polite ways; say what someone "might say"

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep Chapter 49's Law of the Swapped Vowel close (*amem, habeam, dūcam, sim, velim*): today adds no new endings, only new jobs. And remember Chapter 11's polite words, *quaesō* and *amābō tē*.

## 1. If only!

:: Utinam veniat! || If only he would come! (teaching line)

Utinam!

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 49's *Veniat!* meant "let him come!" A wish-order. Now put one small word in front: *Utinam veniat!* What changes? | It's no longer an order. It's a **wish**: "**If only he would come!**", "I hope he comes!" *Utinam* means "**if only**, oh that…, I wish that…" |
| So is the form *veniat* new? | **No.** It's Chapter 49's present subjunctive. Same form, new job. |
| Grammar books call this job the **optative subjunctive** (*optō*, wish, from Chapter 45's *Rōmae rūs optās*). Plain words? | **The "if only / may it" subjunctive**: a wish. |
| Three wishes: *Utinam Marcus veniat! · Utinam cāseum habeāmus! · Utinam valeās!* (*Valeō*: be well, the verb inside Chapter 1's *Valē!*) | "If only Marcus would come! · I hope we get some cheese! · I hope you're well!" |
| A present-subjunctive wish: does the speaker think it can still come true? | **Yes.** *Utinam* + present = a wish for the future that **may still happen**. (Wishes that are already impossible, "if only he *were* here," need other tenses: Chapters 52–53.) |

## 2. Old wishes, named at last

:: Fīat voluntās tua. || Thy will be done. (Matthew 6:10; Chapters 32 and 49)

Adveniat! Fīat! Sanctificētur!

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 49 read the Lord's Prayer: *sanctificētur nōmen tuum, adveniat rēgnum tuum, fīat voluntās tua*, and promised that a prayer's "let…" is "really a wish." Why a wish? | Nobody can **order** God's name to be holy. The speaker **hopes** for it: "**May** your name be made holy, **may** your kingdom come, **may** your will be done." Optative. |
| So *fīat* in Genesis (*Fīat lūx*) and *fīat* in the prayer: same form, same job? | Same form, **different job**. *Fīat lūx*: God **orders** light (jussive). *Fīat voluntās tua*: a person **wishes** (optative). Only the sense decides. |
| Chapter 49 also said *velim* was a famous polite "I'd like…", "Chapter 50's job." Its fact twin is *volō*. What's the difference in tone? | *Volō*: "**I want**." Blunt. *Velim*: "**I'd like**, I would want." Soft. §§10–11 put it to work. |
| Chapter 11 already had two polite words. Which? | ***Quaesō*** ("please," "I ask") and ***amābō tē*** ("pretty please," literally "I will love you"). Today adds a third and a fourth. |

## 3. *Utinam* in real Latin

:: Utinam tibi istam mentem dī immortālēs duint! || If only the immortal gods would give you that idea! (Cicero, *Against Catiline* 1.22)

Utinam… duint!

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 43 met Cicero's *Quō usque tandem…?* to Catiline. In the same speech, Cicero wishes Catiline would just leave Rome. The motto: *Utinam tibi istam mentem dī immortālēs duint!* (*Iste, ista, istud*: that (of yours); *mēns*: mind, here "idea"; *dī*: gods, plural of *deus*.) Find the verb. | ***Duint***, an **old form of *dent*** (Chapter 49's *dem, dēs, det… dent*). Cicero picks the old-fashioned form for a solemn wish. |
| Translate it. | "**If only** the immortal gods **would give** you that idea!" (The idea of going into exile.) |
| Is it a wish that can still come true? | **Yes**, and it did: Catiline left Rome that very night. Present subjunctive, a hopeful wish. |
| Build: "If only Grandma would give us cheese!" (*dō, dare*) | ***Utinam Avia nōbīs cāseum det!*** |

## 4. Wishing something *won't* happen: *nē*

:: Utinam nē pluat! || If only it doesn't rain! (teaching line)

Utinam nē!

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 49's Law: *nōn* is for facts, ***nē*** for "let's not" and "let him not." A wish is not a fact. So, "If only it doesn't rain!" (*pluit*: it rains) | ***Utinam nē pluat!*** Wishes take ***nē***. |
| "May the dog not eat the cheese!" "If only Dad doesn't sing!" | ***Utinam nē canis cāseum edat! Utinam nē Pater cantet!*** |
| Can you drop *utinam* and keep *nē*? | Yes: ***Nē canis cāseum edat!*** "**May** the dog not eat the cheese!" (Or "let the dog not…": the wish and the order look the same.) |
| Wish or fact? *Nōn pluit. · Utinam nē pluat.* | *Nōn pluit*: a **fact**, "it isn't raining." *Utinam nē pluat*: a **wish**, "if only it doesn't rain." |

## 5. Wishes without *utinam*

:: Dī tē ament! || May the gods love you! (Plautus, *The Pot of Gold* 183)

Dī tē ament!

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Plautus, *The Pot of Gold* 183. A rich neighbor greets the old miser Euclio warmly; Euclio answers: *Dī tē ament, Megadōre.* (*Megadōrus*: the neighbor.) No *utinam*. Still a wish? | Yes: "**May the gods love you**, Megadorus." A greeting and a thank-you at once, like "God bless you." Plain subjunctive, no *utinam* needed. |
| Romans could also say goodbye with *Valeās!* What is it, and how is it different from *Valē!*? | "**May you be well!**", "Farewell, I hope you're well." *Valē* is a command ("be well!"); ***Valeās*** is a wish. Same goodbye, softer. |
| Roman tombstones often end *S·T·T·L*: ***Sit tibi terra levis.*** (*Levis*: light, not heavy.) | "**May the earth be light on you.**" *Sit*: Chapter 49's *sim, sīs, sit*. (Martial 9.29.11 quotes it in a joke.) |
| Christian tombstones still carry R.I.P.: ***Requiēscat in pāce.*** (*Requiēscō*: rest.) | "**May he (or she) rest in peace.**" Fourth family *requiēscō* → *requiēscat*. Plural: *Requiēscant in pāce.* |
| Livy 1.17.10: after Romulus died, Rome had no king. An official called the people together and said: *Quod bonum, faustum fēlīxque sit, Quirītēs, rēgem creāte.* (*Faustus*: lucky; *Quirītēs*: "citizens"; *creāte*: choose, elect.) | "**May it be** good, lucky, and happy, citizens: choose a king." *Quod … sit* is the Romans' formula for wishing luck on a public act. |

## 6. *Vīvat!*

:: Vīvant professōrēs! || Long live the teachers! (medieval student song *Gaudeāmus*)

Vīvat! Vīvant!

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 49's student song *Gaudeāmus igitur* has a later verse: *Vīvant professōrēs!* | "**Long live** the teachers!" *Vīvō* → *vīvat, vīvant*: "**may they live**." |
| "Long live Grandma!" "Long live the cheese!" | ***Vīvat Avia! Vīvat cāseus!*** |
| English "Long live the king!" | ***Vīvat rēx!*** (Chapter 13's *rēx*.) |
| "May Marcus be happy!" "May y'all be well!" | ***Marcus fēlīx sit! Valeātis!*** (*Valeātis*: the y'all form.) |

!!! success "The Law of If Only"
    A wish uses the **optative subjunctive**: the "if only / may it" subjunctive.

    | how | example | English |
    |---|---|---|
    | *utinam* + subjunctive | *Utinam veniat!* | If only he would come! |
    | subjunctive alone | *Dī tē ament! · Vīvat rēx! · Valeās!* | May the gods love you! · Long live the king! · Farewell! |
    | "not": ***nē*** | *Utinam nē pluat! · Nē canis cāseum edat!* | If only it doesn't rain! · May the dog not eat the cheese! |

    The **present** subjunctive wishes for something that **can still happen**. Same forms as Chapter 49; the job is new.

## 7. Wish or order?

:: Adveniat rēgnum tuum. || Thy kingdom come. (Matthew 6:10)

Fīat lūx. Fīat voluntās tua.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Marcus cāseum ferat!* Wish or order? | It can be either: "**Let** Marcus carry the cheese" (Mom gives an order) or "**May** Marcus carry the cheese" (Grandpa, tired, hopes). Same form. |
| So how do you tell them apart? | **Who** is speaking and to **whom**. A boss gives orders (jussive); someone who has no power over it wishes (optative). And ***utinam*** always marks a wish. |
| *Requiēscat in pāce.* Order or wish? | **Wish.** Nobody can order the dead to rest. |
| *Canis domī maneat!* (Chapter 49, Grandma). And *Utinam canis domī maneat!*? | Grandma: an **order** about the dog ("let the dog stay home"). With *utinam*: a **wish** ("if only the dog would stay home!"), maybe because he won't. |

## 8. "Would" and "might"

:: Dīcat aliquis… || Someone might say… (teaching line)

Dīcat aliquis…

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Dīcat aliquis: "Avus nimis edit."* (*Aliquis*: someone, Chapter 29; *nimis*: too much.) Not a wish, not an order. What is it? | "**Someone might say**, 'Grandpa eats too much.'" A **possibility**: what *could* happen. |
| Grammar books call this the **potential subjunctive** (*possum*, can). Plain words? | **The "would / might" subjunctive**: what would or might be, softly said. |
| *Quis hoc crēdat?* | "**Who would believe** this?" (Answer: nobody.) A potential question. |
| The "not" of a wish is *nē*. The potential is about a fact that might be. Guess its "not." | ***Nōn***, like the deliberative: *Nōn crēdat*, "he **wouldn't** believe it." |
| *Hoc nōn dīcam.* Future or potential? | Either: "I **won't** say this" or "I **wouldn't** say this." As in Chapter 49's *faciam*, the "I" form is the same; sense decides. |

## 9. *Crēdās*: "you'd think"

:: pelagō crēdās innāre revulsās Cycladas || you'd think the Cyclades, torn loose, were swimming on the sea (Vergil, *Aeneid* 8.691–692)

Crēdās!

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Vergil, *Aeneid* 8.691–692. The great sea battle of Actium on Aeneas's shield: the ships are so huge that… *pelagō crēdās innāre revulsās Cycladas.* (*Pelagus*: sea; *innō*: swim on; *revulsus*: torn loose; *Cyclades*: the Greek islands.) | "**You'd think** the Cyclades, torn loose, were swimming on the sea." *Crēdās* + Chapter 42's accusative + infinitive. |
| Who is "you" in *crēdās*? John? | **Anyone**: "you'd think," "one would think." A **general** "you." That's the second-person subjunctive Romans used freely in prose (Chapter 49's *Nē timeās* to one person was poetry and Bible). |
| "You'd think the dog was praying." (*Ōrō*: Chapter 49.) | ***Crēdās canem ōrāre.*** |
| "You wouldn't believe it!" | ***Nōn crēdās!*** |

!!! success "The Law of Would and Might"
    The **potential subjunctive**: the "would / might" subjunctive, for what **could** be.

    | example | English |
    |---|---|
    | *Dīcat aliquis…* | Someone might say… |
    | *Quis hoc crēdat?* | Who would believe this? |
    | *Crēdās canem ōrāre.* | You'd think the dog was praying. |
    | *Nōn crēdās!* | You wouldn't believe it! |

    "Not": ***nōn***. Its "you" is anybody: "you'd think," "one would think."

## 10. *Velim, nōlim, mālim*

:: Velim. Nōlim. Mālim. || I'd like. I'd rather not. I'd prefer. (teaching line)

Volō? Velim!

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 32's *volō, nōlō, mālō* = want, not want, prefer. Chapter 49 built *velim, nōlim, mālim*. Now as potentials: plain English? | ***Velim***: "**I'd like**." ***Nōlim***: "**I'd rather not**." ***Mālim***: "**I'd prefer**." The same softness English gets from "would." |
| *Volō cāseum!* vs *Velim cāseum.* Which would Grandma rather hear? | ***Velim cāseum***: "I'd like some cheese." *Volō cāseum!* is "I want cheese!" |
| "I'd like to go to the river." (*Volō* + infinitive, as in Chapter 32.) | ***Ad flūmen īre velim.*** |
| "I'd rather not sing." "I'd prefer to sleep." | ***Cantāre nōlim. Dormīre mālim.*** |
| *Velim* or *velīs* to ask someone else? "What would you like?" | ***Quid velīs?*** Softer than *Quid vīs?* ("what do you want?"). |

## 11. The polite ask

:: Velim mihi scrībās. || I'd like you to write to me. (teaching line; the way Cicero asks his friends)

Dā mihi, sīs!

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 11's *quaesō* and *amābō tē*, plus today's *velim*. Ask Grandma for cheese three polite ways. | ***Dā mihi cāseum, quaesō! · Amābō tē, dā mihi cāseum! · Velim cāseum.*** |
| A fourth "please," common in Roman comedy and letters: ***sīs***. It is short for *sī vīs*, "if you want." Translate *Dā mihi, sīs, cāseum.* | "Give me the cheese, **if you please**." *Sī vīs* ("if you want") squeezed into one word. |
| Careful! Chapter 49's *sīs* was a form of *sum*. Two *sīs*? | Yes: ***sīs*** = "you may be" (*sim, sīs, sit*) and ***sīs*** = "please" (*sī vīs*). With a command (*Dā mihi, sīs*) it's "please." |
| Cicero's letters often ask this way: *Velim mihi scrībās.* Find the subjunctive. | ***Scrībās***: "I'd like **you to write** to me." *Velim* plus a second subjunctive is a full sentence-pattern that Chapter 56 explains. Today: use it as a **chunk**. |

!!! success "The Law of the Polite Ask"
    | blunt | polite |
    |---|---|
    | *Volō cāseum!* I want cheese! | *Velim cāseum.* I'd like some cheese. |
    | *Dā mihi cāseum!* Give me cheese! | *Dā mihi, sīs / quaesō / amābō tē, cāseum.* Please give me cheese. |
    | *Quid vīs?* What do you want? | *Quid velīs?* What would you like? |
    | *Nōlō!* I don't want to! | *Nōlim.* I'd rather not. |

    The "would" in English is the subjunctive in Latin.

## 12. *Vellem*: "I wish… but no"

:: Quam vellem nescīre litterās! || How I wish I didn't know how to write! (Nero, in Suetonius, *Nero* 10.2)

Velim… vellem.

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| ***Vellem*** is a cousin of *velim*. Learn it as a chunk: "**I wish** (but it can't be)," "I'd like, if only I could." *Velim cāseum* vs *Vellem cāseum.* | *Velim cāseum*: "I'd like some cheese" (and maybe I'll get it). ***Vellem cāseum***: "I **wish** I had cheese" (but I don't, and won't). |
| Suetonius, *Nero* 10.2. The young emperor Nero, asked to sign a man's death sentence, sighed: *Quam vellem nescīre litterās!* (*Quam*: how!; *nescīre*: not to know, Chapter 46's *nesciō*; *litterae*: letters, writing.) | "**How I wish** I didn't know how to write!" He has to sign; he can't un-learn his letters. A wish that can't come true: ***vellem***. |
| Is *vellem* a present subjunctive like *velim*? | **No.** It's another tense of the subjunctive, which Chapter 52 builds. Today: just the chunk. |
| *Cāseum ēsse velim.* vs *Vellem tōtum cāseum ēsse.* (*Ēsse*: to eat, Chapter 4.) | "I'd like to eat cheese" (possible) vs "**I wish** I could eat the whole cheese" (but it isn't allowed). |

## 14. Traps and fix-its

:: Errāre licet; corrigere melius. || Making a mistake is allowed; correcting it is better. (teaching line)

Utinam nōn? Utinam nē!

{#g-14}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "If only it doesn't rain!": *Utinam nōn pluat!* | ***Utinam nē pluat!*** Wishes take *nē*. |
| "I'd like some cheese": *Volō cāseum.* | ***Velim cāseum.*** *Volō* is the blunt fact "I want." |
| "If only he would come!": *Utinam venit!* | ***Utinam veniat!*** *Venit* is a fact ("he comes / came"); a wish needs the subjunctive. |
| "You wouldn't believe it!": *Nē crēdās!* | ***Nōn crēdās!*** The potential's "not" is *nōn*. (*Nē crēdās* would be "don't believe it!") |
| "Give me the cheese, please": *Dā mihi, es, cāseum.* | ***Dā mihi, sīs, cāseum.*** "Please" is *sīs* (*sī vīs*), not *es* ("you are"). |

## 15. Lectiō: *Utinam nē pluat!*

:: Utinam crās quoque maneat! || If only it stays tomorrow too! (teaching line)

Quis cāseum servet?

Diē duodēvīcēsimā: the day after Chapter 49. The dog ate Grandpa's forum cheese, but the family's new cheese is safe. Today it's raining. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. Hunt every subjunctive and ask: wish, "would," or polite ask? New in passing: *duodēvīcēsimus*, eighteenth; *ēmptus*, bought (Chapter 37's participle of *emō*); *cōmiter*, politely; *nimis*, too much. Old friends: *cārus*, dear (Chapter 44); *licet* (Chapter 44); *vesperī*, in the evening (Chapter 17).

## 16. Sound check

:: Clārē loquāmur! || Let's speak clearly! (teaching line; Chapter 49)

Audī et dīc.

{#g-16}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *utinam · utinam nē*? | U-ti-nam · U-ti-nam NAY. All three vowels short; the emphasis (stress) goes back to the first. |
| *valeās · valeātis · requiēscat*? | WA-le-ahs · wa-le-AH-tis · re-kwi-AYS-kat. The short *e* before a vowel never takes the emphasis. |
| *velim · vellem · nōlim · mālim*? | WE-lim · WEL-lem · NOH-lim · MAH-lim. Hold both *l*'s in *vellem*. |
| *crēdās · Dī tē ament · Quirītēs · faustum*? | KRAY-dahs · DEE TAY A-ment · kwi-REE-tays · FOWS-tum. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Loquāmur Latīnē! || Let's speak Latin! (teaching line)

1. In your own words: what's an optative and what's a potential subjunctive? Give one example each.
2. Make five wishes with *utinam*, two of them with *nē* (*Utinam sōl redeat! Utinam nē pluat!…*).
3. Make three wishes without *utinam* (*Dī tē ament! Vīvat Avia! Valeās!*).
4. Ask for three things politely: with *velim*, with *sīs*, and with *quaesō*. Then turn down an offer with *Nōlim*.
5. Say three "would / might" sentences (*Dīcat aliquis… · Quis hoc crēdat? · Crēdās…*).
6. Contrast *velim* and *vellem* in two sentences of your own.
7. The three Laws in your own words: If Only, Would and Might, the Polite Ask.
8. The real lines: *Fīat voluntās tua · Utinam tibi istam mentem dī immortālēs duint! · Dī tē ament · Sit tibi terra levis · Requiēscat in pāce · Quod bonum, faustum fēlīxque sit · Vīvant professōrēs! · pelagō crēdās innāre revulsās Cycladas · Quam vellem nescīre litterās!* Then read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part, and rebuild it from the English.
