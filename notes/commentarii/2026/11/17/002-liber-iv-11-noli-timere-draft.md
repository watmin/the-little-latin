# Caput XLVII: Nōlī Timēre

*Liber IV, Verba Agentia · Chapter 47*

*In which the "don't" gets its whole family. Back in Chapter 6 you learned to forbid things with* Nōlī timēre! *and* Nōlīte dormīre! *Chapter 18 showed that* nōlī *is the command of* nōlō, *and Chapter 39's Horace said* Aequam mementō, *with a promise: its form waits for Chapter 47. Today: a second command form with a long reach, the one Roman laws, recipes, and letters use (*amātō, estōte, suntō, mementō*). Then every way a Roman said "don't": the everyday* nōlī, *the lawgiver's* nē … -tō, *the poet's* nē *with a plain command, the Bible's "thou shalt not," and one more that waits for Liber V. Along the way: shepherds told not to fear, a friend told not to touch, consuls who obey no one, Cato's cheese cake, a burial law of the Twelve Tables, a priest who warns about a horse, and a prophetess who says "don't give in." Speaking goal: forbid anything, politely or like a lawgiver; give orders for later; write house rules.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Nōlī timēre! · Nōlīte timēre! · Mementō! · Scītō… · Estōte bonī! · Cāseum nē tangitō! · Canis forīs manētō! · Equō nē crēdite · Nōn occīdēs*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** present imperative (the "now" command) · future imperative (the "shall" command; old-school "second imperative") · prohibition (a "don't" command; also "negative command")
    - **Forms:** *amātō, amātōte, amantō* · *habētō, habentō* · *dūcitō, dūcuntō* · *capitō, capiuntō* · *audītō, audiuntō* · *estō, estōte, suntō* · *ītō, euntō* · *fertō* · *dīcitō, facitō* · *mementō, mementōte* · *scītō, scītōte*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the "Shall" Command · the Law of Later and Forever · the Law of the Five Don'ts
    - **Words:** *mementō, scītō, nē, nēve, lībum, addō, permisceō, focus, tangō, sepeliō, ūrō, occīdō, dīligō, fīdūcia, perfectus, salūs, suprēmus, forīs, violō*
    - **Reading:** *Lēgēs Aviae* (Grandma writes house laws for her second cheese, like the Twelve Tables, and Grandpa reads them like a lawyer)
    - **Speaking:** forbid things three ways; give orders for later; write rules with *-tō* and *nē … -tō*

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. Keep Chapter 6 close (*amā, amāte; dīc, dūc, fac, fer; nōlī*), Chapter 18 (*nōlō*), and Chapter 46 (*meminī*).

## 1. *Nōlī timēre*, again

:: Nōlī mē tangere. || Don't touch me. (John 20:17; Chapter 6)

Nōlī timēre. Nōlīte dormīre.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 6's Law of *Nōlī*. "Don't be afraid!" to one person, then to y'all. | ***Nōlī timēre! Nōlīte timēre!*** *Nōlī* or *nōlīte* + the infinitive. |
| Chapter 18 opened the chunk. What is *nōlī*, literally? | The command of ***nōlō***, "I don't want": "**be unwilling** to be afraid!" That's why the next verb is an infinitive, like *Dormīre nōlō*. |
| Chapter 6's ordinary commands: "Love!" "See!" "Lead!" "Hear!" to one, then to y'all. Grammar books call these the **present imperative**. Plain words? | *Amā! Vidē! Dūc! Audī!* · *Amāte! Vidēte! Dūcite! Audīte!* Plain words: "**the 'now' command**," for the person in front of you, to do now. |
| Why not just put *nōn* in front: *Nōn timē*? | Romans didn't, in everyday prose. *Nōn* is the "not" of **facts**: *Nōn timeō*, "I'm not afraid." A command that says "don't" is a **prohibition**: plain words, "**a 'don't' command**" (also called a negative command). Prose makes it with *nōlī*. |
| Chapter 43: "Don't lie!" Chapter 46: "Don't forget the cheese!" | ***Nōlī mentīrī! · Nōlī oblīvīscī cāseī!*** A deponent's infinitive (*-ī*) follows *nōlī* like any other. |
| "Don't be sad, Grandpa!" (Chapter 32's Lectiō.) Which case is *trīstis*? | ***Nōlī trīstis esse, Ave!*** **Nominative**: it describes the person told, "you," through *esse*. |

## 2. *Nōlīte timēre*: the Gospels' favorite "don't"

:: Nōlīte timēre. || Don't be afraid, y'all. (Luke 2:10)

Nōlīte timēre! Ego sum.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Luke 2:10. Night, shepherds, an angel; Chapter 37 read verse 12. First the angel says: *Nōlīte timēre; ecce enim ēvangelizō vōbīs gaudium magnum.* (*Ēvangelizō*: bring good news; *gaudium*: joy, Chapter 35.) | "**Don't be afraid, y'all**; for look, I bring you good news of great joy." Plural *nōlīte*: a whole field of shepherds. |
| Matthew 14:27. Night, a storm; the disciples see Jesus walking on the water and scream "A ghost!" He says: *Habēte fīdūciam, ego sum; nōlīte timēre!* (*Fīdūcia*: confidence, courage.) | "**Have courage**, it is I; **don't be afraid!**" A "do" and a "don't" side by side: present imperative *habēte*, then *nōlīte* + infinitive. |
| John 20:17, the whole verse behind Chapter 6's motto. Mary Magdalene, at the empty tomb, recognizes Jesus: *Nōlī mē tangere, nōndum enim ascendī ad Patrem meum.* (*Ascendō*: go up; *nōndum*: not yet, Chapter 27.) | "**Don't touch me**, for I have not yet gone up to my Father." *Nōlī* to one person. |
| Matthew 10:31: *Nōlīte ergō timēre; multīs passeribus meliōrēs estis vōs.* (*Passer*: sparrow, Catullus's bird from Chapter 11.) | "So **don't be afraid**: you are worth more than many sparrows." *Passeribus* is Chapter 33's ablative of comparison: "better **than** many sparrows." |
| "Don't be afraid of the dog, kids!" (*Timeō* takes a plain accusative.) | ***Nōlīte canem timēre, līberī!*** |

## 3. *Mementō*: a command for later

:: Aequam mementō rēbus in arduīs servāre mentem. || Remember to keep a level mind when things are hard. (Horace, *Odes* 2.3.1–2; Chapter 39)

Mementō! Amātō! Audītō!

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 39's Horace: *Aequam mementō … servāre mentem.* Chapter 46 said *mementō* belongs to *meminī*. But *meminī* has no present stem. How can it give a command? | With a **second** command form, ending in ***-tō***: ***mementō***, "**remember!**" (to y'all, *mementōte*). It's the only command *meminī* has. |
| Grammar books call the *-tō* form the **future imperative**. Plain words? | "**The 'shall' command**": a command for **later**, or for **always**. Old school books call it the "second imperative." Chapter 6's *amā, vidē* is the present imperative. |
| Build it from the infinitive stem: *amā-re, vidē-re, audī-re* → add *-tō*. | ***amātō, vidētō, audītō.*** "You shall love, see, hear." The long vowel stays: a-MAH-toh. |
| The third family has a short *e* in *dūcere, legere, capere*. Before *-tō* it turns to *i*, as in *dūcite, legite*. | ***dūcitō, legitō, capitō.*** Same *i* as Chapter 6's *dūcite* and *capite*. |
| *Crās cāseum emitō!* (Chapter 37's *emō*, buy.) | "**Buy** cheese tomorrow!" The order is for later, so the "shall" command fits. |
| Chapter 6's four rebels clip their commands: *dīc, dūc, fac, fer*. Does the "shall" command clip them? | **No:** ***dīcitō, dūcitō, facitō***. And *ferō*, which has no vowel there (*fert, ferte*), gives ***fertō***. |

## 4. Y'all shall, he shall, they shall

:: Estōte ergō vōs perfectī. || So you all, be perfect. (Matthew 5:48)

Amātō, amātōte, amantō.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| To y'all, add Chapter 1's *-te* again: *amātō* → ? | ***amātōte***, "y'all shall love." Also *vidētōte, dūcitōte, audītōte*. |
| The "shall" command has a **third person** too. ***Amātō*** also means "**he (she, it) shall love**," "let him love." How do you tell "you shall" from "he shall"? | Same form; the **context** tells. A subject word (*Avus amātō*, "Grandpa shall love") or a law about "anyone" means "he shall." A person spoken to means "you shall." |
| "They shall": add *-ō* to the "they" form. *amant, vident, dūcunt, audiunt, capiunt* → ? | ***amantō, videntō, dūcuntō, audiuntō, capiuntō.*** |
| Chant *amō*'s "shall" command: you, he, y'all, they. | ***Amātō, amātō, amātōte, amantō.*** |
| "The kids shall guard the cheese. Grandpa shall sleep." | ***Līberī cāseum custōdiuntō. Avus dormītō.*** |

## 5. The rebels: *estō, ītō, fertō, scītō*

:: Estōte prūdentēs sīcut serpentēs. || Be wise as serpents. (Matthew 10:16)

Estō, estōte, suntō.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Sum* has a "shall" command too: *es-* + *-tō*, *es-* + *-tōte*, and *sunt* + *-ō*. | ***Estō, estōte, suntō.*** "Be! (or: let him be) · y'all be! · let them be!" The plain *es, este* exist, but *estō* is the one you'll meet in laws, letters, and the Bible. |
| Matthew 5:48: *Estōte ergō vōs perfectī, sīcut Pater vester caelestis perfectus est.* (*Perfectus*: complete, perfect; *caelestis*: heavenly.) | "**Be perfect**, therefore, as your heavenly Father is perfect." *Perfectī* is nominative plural, agreeing with *vōs*. |
| Chapter 32's *eō*: *ī-* before a consonant, *e-* before a vowel. Its "shall" command? | ***Ītō, ītōte, euntō.*** Chapter 32's rule holds: *ī-tō*, but *e-untō*. |
| Luke 23:42. A thief, crucified beside Jesus: *Domine, mementō meī, cum vēneris in rēgnum tuum.* (*Cum vēneris*: when you come, Chapter 27's future perfect.) | "Lord, **remember me** when you come into your kingdom." *Meī*: Chapter 46's genitive with remembering. |
| *Sciō*, know. Its "Know!" is nearly always ***scītō*** (y'all: *scītōte*). Cicero, *Letters to Atticus* 1.2.1, with big news: *fīliolō mē auctum scītō, salvā Terentiā.* (*Fīliolus*: little son; *augeō, auctum*: increase, enrich; *Terentia*: his wife.) | "**Know that** I've been blessed with a little son, and Terentia is safe." *Mē auctum*: Chapter 42's accusative and infinitive with *esse* left out (Chapter 41); *salvā Terentiā*: Chapter 40's ablative absolute. |
| Chapter 31's *Iacta ālea est* (Suetonius, *Julius* 32). Some editors, following Erasmus, print ***Iacta ālea estō***, because Plutarch reports Caesar's words as a Greek command. Meaning? | "**Let the die be cast!**" A third-person *estō*: "let it be." |

!!! success "The Law of the \"Shall\" Command"
    The future imperative: infinitive stem + **-tō** (third family **-itō**); y'all **-tōte**; "they" form + **-ō**.

    | | you, he/she | y'all | they |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | *amō* | *amātō* | *amātōte* | *amantō* |
    | *habeō* | *habētō* | *habētōte* | *habentō* |
    | *dūcō* | *dūcitō* | *dūcitōte* | *dūcuntō* |
    | *capiō* | *capitō* | *capitōte* | *capiuntō* |
    | *audiō* | *audītō* | *audītōte* | *audiuntō* |
    | *sum* | *estō* | *estōte* | *suntō* |
    | *eō* | *ītō* | *ītōte* | *euntō* |
    | *ferō* | *fertō* | *fertōte* | *feruntō* |
    | *meminī* | *mementō* | *mementōte* | |
    | *sciō* | *scītō* | *scītōte* | |

    No clipping: *dīcitō, dūcitō, facitō*. The "you" and "he" forms are the same word.

## 6. Laws: the "shall" of the state

:: Salūs populī suprēma lēx estō. || The welfare of the people shall be the highest law. (Cicero, *On the Laws* 3.8)

Suntō. Habentō. Pārentō. Estō.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Roman laws spoke in the "shall" command. Cicero writes his own law code in the old style; *On the Laws* 3.8, on the consuls: *Rēgiō imperiō duo suntō.* (*Rēgius*: royal; *imperium*: power of command.) | "**There shall be** two with royal power." *Suntō*: "they shall be." *Rēgiō imperiō*: Chapter 35's ablative of description. |
| Next: *Mīlitiae summum iūs habentō, nēminī pārentō.* (*Summum iūs*: the highest authority; Chapter 11's *iūs*.) | "**In the field they shall have** the highest authority; **they shall obey** no one." *Mīlitiae*: Chapter 45's "in war"; *nēminī*: Chapter 44's dative with *pāreō*. |
| Then: *Ollīs salūs populī suprēma lēx estō.* (*Ollīs*: old Latin for *illīs*; *salūs*: safety, welfare; *suprēmus*: highest.) | "**For them, the welfare of the people shall be the highest law.**" Third-person *estō*. Without *ollīs*, it's the state motto of Missouri. |
| Why would a law use the "shall" command instead of a plain *es, este*? | A law gives orders for **later and always**: whoever is consul, whenever. The plain command is for the person in front of you, now. |
| Grandma's first house law: "The dog shall stay outdoors." (*Forīs*: outdoors.) | ***Canis forīs manētō.*** "He shall stay": third person. |

## 7. Recipes and letters: do it later

:: Lībum hoc modō facitō. || Make a cheese cake this way. (Cato, *On Agriculture* 75)

Facitō. Additō. Coquitō.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Cato the Elder (Chapter 41's old Cato) wrote a farming book full of recipes, all in the "shall" command. Chapter 75: *Lībum hoc modō facitō.* (*Lībum*: a cake of cheese and flour, offered to the gods; *modus*: way, Chapter 34.) | "**Make** a cheese cake this way." *Facitō*: Chapter 6's *fac*, unclipped. |
| Cato's next lines grind two pounds of cheese in a mortar and mix in flour. Then: *Ōvum ūnum additō et ūnā permiscētō bene.* (*Addō*: add; *ūnā*: together; *permisceō*: mix thoroughly.) | "**Add** one egg and **mix** it all together well." |
| *Inde pānem facitō, folia subditō, in focō caldō … coquitō lēniter.* (*Inde*: then; *folium*: leaf; *subdō*: put under; *focus*: hearth; *caldus* = *calidus*, hot; *lēniter*: gently.) | "Then **make** a loaf, **put** leaves under it, and **bake** it gently on a hot hearth." Not one of these is for right now: a recipe is for whenever you cook. |
| John 20:15. Mary Magdalene thinks the man at the tomb is the gardener: *Domine, sī tū sustulistī eum, dīcitō mihi ubi posuistī eum.* (*Sustulī*: I took away, perfect of *tollō*.) | "Sir, if you have taken him away, **tell** me where you have put him." The telling comes after the "if": *dīcitō*. |
| "Tomorrow, if you go to Rome, buy cheese." | ***Crās, sī Rōmam ībis, cāseum emitō.*** A command hung on a later "if" takes the "shall" command. |

!!! success "The Law of Later and Forever"
    The **present imperative** (*amā, amāte*) is for **now**, to the person in front of you.

    The **future imperative** (*amātō, amātōte, amantō*) is for **later** or **always**: after an "if" or a "when," and in **laws** ("he shall…"), **recipes**, **wills**, and **letters**. A few verbs use it for almost every command: ***mementō, scītō, estō***.

## 8. *Nē*: the lawgiver's "don't"

:: In urbe nē sepelītō nēve ūritō. || One shall not bury or burn a dead man in the city. (Twelve Tables, quoted by Cicero, *On the Laws* 2.58)

Nē tangitō! Nē cantātō!

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 34's motto: *Nē quid nimis*. Which "not" is that? | ***Nē***, Latin's "not" for **don'ts and wishes**. *Nōn* is the "not" of facts. "Nothing too much!": the verb is left out, as in the English. |
| Laws say "don't" with ***nē*** + the "shall" command. "Dad shall not sing." | ***Pater nē cantātō!*** |
| The Twelve Tables (about 450 BC), as Cicero quotes them, *On the Laws* 2.58: *"Hominem mortuum," inquit lēx in XII, "in urbe nē sepelītō nēve ūritō."* (*XII*: the Twelve Tables; *sepeliō*: bury; *ūrō*: burn; *nēve*: and not.) | "'A dead man,' says the law in the Twelve Tables, '**one shall not bury** in the city, **nor burn**.'" *Nēve* (or *neu*) adds a second "don't." And there's Chapter 46's slipped-in *inquit*, used for a law. |
| "No one shall touch the cheese." Two law-style ways. | ***Cāseum nēmō tangitō*** ("no one shall touch") · ***Cāseum nē tangitō*** ("one shall not touch"). |
| "Grandpa shall not come near the cheese." (Chapter 44's *appropinquō* + dative.) | ***Avus cāseō nē appropinquātō.*** |

## 9. The poet's "don't": *nē* + the plain command

:: Equō nē crēdite, Teucrī. || Don't trust the horse, Trojans. (Vergil, *Aeneid* 2.48)

Nē crēdite! Nē cēde!

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Vergil, *Aeneid* 2.48. Laocoön, one line before Chapter 38's *Timeō Danaōs*: *equō nē crēdite, Teucrī.* (*Teucrī*: Trojans; Chapter 44's *crēdō* + dative.) | "**Don't trust** the horse, Trojans." ***Nē*** + the **present** imperative *crēdite*. In prose: *Nōlīte equō crēdere.* Poets like the shorter way. |
| *Aeneid* 6.95, the Sibyl (a prophetess) to Aeneas: *tū nē cēde malīs, sed contrā audentior ītō.* (*Cēdō*: yield, with a dative; *mala*: troubles; *contrā*: against them; *audentior*: more daring, Chapter 33's comparative of *audēns*, from Chapter 43's *audeō*.) | "**Don't give in** to troubles, but **go** against them more boldly." Two command styles in one line: the poet's *nē cēde*, and *ītō*, the "shall" command of *eō*. |
| "Don't be afraid, y'all," three ways: talk, law, poetry. | ***Nōlīte timēre*** (talk) · ***Nē timētōte*** (law) · ***Nē timēte*** (poetry). |

## 10. "Thou shalt not": the Bible's "don't"

:: Nōn fūrtum faciēs. || You shall not steal. (Exodus 20:15)

Nōn occīdēs.

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Exodus 20:13 in the Latin Bible: *Nōn occīdēs.* (*Occīdō*: kill.) Which tense? | The plain **future** (Chapter 17): "you will not kill," that is, "**You shall not kill.**" English Bibles keep it: "Thou shalt not." |
| Exodus 20:15: *Nōn fūrtum faciēs.* | "**You shall not steal**": literally "you will not do theft," Chapter 46's *fūrtum*. |
| Matthew 5:43 quotes the old law: *Dīligēs proximum tuum.* (*Dīligō*: love, Chapter 19's *dīlēctus*; *proximus*: neighbor, "the nearest one.") | "**You shall love** your neighbor." A "do" in the same style. |
| Grandma, Bible style: "You shall not touch the cheese." | ***Cāseum nōn tangēs.*** Plain future with *nōn*. |

## 11. One more "don't", for Liber V

:: Nē timeās. || Don't be afraid. (Luke 1:30)

Nē timeās! Nē timuerītis!

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The angel Gabriel greets Mary (Luke 1:30) with *Nē timeās*. Is *timeās* a form you know? | **No.** It belongs to a new mood, the **subjunctive**, the subject of Liber V. For now, keep ***Nē timeās*** as a chunk: "Don't be afraid." |
| Matthew 10:26 and 10:31, one speech: *Nē ergō timuerītis eōs … Nōlīte ergō timēre.* | "So **don't be afraid** of them … So **don't be afraid**." Two styles, one meaning. *Timuerītis* looks almost like Chapter 27's future perfect *timueritis*, but it's a subjunctive; *nē* + this form is classical prose's other "don't." Liber V explains it. |
| So which do you use when you talk? | ***Nōlī / Nōlīte*** + infinitive: always right. Read the others; write *nōlī*. |

!!! success "The Law of the Five Don'ts"
    | style | example | where you meet it |
    |---|---|---|
    | *nōlī, nōlīte* + infinitive | *Nōlī timēre!* | talk and prose: **use this one** |
    | *nē* + "shall" command | *Cāseum nē tangitō.* | laws, rules, recipes |
    | *nē* + plain command | *Equō nē crēdite.* | poetry |
    | *nōn* + future | *Nōn occīdēs.* | the Ten Commandments: "thou shalt not" |
    | *nē* + subjunctive | *Nē timeās · Nē timuerītis* | chunks for now (Liber V) |

    *Nōn* is the "not" of facts; ***nē*** is the "not" of don'ts and wishes. ***Nēve*** (*neu*) adds a second "don't."

## 12. Give orders

:: Mementō, Iōannēs! || Remember, John! (teaching line)

Nōlī! Mementō! Estō!

{#g-12}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Tell John not to be afraid, and to speak Latin. | ***Nōlī timēre, Iōannēs: Latīnē loquere!*** (Chapter 43's *loquere*.) |
| Tell the kids: "Remember the laws, y'all!" | ***Lēgum mementōte!*** (or *lēgēs*: Chapter 46 lets a thing be genitive or accusative). |
| Write two house rules: "Grandpa shall not touch the cheese. The kids shall be good." | ***Avus cāseum nē tangitō. Līberī bonī suntō.*** |
| Start a letter: "Know that Grandpa ate the cheese." | ***Scītō Avum cāseum ēdisse.*** (Chapter 42's accusative and infinitive.) |
| Write a recipe: "Take cheese, add an egg, bake it." | ***Cāseum capitō, ōvum additō, coquitō.*** |

## 13. Look-alikes and traps

:: Nōn est, sed estō. || Not "is," but "shall be." (teaching line)

Est? Estō? Ēst?

{#g-13}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Amātō*: "you shall love" or "he shall love"? | **Both.** Same form; context decides. Laws usually mean "he (anyone) shall"; letters mean "you shall." |
| *estō · est · ēst*? | ***Estō***: be! / let it be. ***Est***: is. Chapter 6's ***ēst***: eats. |
| *Dūcitō* or *dūctō*? *Fertō* or *feritō*? | ***Dūcitō***: the third family's *i* comes back; only the present command is clipped (*dūc*). But ***fertō***: *ferō* has no vowel to bring back (*fert, ferte*). |
| *Nōlī tangitō!* | Two commands piled up. *Nōlī* takes an infinitive: ***Nōlī tangere!*** Or law style: ***Nē tangitō!*** |
| *Nōn timē!* for "Don't be afraid!"? | *Nōn* + a command is rare. Say ***Nōlī timēre!*** |

## 14. Fix it

:: Errāre licet; mementō corrigere. || Mistakes are allowed; remember to fix them. (teaching line)

Nōlīte timēte? Nōlīte timēre!

{#g-14}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Don't be afraid, y'all": *Nōlīte timēte.* | ***Nōlīte timēre.*** After *nōlīte*, the infinitive. |
| "Remember me!": *Meminī meī!* | ***Mementō meī!*** *Meminī* is "I remember"; the command is *mementō*. |
| "The kids shall guard the cheese": *Līberī cāseum custōdītō.* | ***Līberī cāseum custōdiuntō.*** "They shall": *-ntō*. |
| A law, "One shall not touch the cheese": *Cāseum nōn tangitō.* | ***Cāseum nē tangitō.*** Laws forbid with *nē*. |

## 15. Lectiō: *Lēgēs Aviae*

:: Dūra lēx, sed lēx. || The law is hard, but it is the law. (later legal saying)

Lēx prīma: cāseum nēmō tangitō.

Diē quīntā decimā: the evening after Chapter 46. The family has walked home from Rome, and Grandma's other cheese sits on the table. Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English. As you read, hunt every *-tō* and *-ntō*, every *nē*, and every *nōlī*. New in passing: *quīntus decimus*, fifteenth; *Duodecim Tabulae*, the Twelve Tables; *iniūstus*, unjust (Chapter 46's *iūstus*); *terō*, grind; *violō*, break (a law); *tetigī*, I touched (perfect of *tangō*); *dūrus*, hard. Old friends: *redeō* (Chapter 32), *similis* + dative (Chapter 44), *appropinquō* (Chapter 44), *pāreō* (Chapter 44), *fenestra* (Chapter 8), *merīdiē* (Chapter 23), *perītus* + genitive (Chapter 46), and Chapter 45's *ūnā hōrā post*.

Avia | Audīte, omnēs! Herī cāseus meus in stomachō canis erat. Hodiē lēgēs scrībam, Duodecim Tabulīs similēs! || Grandma | Listen, everyone! Yesterday my cheese was in the dog's stomach. Today I'll write laws, like the Twelve Tables!

{#g-15}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Grandma's six laws use *tangitō, manētō, appropinquātō, cantātō, custōdiuntō, dormītō*. Which one is "they shall"? | ***Custōdiuntō*** (*-ntō*: the children). The rest are "he (anyone) shall." |
| *Cāseum nēmō tangitō · Avus cāseō nē appropinquātō.* Two ways to forbid. And why *cāseō*? | ***Nēmō*** + *-tō*: "no one shall." ***Nē*** + *-tō*: "shall not." *Cāseō* is dative: Chapter 44's *appropinquō*, "come near **to**." |
| Grandma: *Salūs cāseī suprēma lēx estō!* Whose line is she borrowing? | **Cicero's** consul law, *On the Laws* 3.8: *salūs populī suprēma lēx estō*, with the cheese in place of the people. |
| Grandma reads Cato: *Lībum hoc modō facitō.* Why a "shall" command in a cookbook? | A recipe is for **whenever** you cook: the Law of Later and Forever. |
| Grandpa's defense, and Grandma's answer. Who wins? | Grandpa: law three says **cheese**, and this is a **cake**. Grandma: the cake was **made from cheese** (*ē cāseō factum est*), so law six applies. *Dūra lēx, sed lēx*: Grandpa loses. |
| *Nōlīte timēre, līberī · Nē cantātō!* Which styles of "don't"? | *Nōlīte* + infinitive: talk. *Nē* + *-tō*: law style; the family quotes law four back at Dad. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Nōlī timēre; loquere! || Don't be afraid; speak! (teaching line)

1. Forbid five things to one person and to y'all with *nōlī / nōlīte*: *Nōlī rīdēre! Nōlīte dormīre!…*
2. Chant the "shall" command of *amō, habeō, dūcō, capiō, audiō*: you, he, y'all, they.
3. Chant *estō, estōte, suntō*; *ītō, euntō*; *fertō*; *mementō, mementōte*; *scītō*.
4. The three Laws in your own words: the "Shall" Command, Later and Forever, the Five Don'ts.
5. Write and say six house rules, three with *-tō* and three with *nē … -tō* (*Canis forīs manētō · Pater nē cantātō…*).
6. Say "Don't be afraid" in every style: *Nōlī timēre · Nē timētō · Nē timē · Nōn timēbis · Nē timeās* (the last as a chunk).
7. Give three orders for later with *sī … -tō* (*Sī Rōmam ībis, cāseum emitō*).
8. The real lines: *Nōlīte timēre · Habēte fīdūciam, ego sum; nōlīte timēre · Nōlī mē tangere · Estōte ergō vōs perfectī · Mementō meī · Salūs populī suprēma lēx estō · Lībum hoc modō facitō · In urbe nē sepelītō nēve ūritō · Equō nē crēdite, Teucrī · Tū nē cēde malīs, sed contrā audentior ītō · Nōn occīdēs.* Then read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part, and rebuild it from the English.
