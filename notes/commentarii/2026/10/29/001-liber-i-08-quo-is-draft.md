# Caput VIII: Quō īs?

*Liber I, Prīma Verba · Chapter 8*

*In which everybody is going somewhere: to the forum, into the kitchen, across the street, home, even to Rome. One tiny verb gets you moving, and Chapter 4's object ending turns out to be the "where to" ending too.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Quō īs?* · *Ad forum eō.* · *In culīnam currō.* · *Domum īmus!* · *Rōmae habitō.*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** place where, place to which, place from which; locative (the "at" case)
    - **Forms:** *eō, īs, it, īmus, ītis, eunt* (as a chant); *ad, in, per, circum, trāns* + accusative; *in culīnā* vs *in culīnam*; *domum, domī, domō*; *Rōmam, Rōmae, Rōmā*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Switch, the Law of the Target Words, the Law of Home and Rome
    - **Words:** school, temple, baths, woods, window, *quō, usque*
    - **Reading:** Where are you going?
    - **Speaking:** ask and answer "where are you going?"

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. This time, move: walk into the kitchen as you say *in culīnam*, and stand still for *in culīnā*.

---

## 1. *Quō?*

:: Quō vādis? || Where are you going? (the Latin Bible, John 13:36)

Marcus currit. Ubi est? Nōn hīc. Quō currit?

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 7 gave you two question words for place: *ubi?* and *unde?* What does each ask? | *Ubi?* "Where?" (sitting still). *Unde?* "From where?" |
| One is missing: "where to?" Latin has it: *quō?* Say it. | KWOH, a long ō. Older English had all three: *where* (*ubi*), *whence* (*unde*), *whither* (*quō*). |
| The most useful question with *quō*: *Quō īs?* | KWOH EES? "Where are you going?" *Īs* is "you go": a long ī plus Chapter 5's "you" ending *-s* (*ambulās, venīs, es*). |
| A Roman answers: *Eō ad hortum.* Which word is "I go"? | *Eō.* E-oh, two syllables, ending in Chapter 5's "I" ending *-ō*. |
| *Ad* means "to, toward" (think "advent," a coming-to). And look: *hortum*, not *hortō*. Which form is *hortum*? | The object form, the accusative (Chapter 4): *Marcus hortum videt.* |
| So: *Ubi es? In hortō. · Unde venīs? Ex hortō. · Quō īs? Ad hortum.* What's the pattern? | "Where" and "from where" take the ablative. "Where to" takes the **accusative**. |
| The motto, *Quō vādis?*, asks the same thing with another verb (*vādō*, "I go, I march"). Where's it from? | The Latin Bible (John 13:36): Peter asks Jesus *Domine, quō vādis?* "Lord, where are you going?" Later a famous novel and film. |

## 2. *Eō, īs, it*

:: Īte, missa est. || Go, it is the dismissal. (end of the Latin Mass)

Ego eō. Tū īs. Marcus it. Nōs īmus!

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Eō* "I go," *īs* "you go." Guess "he goes, she goes." | *It.* Short i. *Marcus it*: Marcus goes. (Yes: in Latin, *it* goes.) |
| "We go"? Chapter 5's ending is *-mus*. | *Īmus.* EE-mus. |
| "Y'all go"? | *Ītis.* EE-tis. |
| "They go" is the odd one: *eunt*. Say it. | E-unt, two syllables. Like *eō*, it starts with *e-*. |
| Line up all six. | *eō, īs, it, īmus, ītis, eunt*: I go, you go, he/she goes, we go, y'all go, they go. Learn it as a chant for now; Chapter 32 takes this tiny verb apart. |
| *Puerī ad forum ___.* (go) | *Eunt.* |
| The command "Go!" is one letter: *Ī!* And to y'all? | *Īte!* Chapter 1's *-te* law again. |
| The motto: *Īte, missa est.* Heard it before? | The priest's dismissal at the end of the Latin Mass: "Go, it is the dismissal." English "Mass" comes from *missa*. |
| "To go" (the infinitive, Chapter 6's "to" form)? | *Īre.* EE-re. So *Nōlī īre!*: "Don't go!" |

## 3. *Ad forum*

:: Ab ōvō usque ad māla. || From the egg all the way to the apples: start to finish. (Horace, *Satires* 1.3.6–7)

Ad forum eō. Ad tabernam īmus. Ad mēnsam venīte!

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Ad hortum*: the accusative after *ad*. "To the shop"? | *Ad tabernam.* |
| Dinner's ready: "Come to the table, y'all!" | *Ad mēnsam venīte!* |
| "To the shops"? | *Ad tabernās.* Chapter 4's plural accusative *-ās*. |
| *Lūdus*: school (also "game"; LOO-dus). "I'm going to school." | *Ad lūdum eō.* |
| *Thermae*: the public baths, a word that is always plural. "We're going to the baths." | *Ad thermās īmus.* TER-mahs: th is t with a puff. |
| *Ad* works with people too. "They're going to Marcus" (to his place). | *Ad Marcum eunt.* |
| Does *ad* take you inside? | Not necessarily. *Ad* is "to, toward, up to." *Ad tabernam*: to the shop, maybe just its door. Inside is section 4. |
| Chapter 7 left a loose end: *ab ōvō usque ad māla*. Decode *ad māla*. | "To the apples." *Māla* is the accusative plural of *mālum*; for *-um* words it looks just like the naming form (Chapter 4). Long ā: apples. Short *mala* would be "bad things," a much worse dessert. |
| Now the whole motto. *Usque* means "all the way." | *Ab ōvō* (ablative: from the egg) *usque ad māla* (accusative: all the way to the apples). A Roman dinner opened with eggs and ended with fruit: start to finish. |

## 4. *In culīnam*

:: In culīnā sedeō; in culīnam currō. || I sit in the kitchen; I run into the kitchen.

Iūlia in culīnā est. Marcus in culīnam currit!

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 7: *Iūlia in culīnā est.* Where is she? | In the kitchen. *In* + ablative: sitting still. |
| Now: *Marcus in culīnam currit.* What changed? | *Culīnam*, with *-am*: the accusative. Marcus is running *into* the kitchen. *In* + accusative = "into." |
| One little word, two meanings. What decides? | The ending after it. Ablative: in, on (where). Accusative: into, onto (where to). |
| "I'm going into the garden." | *In hortum eō.* |
| *In hortō ambulō* vs *in hortum ambulō*? | "I'm walking (around) in the garden" vs "I'm walking into the garden." Same verb; the ending changes the picture. |
| "I carry the apples onto the table." | *Māla in mēnsam portō.* Onto: motion, so accusative. |
| "The apples are on the table." | *Māla in mēnsā sunt.* |
| *Templum*: temple. "They go into the temple." | *In templum eunt.* |
| Chapter 7's error card: *In hortōs sumus* was wrong. Why? | *Sumus*, "we are," is sitting still, so it needs the ablative: *in hortīs sumus*. |
| Now make *in hortōs* right by changing only the verb: "We run into the gardens." | *In hortōs currimus.* Motion makes it perfect Latin. |
| Marcus's favorite direction: "Cheese, into the stomach!" | *Cāseus in stomachum!* |

!!! success "The Law of the Switch"
    *In* has two faces, and the ending tells you which:

    | | ending | means | example |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | *in* + **ablative** | *-ā, -ō, -īs* | in, on (where) | *in culīnā sedeō* |
    | *in* + **accusative** | *-am, -um, -ās, -ōs, -a* | into, onto (where to) | *in culīnam currō* |

    Sitting still: ablative. Going in: accusative.

## 5. *Per, circum, trāns*

:: Per aspera ad astra. || Through hardships to the stars. (proverb)

Per viās ambulāmus. Circum mēnsam currimus!

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Per* means "through" (think "perforate"). *Per hortum ambulō.* | "I walk through the garden." Accusative again: *hortum*. |
| *Silva*: woods, forest (think "sylvan"). "We walk through the woods." | *Per silvam ambulāmus.* |
| *Fenestra*: window. "Marcus looks through the window." | *Marcus per fenestram spectat.* |
| *Circum*: "around" (think "circle"). "The boys run around the table." | *Puerī circum mēnsam currunt.* |
| *Trāns*: "across" (think "transatlantic"). Long ā. "Across the street"? | *Trāns viam.* trahns WI-am. |
| The motto: *Per aspera ad astra.* Find the two prepositions and their case. | *Per* and *ad*, both with the accusative: *aspera* ("rough things") and *astra* ("stars") are neuter plurals in *-a*. "Through hardships to the stars." |
| Do all of these take the accusative only when moving? | No. *Ad, per, circum, trāns* always take the accusative. *Circum mēnsam sedent*: "they sit around the table," no motion, still accusative. Only *in* switches. |

!!! success "The Law of the Target Words"
    These take the **accusative**:

    | word | means | example |
    |---|---|---|
    | *ad* | to, toward | *ad forum* |
    | *in* (going) | into, onto | *in culīnam* |
    | *per* | through | *per viās* |
    | *circum* | around | *circum mēnsam* |
    | *trāns* | across | *trāns viam* |

    Chapter 7's five little words (*in, ex, ab, cum, sine*) take the ablative. *In* is on both lists; its ending decides.

!!! info "Grammar words, in plain English"
    Old-school grammars name three place questions:

    - **Place where** (*ubi?*): where something is. Ablative: *in culīnā*.
    - **Place to which** (*quō?*): where something is going, also called "motion toward." Accusative: *in culīnam, ad forum*.
    - **Place from which** (*unde?*): where something comes from. Ablative: *ē culīnā, ā forō*.

## 6. *Domum*

:: Domī sum. || I'm at home.

Domum eō. Domī edō. Domī dormiō.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 7 promised a rule-breaker: *domus*, "home, house" (think "domestic"). DOM-us, short o. "I'm going home." Guess: *ad domum eō*? | Romans just say *Domum eō.* No preposition at all! |
| *Domum* already means "(to) home." Which case does its ending look like? | The accusative, *-um* like *hortum*: the "where to" case, working alone. |
| "Go home!" To one, then to y'all. | *Ī domum! Īte domum!* (Word order is free: *Domum ī!* works too.) |
| "At home" is *domī*, DOM-ee. "I'm at home." | *Domī sum.* No *in*. |
| "We're staying home today." | *Hodiē domī manēmus.* |
| "From home" is *domō*, again with no *ex*. "I'm coming from home." | *Domō veniō.* |
| *Domī* ends in *-ī*, but it doesn't mean "houses." What is it? | A leftover "at" form called the **locative**. Latin kept it in a few words like *domī* and in the names of towns (next section). So: *domum, domī, domō*, three chunks. (*Domus* is feminine; its full set of endings mixes two families and waits for Chapter 23.) |

## 7. *Rōmam!*

:: Omnēs viae Rōmam dūcunt. || All roads lead to Rome. (medieval proverb)

Rōma magna est. Rōmam īmus!

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Rōma*: Rome. ROH-ma, long ō. "I'm going to Rome." Guess: *ad Rōmam eō*? | Romans say *Rōmam eō*, just like *domum eō*. A town's name needs no preposition. |
| The motto: *Omnēs viae Rōmam dūcunt.* Which word is "to Rome"? | *Rōmam*, a plain accusative. *Viae*: roads. *Dūcunt*: lead (Chapter 6's *dūcō*). *Omnēs*, "all," belongs to a family you meet in Book II. |
| "We live in Rome." Guess from *domī*: no *in*, and a special "at" form. | *Rōmae habitāmus.* ROH-mye: "at Rome," the locative again. It looks like a plural (*mēnsae*), but there's only one Rome. |
| "I'm coming from Rome." No *ex* or *ab*. | *Rōmā veniō.* Ablative, long ā. |
| *Capua* is a city south of Rome. To Capua, at Capua, from Capua? | *Capuam, Capuae, Capuā.* |
| *Tarentum* (a city in southern Italy) is an *-um* word. "At Tarentum" ends just like *domī*. | *Tarentī.* To Tarentum: *Tarentum*. From Tarentum: *Tarentō*. |
| *Ad Rōmam* isn't wrong Latin. What would it mean? | "Toward Rome, to the area near Rome," like an army camping outside the walls. Into the city itself: just *Rōmam*. |
| Which nouns skip the preposition? | Names of cities and towns, and *domus* (plus a couple more, later). Everything else keeps it: *ad forum, in hortum, ex oppidō, in culīnā*. |

!!! success "The Law of Home and Rome"
    Town names and *domus* use the bare case, **no preposition**:

    | | where to (*quō?*) | where (*ubi?*) | from where (*unde?*) |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | home | *domum* | *domī* | *domō* |
    | Rome | *Rōmam* | *Rōmae* | *Rōmā* |
    | Tarentum | *Tarentum* | *Tarentī* | *Tarentō* |

    The middle column is the **locative**, the "at" case: *-ae* for *-a* towns, *-ī* for *-us/-um* towns and *domus*. (Towns with plural names, like *Athēnae*, work differently; later.)

!!! info "Grammar words, in plain English"
    **Locative** (from *locus*, "place"): an old "at" case. Most nouns lost it and use *in* + ablative instead; it survives in town names (*Rōmae, Tarentī*) and a few words like *domī*. It looks like other forms, so let the meaning guide you.

## 8. *Quō? Ubi? Unde?*

:: Nusquam est quī ubīque est. || Whoever is everywhere is nowhere. (Seneca, *Letters* 2.2)

Quō īs? Ubi es? Unde venīs?

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Marcus in culīnam currit.* *Quō, ubi,* or *unde?* | *Quō*: into the kitchen. |
| *Iūlia ē tabernā venit.* | *Unde*: out of the shop. |
| *Puellae in hortō sedent.* | *Ubi*: in the garden. |
| *Rōmae habitō.* | *Ubi*: at Rome. |
| *Domum īmus.* | *Quō*: home. |
| *Domō venītis.* | *Unde*: from home. |
| *Quō īs?* Answer three ways: to the forum, into the garden, home. | *Ad forum eō. In hortum eō. Domum eō.* |
| The motto, *nusquam est quī ubīque est*: which question word hides inside *ubīque*? | *Ubi!* *Ubīque* is "everywhere." This *-que* isn't "and" (Chapter 3); it turns "where" into "every-where." Note the long ī. *Nusquam*: nowhere. Seneca's advice: stop running everywhere, settle somewhere. |

## 9. Lectiō: *Quō īs?*

:: Quō īs? Ad tabernam! || Where are you going? To the shop!

Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English.

::: dialogue lectio
Iūlia | Salvē, Iōannēs! Quō īs? || Iūlia | Hi, John! Where are you going?
Iōannēs | Ad tabernam eō. Cāseum emō. || John | I'm going to the shop. I'm buying cheese.
Iūlia | Iterum? Ubi est cāseus? || Iūlia | Again? Where's the cheese?
Iōannēs | Marcus! Semper Marcus. || John | Marcus! Always Marcus.
Iūlia | Ego quoque ad forum eō. Venī mēcum! || Iūlia | I'm going to the forum too. Come with me!
Iōannēs | Bene. Per viās īmus. || John | Good. We go through the streets.
Iūlia | Ecce Cornēlia! Salvē, Cornēlia! Quō īs? || Iūlia | Look, Cornēlia! Hi, Cornēlia! Where are you going?
Cornēlia | Rōmam eō! || Cornēlia | I'm going to Rome!
Iōannēs | Rōmam? Longa via est! || John | To Rome? That's a long road!
Cornēlia | Longa, sed Rōmae tabernae optimae sunt. || Cornēlia | Long, but at Rome the shops are the best.
Iūlia | Et Marcus? Ubi est? || Iūlia | And Marcus? Where is he?
Cornēlia | Marcus domī est. Dormit. || Cornēlia | Marcus is at home. He's asleep.
Iōannēs | Domī dormit, et cāseus in stomachō est! || John | He's sleeping at home, and the cheese is in his stomach!
Cornēlia | Rīdeō! Valēte! || Cornēlia | Ha! Bye, y'all!
Iūlia | Valē! Nunc in tabernam īmus. || Iūlia | Bye! Now we go into the shop.
Iōannēs | Cāseum magnum emō. || John | I'm buying a big cheese.
Iūlia | Et ego pira et ūvās emō. || Iūlia | And I'm buying pears and grapes.
Iōannēs | Nunc domum! Per hortum et trāns viam. || John | Now home! Through the garden and across the street.
Marcus | Salvēte! Quid portātis? || Marcus | Hi, y'all! What are you carrying?
Iōannēs | Cāseum! Cāseus in mēnsam it, nōn in stomachum! || John | Cheese! The cheese goes onto the table, not into your stomach!
Marcus | Bene, bene. Ad mēnsam currō! || Marcus | OK, OK. I'm running to the table!
Iūlia | Ab ōvō usque ad māla? Hodiē ā cāseō usque ad cāseum! || Iūlia | From the egg to the apples? Today it's from cheese all the way to cheese!
:::

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Ad tabernam eō.* *Quō, ubi,* or *unde?* | *Quō*: to the shop. |
| *Rōmae tabernae optimae sunt.* Is *Rōmae* "Romes"? | No: "at Rome," the locative. *Tabernae* is the subject. |
| *Marcus domī est.* Why no *in*? | *Domī* already means "at home." |
| *Nunc in tabernam īmus.* Compare Chapter 7's *ē tabernā*. | Into the shop (accusative) vs out of the shop (ablative). |
| *Per hortum et trāns viam*: which case, and why? | Accusative, *hortum* and *viam*: *per* and *trāns* always take it. |
| *Cāseus in mēnsam it, nōn in stomachum!* Why accusatives? | The cheese is going somewhere: onto the table, not into Marcus. |
| *Ā cāseō usque ad cāseum.* Unpack it. | From cheese (ablative) all the way to cheese (accusative): Horace's dinner, all cheese. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking.

1. Walk around your house and narrate: *In culīnam eō. In culīnā sum. Ē culīnā veniō.* Do it for three rooms.
2. Chant the verb five times, stepping on each form: *eō, īs, it, īmus, ītis, eunt.*
3. Ask and answer *Quō īs?* five ways: to the forum, to the shop, into the garden, home, to Rome.
4. Flip the switch, out loud: *in hortō → in hortum · in mēnsā → in mēnsam · in viā → in viam · in tabernīs → in tabernās.*
5. Home and Rome, no prepositions: *Domum eō. Domī sum. Domō veniō. · Rōmam eō. Rōmae sum. Rōmā veniō.*
6. Give orders: *Ī domum! Īte ad mēnsam! Nōlī īre!*
7. Say the mottos and point at each accusative: *Ab ōvō usque ad māla. · Per aspera ad astra. · Omnēs viae Rōmam dūcunt.*
8. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing all four parts. Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
