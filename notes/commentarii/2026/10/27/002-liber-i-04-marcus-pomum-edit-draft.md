# Caput IV: Marcus pōmum edit

*Liber I, Prīma Verba · Chapter 4*

*In which people finally do things: Marcus eats, Iūlia drinks, the farmer sells, and every noun that gets eaten, bought, or loved puts on a new ending to show it. We meet the accusative.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Marcus mālum edit.* · *Puellae ūvās edunt.* · *Quid bibit Iūlia? Aquam bibit.*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** verb, subject, object, case, accusative
    - **Forms:** the accusative *-am, -um* (one) and *-ās, -ōs, -a* (many); verbs in *-at, -et, -it* (one person does it) and *-ant, -ent, -unt* (several do it)
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Object, the Law of the Neuter Twins, the Law of Endings over Order
    - **Words:** eating, drinking, buying, selling, carrying, seeing, loving
    - **Reading:** people-watching at the market
    - **Speaking:** say who eats (or drinks, or buys) what

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as before. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check.

---

## 1. Somebody does something

:: Ēsse oportet ut vīvās, nōn vīvere ut edās. || Eat to live, don't live to eat. (Rhētorica ad Herennium 4.39)

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Marcus edit.* Who is Marcus? | A Roman boy. MAR-kus. |
| What is he doing? | Eating. *Edit* means "he eats" or "he is eating." E-dit, short e. |
| What English word comes from *edere*, "to eat"? | Edible: "eatable." |
| *Iūlia bibit.* What is Iūlia doing? | Drinking. *Bibit*, "she drinks." BI-bit. |
| What English words come from *bibere*? | Imbibe, and the rarer bibulous (fond of drink). |
| *Edit* and *bibit*: what kind of word is each? | A doing word. In grammar terms, a **verb**. |
| Have you met a verb before? | Yes: *est*, "is." A verb doesn't have to be busy. Being counts. |
| Does *edit* mean "he eats," "she eats," or "it eats"? | Any of them. The *-t* means "one person or thing (not me, not you) does it." The context says who. |
| So *edit* alone is a whole sentence? | Yes: "He's eating." Latin can leave the "he" out. |
| *Marcus edit.* Who does the eating? | Marcus. The one who does the verb is the **subject**. |
| What case is Marcus in? | Nominative, the naming case. The subject is always nominative. |
| Does *edit* mean "eats," "is eating," or "does eat"? | All three. Latin has one present tense where English has three. |

!!! info "Grammar words, in plain English"
    **Verb** (*verbum*, "word"): a doing or being word. *edit, bibit, est*

    **Subject**: who or what does the verb. It's in the nominative. ***Marcus** edit.*

## 2. Something gets eaten

:: Canis canīnam nōn ēst. || A dog doesn't eat dog. (old proverb, quoted by Varro, Dē Linguā Latīnā 7.31)

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Marcus eats an apple. Try: *Marcus mālum edit.* What does Marcus eat? | *Mālum*, an apple. |
| What is the grammar term for the thing a verb is done to? | The **object** (or direct object). The apple gets eaten. |
| Now Marcus eats a pear (*pirum*). | *Marcus pirum edit.* |
| Now Marcus eats a grape. Try: *Marcus ūva edit*? | Close, but no. *Marcus ūvam edit.* |
| What changed? | *Ūva* became *ūvam*. It grew an *-m*. |
| Why? | The grape is the object now, not the subject. Latin marks the object with a new ending. |
| Marcus eats cheese (*cāseus*). | *Marcus cāseum edit.* |
| What changed in *cāseus*? | *-us* became *-um*. |
| Marcus eats an egg (*ōvum*). | *Marcus ōvum edit.* |
| Did *ōvum* change? | No. It already ended in *-um*. Hold that thought. |
| What do *ūvam, cāseum, ōvum* all end in? | *-m*. |
| These are all one case, the "object case." What is its name? | The **accusative**. AK-kyoo-za-tiv in English. |
| Why "accusative"? Nobody is accused. | A Roman translation slip. The Greek name meant "the case of the thing affected," but the Greek word for "cause" also meant "charge, accusation," and the Romans picked the wrong sense. The name stuck. |
| The proverb above ends in *ēst*, long e. Is that "is"? | No! It's an old, shorter way to say *edit*, "eats." Romans had both. You only need *edit*. (The very first motto's *ēsse* is the same verb: "to eat," not "to be.") |

!!! success "The Law of the Object"
    The thing a verb is done to goes in the **accusative**. In the singular, it ends in ***-m***: *ūvam, cāseum, ōvum*.

    The subject stays nominative: ***Marcus** cāseum edit.*

## 3. One object, three families

:: Manus manum lavat. || One hand washes the other. (Petronius)

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Feminine *-a* nouns: *mēnsa → ?* | *Mēnsam.* |
| Masculine *-us* nouns: *stilus → ?* | *Stilum.* |
| Neuter *-um* nouns: *vīnum → ?* | *Vīnum.* No change. |
| Masculine *-er* nouns: *puer → ?* | *Puerum.* It keeps its e, like *puerī*. |
| *Liber → ?* | *Librum.* It drops its e, like *librī*. |
| *Magister → ?* | *Magistrum.* |
| *Agricola* is masculine. Its accusative? | *Agricolam.* It follows its spelling here, not its gender: *-a* nouns take *-am*. |
| So the noun's ending follows its **pattern**. What follows the noun's **gender**? | The adjective. *Agricolam bonum.* Same rule as Chapter 3. |
| Iūlia drinks water. | *Iūlia aquam bibit.* |
| Iūlia drinks cold water. | *Iūlia aquam frīgidam bibit.* |
| Marcus eats salty cheese. | *Marcus cāseum salsum edit.* |
| Marcus eats a red apple. | *Marcus mālum rubrum edit.* |
| Iūlia drinks dark wine. | *Iūlia vīnum nigrum bibit.* |
| Back to the motto: *Manus manum lavat.* Which is the subject? Which is the object? | *Manus* (a hand) is the subject. *Manum* is the object. A hand washes a hand. |
| *Lavat* means "washes." Any English cousin? | Lavatory (a washing room), laundry, and lotion. |

## 4. Many objects

:: In vīnō vēritās. || In wine, truth. (proverb)

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Marcus eats grapes, lots of them. *Ūvae* is the plural subject form. As an object? | *Marcus ūvās edit.* |
| What is the ending? | *-ās*, long ā. The *-a* family plural object. |
| Marcus eats cheeses (*cāseī*). | *Marcus cāseōs edit.* |
| What is the ending? | *-ōs*, long ō. The *-us* family plural object. |
| Marcus eats eggs (*ōva*). | *Marcus ōva edit.* |
| Did *ōva* change? | No. Neuter plural stays *-a*. |
| Two neuter forms never changed: *ōvum* and *ōva*. Pattern? | In the neuter, the subject form and the object form are always the same. |
| So *Vīnum bonum est* and *Iūlia vīnum bonum bibit*: same spelling of *vīnum bonum*? | Yes. First it's nominative, then accusative. Same clothes, different job. |
| Marcus eats apples. | *Marcus māla edit.* |
| Marcus eats small pears. | *Marcus pira parva edit.* |
| Iūlia eats white grapes. | *Iūlia ūvās albās edit.* |
| The teacher sees the boys. "Sees" is *videt*. | *Magistra puerōs videt.* WI-det. |
| The teacher sees the books. | *Magistra librōs videt.* |
| The farmer sees the sailors. | *Agricola nautās videt.* |
| What English words come from *vidēre*? | Video ("I see"), vision, evident. |

!!! success "The Law of the Neuter Twins"
    A neuter noun's **nominative** and **accusative** are always twins. *ōvum · ōvum · ōva · ōva*

!!! success "The accusative so far"
    | | *-a* nouns | *-us* / *-er* nouns | *-um* nouns |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | one, subject | *ūva* | *cāseus, puer* | *ōvum* |
    | one, object | *ūvam* | *cāseum, puerum* | *ōvum* |
    | many, subject | *ūvae* | *cāseī, puerī* | *ōva* |
    | many, object | *ūvās* | *cāseōs, puerōs* | *ōva* |

## 5. Endings over order

:: Omnia vincit Amor. || Love conquers all. (Virgil)

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Loves" is *amat*. *Puer puellam amat.* Who loves whom? | The boy loves the girl. *Puer* is nominative, *puellam* is accusative. |
| *Puella puerum amat.* | The girl loves the boy. |
| *Puellam puer amat.* Who loves whom? | Still: the boy loves the girl. *Puer* is still the subject. |
| What changed? | Only the order. Now "the girl" gets the weight: "It's the girl the boy loves." |
| *Amat puellam puer*? | Same meaning again. Unusual order, still correct. Poets do it all the time. |
| In English, what tells you who loves whom? | Word order: "the boy loves the girl" vs "the girl loves the boy." |
| In Latin? | The endings. |
| What's the usual Latin order, though? | Subject, object, verb: *Marcus mālum edit.* The verb likes to go last. |
| *Mālum Marcus edit.* Who eats what? | Marcus eats the apple. *Marcus* can only be nominative, so *mālum* must be the object. (And apples don't eat boys.) |
| Back to the motto: *Omnia vincit Amor.* Which is the subject? | *Amor*, Love. *Omnia* (all things) is the object, a neuter plural, which is why it ends in *-a*. |
| What's the English word "amateur" from? | *Amāre*, to love. An amateur does it for love, not money. |

!!! success "The Law of Endings over Order"
    In Latin the **endings** say who does what. Order adds emphasis, not meaning. When the endings can't decide, sense does.

## 6. Who and what: asking about the object

:: Quid novī? || What's new?

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Quis edit?* | "Who is eating?" Answer: *Marcus edit.* |
| *Quid edit Marcus?* | "What is Marcus eating?" Answer: *Mālum edit.* |
| In Chapter 2, *quid* was nominative (*quid est?*). Here it's the object. Why no new form? | *Quid* is neuter. Its nominative and accusative are twins. The Law of the Neuter Twins, again. |
| *Quid bibit Iūlia?* (water) | *Aquam bibit.* |
| Why *aquam* and not *aqua*? | The answer keeps the case of the thing asked about. Iūlia drinks it, so it's the object. |
| *Quid bibit agricola?* (wine) | *Vīnum bibit.* |
| *Quem videt Marcus?* (Iūlia) | *Iūliam videt.* |
| What is *quem*? | "Whom?" The accusative of *quis*. Just recognize it for now. |
| *Edit Marcus ōva?* Make it a yes/no question. | *Editne Marcus ōva?* "Is Marcus eating eggs?" |
| Answer no. | *Minimē, Marcus ōva nōn edit.* |
| *Nōnne Iūlia aquam bibit?* What answer is expected? | Yes. *Ita, aquam bibit.* |
| *Num agricola librōs edit?* What answer is expected? | No. *Minimē! Agricola librōs nōn edit.* |

## 7. More verbs, three families

:: Age quod agis. || Do what you're doing. (proverb: keep your mind on the task)

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Carries" is *portat*. The sailor carries wine. | *Nauta vīnum portat.* |
| What English words come from *portāre*? | Porter, portable, transport. |
| "Prepares" is *parat*. The girl prepares dinner. *Cēna* is dinner. | *Puella cēnam parat.* |
| "Has" is *habet*. The farmer has apples. | *Agricola māla habet.* HA-bet. |
| "Habit" comes from it. How? | A habit is how you "have" yourself, your condition. |
| "Buys" is *emit*. Iūlia buys cheese. | *Iūlia cāseum emit.* E-mit. |
| "Sells" is *vendit*. The farmer sells grapes. | *Agricola ūvās vendit.* WEN-dit. |
| What English word comes from *vendere*? | Vendor, someone who sells. |
| "Reads" is *legit*. The teacher reads a book. | *Magister librum legit.* LE-git. |
| "Laughs, smiles" is *rīdet*. Does *rīdet* take an object? | Not here. *Iūlia rīdet*: "Iūlia laughs." Some verbs don't need one. |
| Sort the vowel before the *-t*: *amat, portat, parat, lavat*. | *-at*. |
| *habet, videt, rīdet*. | *-et*. |
| *edit, bibit, emit, vendit, legit*. | *-it*. |
| Must you know which family a verb belongs to? | Not yet. Learn each verb as it comes. Chapter 5 sorts them. |

## 8. Several people do it

:: Ede, bibe, lūde. || Eat, drink, play. (old Latin motto, after the Greek epitaph of the Assyrian king Sardanapalus)

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Marcus and Iūlia both eat. *Marcus et Iūlia edunt.* What changed in the verb? | *-it* became *-unt*. E-dunt. |
| What does the *-nt* signal? | "They": more than one person or thing does it. Compare *est / sunt*. |
| The girls drink water. | *Puellae aquam bibunt.* BI-bunt. |
| The boys love apples. | *Puerī māla amant.* |
| What did *amat* become? | *Amant*. The a stays: *-at* becomes *-ant*. |
| The farmers have grapes. | *Agricolae ūvās habent.* |
| What did *habet* become? | *Habent*: *-et* becomes *-ent*. |
| The sailors buy wine. | *Nautae vīnum emunt.* |
| What did *emit* become? | *Emunt*. The *-it* verbs don't make *-int*; they make *-unt*. |
| The teachers read books. | *Magistrī librōs legunt.* |
| The students see the teacher. | *Discipulī magistrum vident.* |
| The farmers sell eggs. | *Agricolae ōva vendunt.* |
| Why *ōva* and not *ōvās*? | Neuter twins: the object form is the same as the subject form, *ōva*. |
| One farmer sells eggs. | *Agricola ōva vendit.* Only the verb changes for one or many sellers. The eggs stay *ōva*. |

!!! success "Verbs: one does it, several do it"
    | one does it | several do it |
    |---|---|
    | *amat* | *amant* |
    | *habet* | *habent* |
    | *edit* | *edunt* |

    The verb agrees with its **subject** in number: *Puer edit. Puerī edunt.*

## 9. Lectiō: People-watching at the market

:: Lege, et intellege. || Read, and understand.

Read each Latin line aloud. Its English sits beside it, under a small wax tablet: guess first, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English.

::: dialogue lectio
Iūlia | Ecce Marcus! || Iūlia | Look, it's Marcus!
Iōannēs | Quid agit Marcus? || John | What's Marcus doing?
Iūlia | Marcus pōma emit. || Iūlia | Marcus is buying fruit.
Iōannēs | Quid emit? || John | What's he buying?
Iūlia | Māla rubra et ūvās nigrās emit. || Iūlia | He's buying red apples and dark grapes.
Iōannēs | Et agricola? || John | And the farmer?
Iūlia | Agricola pōma vendit et pecūniam habet. Agricola rīdet. || Iūlia | The farmer sells fruit and has the money. The farmer is smiling.
Iōannēs | Ecce Cornēlia! Cornēlia cāseum salsum portat. || John | Look, Cornēlia! Cornēlia is carrying salty cheese.
Iūlia | Marcus Cornēliam videt. || Iūlia | Marcus sees Cornēlia.
Iōannēs | Videtne Cornēlia Marcum? || John | Does Cornēlia see Marcus?
Iūlia | Ita! Cornēlia Marcum videt et rīdet. || Iūlia | Yes! Cornēlia sees Marcus and smiles.
Iōannēs | Quid nunc agunt? || John | What are they doing now?
Iūlia | Māla edunt et vīnum bibunt. || Iūlia | They're eating apples and drinking wine.
Iōannēs | Num aquam bibunt? || John | They're not drinking water, are they?
Iūlia | Minimē! Vīnum nigrum bibunt. || Iūlia | Not at all! They're drinking red wine.
Iōannēs | Marcus Cornēliam amat! || John | Marcus loves Cornēlia!
Iūlia | Et Cornēlia vīnum amat. || Iūlia | And Cornēlia loves wine.
:::

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| What is *ecce*? | "Look! Here's…!" It points at something. The thing pointed at stays nominative: *Ecce Marcus!* |
| *Quid agit Marcus?* You've met *quid agis?* What's the link? | *Agere* is "to do." *Quid agis?* is "how are you doing?"; *quid agit?* is "what's he doing?" |
| Why *ūvās nigrās*? | Feminine plural object: the noun takes *-ās*, and so does its adjective. |
| Why *māla rubra*, not *māla rubrās*? | *Māla* is neuter plural: twins, *-a* in both cases. |
| *Pecūnia* is "money." Why *pecūniam*? | The farmer has it: object. "Pecuniary" comes from it. |
| *Marcus Cornēliam videt.* Who sees whom? | Marcus sees Cornēlia. *Cornēliam* is the object. |
| Say "Cornēlia sees Marcus." | *Cornēlia Marcum videt.* |
| Why *agunt*, *edunt*, *bibunt*? | Two people (Marcus and Cornēlia) are the subject. |
| What is *nunc*? | "Now." |
| *Vīnum nigrum* is translated "red wine." Why? | Chapter 3: Romans called dark red wine "black." |

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking.

1. At your next meal, narrate yourself in the third person: *Iōannēs ōva edit. Iōannēs aquam bibit.* (Leave bread out for now: *pānis* belongs to a different family, coming in Book II.)
2. Run the object forms aloud: *ūva, ūvam · ūvae, ūvās*; *cāseus, cāseum · cāseī, cāseōs*; *ōvum, ōvum · ōva, ōva*.
3. Flip the sentence: *Puer puellam videt → Puella puerum videt.* Then *Magister discipulum videt → ?*
4. Scramble the order and keep the meaning: *Marcus mālum edit · Mālum Marcus edit · Edit Marcus mālum.*
5. One to many: *Puer edit → Puerī edunt. Agricola vendit → ? Puella amat → ? Magistra habet → ?*
6. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing both parts. Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
