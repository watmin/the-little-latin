# Caput III: Magnus et Parvus

*Liber I, Prīma Verba · Chapter 3*

*In which apples get big and small, cheese gets salty, wine turns black, and every describing word learns to wear the same family clothes as its noun. We go to the market.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Mālum magnum est.* · *Ūvae parvae sunt.* · *Cāseus salsus sed bonus est.*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** adjective, agreement
    - **Forms:** adjectives like *magnus, magna, magnum* and their plurals *magnī, magnae, magna*; *-er* adjectives like *ruber, rubra, rubrum*
    - **Laws:** the Law of Agreement, the Law of the Family (not the Rhyme)
    - **Words:** sizes, colors, tastes, good and bad, new and old; *et* (and), *sed* (but), *-que* (and)
    - **Reading:** at the fruit stall
    - **Speaking:** describe what's in front of you

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as before. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check.

## 1. Big and small

:: Multum in parvō. || Much in little.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Quid est?* (an apple) | *Mālum est.* |
| Now a huge apple. "Big" is *magnum*. | *Mālum magnum est.* "It's a big apple." |
| Say *magnum*. | MAG-num. Not MAH-nyum, the church and Italian way. (Scholars think a Roman may have said something close to MANG-num.) |
| Now a tiny apple. "Small" is *parvum*. | *Mālum parvum est.* PAR-wum. |
| Where does the describing word go? | Usually right after the noun: *mālum magnum*, "a big apple." |
| Is that a fixed rule? | No, just a habit. *Magnum mālum* is fine too, and puts a little weight on "big." |
| An egg. A big one. | *Ōvum magnum est.* |
| A little glass of wine. | *Vīnum parvum est.* "It's a small wine." Some things should be bigger. |
| What do *magnum* and *parvum* do? | They describe a noun: they tell you what it's like. |
| What is the grammar term for a describing word? | An **adjective**. |
| Where does the word come from? | Latin *adiectīvum*, "thrown at, added to." It's added to a noun. |
| Is *mālum* an adjective? | No. It names a thing: a noun. *Magnum* tells you about it: an adjective. |
| Have you heard *magnum* in English? | Yes: a magnum is a big bottle of wine. Latin for "a big one." |
| And *parvum*? | Rare in English. The parvovirus is named for it: a "small virus." |

!!! info "A grammar word, in plain English"
    **Adjective** (*adiectīvum*, "added on"): a word that describes a noun. Big, small, red, salty, good. *mālum **magnum***

## 2. The adjective changes clothes

:: Similis similī gaudet. || Like delights in like.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| A big table. *Mēnsa* is feminine. Try: *mēnsa magnum*? | Close, but no. *Mēnsa magna.* |
| What changed? | *Magnum* became *magna*. |
| Why? | *Mēnsa* is feminine, so the adjective takes a feminine ending, *-a*. |
| A big cheese. *Cāseus* is masculine. | *Cāseus magnus.* |
| What ending did *magnus* take? | *-us*, the masculine ending, like *cāseus*. |
| So how many forms does "big" have in the singular? | Three: *magnus* (m.), *magna* (f.), *magnum* (n.). |
| Which family endings are these? | The same three you learned in Chapter 2: *-us, -a, -um*. |
| A small chair (*sella*, f.). | *Sella parva.* |
| A small book (*liber*, m.). | *Liber parvus.* |
| A small egg (*ōvum*, n.). | *Ōvum parvum.* |
| A small girl. | *Puella parva.* |
| A big boy. | *Puer magnus.* |
| Do *puer* and *magnus* end the same way? | No. *Puer* ends in *-er*, *magnus* in *-us*. They're still both masculine, and that's what counts. Hold that thought. |
| *Discipula parvus*: right or wrong? | Wrong. *Discipula* is feminine: *discipula parva*. |
| Dictionaries list an adjective as *magnus, -a, -um*. What does that mean? | The masculine, feminine, and neuter forms: *magnus, magna, magnum*. |

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Two big tables. *Mēnsae* is plural. | *Mēnsae magnae.* |
| What changed in the adjective? | *-a* became *-ae*, just like the noun. |
| Many big cheeses. | *Cāseī magnī.* |
| Many small eggs. | *Ōva parva.* |
| Why *parva* and not *parvae*? | *Ōva* is neuter plural, and the neuter plural ends in *-a*. The Law of the Neuter Plural, again. |
| Two small books (*librī*). | *Librī parvī.* |
| Two big boys (*puerī*). | *Puerī magnī.* |
| So the adjective must match its noun in which two ways so far? | **Gender** (m., f., n.) and **number** (one or many). |
| Does it match the noun's case too? | Yes. Everything here is nominative, so the adjective is nominative too. |
| What is the grammar term for matching like this? | **Agreement**. We say the adjective **agrees** with its noun. |

!!! success "The Law of Agreement"
    An adjective wears its noun's **gender**, **number**, and **case**.

    | | masculine | feminine | neuter |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | one | *magnus* | *magna* | *magnum* |
    | more than one | *magnī* | *magnae* | *magna* |

    *cāseus magnus · mēnsa magna · ōvum magnum · cāseī magnī · mēnsae magnae · ōva magna*

## 3. Family, not rhyme

:: Nōn semper ea sunt quae videntur. || Things are not always what they seem. (Phaedrus)

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Agricola* (farmer) ends in *-a*. What gender is it? | Masculine. You met this in Chapter 2. |
| So: a good farmer. "Good" is *bonus, -a, -um*. | *Agricola bonus.* |
| *Agricola bona*? | Wrong. It rhymes, but the farmer is masculine: *bonus*. |
| A big sailor. | *Nauta magnus.* |
| Two good farmers. | *Agricolae bonī.* |
| So does the adjective copy the noun's **ending** or its **gender**? | Its gender. Often they look alike, but not always. |
| A good boy. | *Puer bonus.* The noun ends in *-er*, the adjective in *-us*. Both masculine. |
| A big book. | *Liber magnus.* |
| What's the safe way to check agreement? | Ask "what gender is the noun?" not "what does it end in?" |

!!! success "The Law of the Family (not the Rhyme)"
    The adjective matches the noun's **family** (gender), not its spelling. *Agricola bonus*, *puer magnus*: different endings, same family.

## 4. Colors

:: Dē gustibus et colōribus nōn est disputandum. || There's no arguing about tastes and colors. (proverb)

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "White" is *albus, -a, -um*. A white egg. | *Ōvum album.* |
| Where does "album" in English come from? | A Roman *album* was a white board for public notices. People wrote lists on it. |
| White grapes. | *Ūvae albae.* |
| "Yellow, golden" is *flāvus, -a, -um*. A yellow pear. | *Pirum flāvum.* FLAH-wum. |
| "Sky blue" is *caeruleus, -a, -um*. Say it. | kye-RU-le-us. The *e* before *-us* is short, so the stress steps back to *ru*. |
| Blue water. | *Aqua caerulea.* |
| "Black" is *niger*. Masculine, ends in *-er*. Feminine? | *Nigra.* The e drops out, like *liber → librī*. |
| Neuter? | *Nigrum.* So: *niger, nigra, nigrum*. |
| Black grapes. | *Ūvae nigrae.* |
| A black stylus. | *Stilus niger.* |
| Black stili (plural)? | *Stilī nigrī.* |
| "Red" is *ruber, rubra, rubrum*. A red apple. | *Mālum rubrum.* |
| Red apples. | *Māla rubra.* |
| A red chair. | *Sella rubra.* |
| Does *ruber* keep its e like *puer* or drop it like *liber*? | Drops it: *rubra, rubrum*. Learn the feminine and you'll know. |
| What English words come from *ruber*? | Ruby, rubric (old books printed headings in red), and maybe rubella. |
| Red wine. What do you think a Roman called it? | Often *vīnum nigrum*, "black wine." Dark wine was called black. White wine was *vīnum album*. |

## 5. Tastes and temperatures

:: Ad gustum. || To taste.

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Salty" is *salsus, -a, -um*. Salty cheese. | *Cāseus salsus.* |
| What else comes from *sal*, salt? | Salad (salted greens), sauce, and, by an old story, "salary." |
| "Sour, unripe, harsh" is *acerbus, -a, -um*. A sour grape. | *Ūva acerba.* |
| Is "acerbic" related? | Yes: a sour, harsh comment. |
| "Bitter" is *amārus, -a, -um*. Bitter wine. | *Vīnum amārum.* a-MAH-rum. |
| "Hot" is *calidus, -a, -um*. Hot water. | *Aqua calida.* KA-li-da. |
| "Cold" is *frīgidus, -a, -um*. Cold water. | *Aqua frīgida.* FREE-gi-da. |
| Where's the stress in *frīgida*? | On *frī*. The *gi* is short, so the stress goes back one. |
| "Full" is *plēnus, -a, -um*. A full table. | *Mēnsa plēna.* |
| A cup is *pōculum* (n.). A full cup. | *Pōculum plēnum.* |
| Full cups. | *Pōcula plēna.* |
| "Empty" is *vacuus, -a, -um*. An empty table. | *Mēnsa vacua.* WA-ku-a. |
| Where does "vacuum" come from? | *Vacuum*, the neuter: "an empty thing." |
| "Sweet" is *dulcis*. Can we use it yet? | Not yet. It belongs to a different family of adjectives, coming in Book II. |

## 6. Good and bad, new and old

:: Nihil sub sōle novum. || Nothing new under the sun. (Ecclesiastes)

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Good" is *bonus, -a, -um*. Good water. | *Aqua bona.* |
| "Bad" is *malus, -a, -um*. Bad cheese. | *Cāseus malus.* |
| Bad eggs. | *Ōva mala.* |
| Now say "apples." Careful! | *Māla.* Long ā. |
| So *māla mala* means… | "Bad apples." Hold the first ā, keep the second short. |
| Say it. | MAH-la MA-la. |
| "New" is *novus, -a, -um*. A new book. | *Liber novus.* NO-wus. |
| "Novel," "novice," "renovate" come from… | *Novus*: new. |
| "Old, ancient" is *antīquus, -a, -um*. An old book. | *Liber antīquus.* an-TEE-kwus. |
| Aged cheese? | *Cāseus antīquus.* (Older is better.) |
| "Long" is *longus, -a, -um*. A long table. | *Mēnsa longa.* |
| A long book. | *Liber longus.* |

## 7. Saying what something is like

:: Magna est vēritās. || Truth is great. (Vulgate, 3 Esdras)

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Mālum rubrum.* A sentence or just a phrase? | A phrase: "a red apple." No verb. |
| Add *est*. *Mālum rubrum est.* What can it mean? | "It's a red apple," or "The apple is red." Context decides. |
| You can also say *mālum est rubrum*. Meaning? | "The apple is red." Putting the adjective after *est* makes that reading clearer. |
| Is the adjective still nominative after *est*? | Yes. *Est* links two things that are the same thing, so both stay in the naming case. |
| The grapes are small. | *Ūvae parvae sunt.* |
| Why *sunt*? | *Ūvae* is plural. |
| The cheese is salty. | *Cāseus salsus est.* |
| The water is cold. | *Aqua frīgida est.* |
| The eggs are white. | *Ōva alba sunt.* |
| The farmers are good. | *Agricolae bonī sunt.* |
| How do you say "not"? | *Nōn.* Usually right before the verb. |
| The apple isn't big. | *Mālum nōn est magnum.* Or *mālum magnum nōn est.* |
| What is "and"? | *Et.* |
| The book is long and old. | *Liber longus et antīquus est.* |
| "But" is *sed*. The cheese is salty but good. | *Cāseus salsus sed bonus est.* |
| Latin has a second "and": *-que*, stuck on the end of the second word. *Magnus parvusque.* | "Big and small." |
| Have you met a stuck-on word like *-que* before? | Yes, *-ne*. Both are enclitics. |
| Need to use *-que* yet? | No. Just recognize it. *Senātus Populusque Rōmānus*, SPQR: "the Senate and the Roman People." |

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Estne mālum magnum?* | "Is the apple big?" (or "Is it a big apple?") |
| Answer yes. | *Ita, magnum est.* |
| *Suntne ōva alba?* (they're brown, but say no) | *Minimē, ōva nōn sunt alba.* |
| *Nōnne cāseus bonus est?* What answer is expected? | Yes. *Ita, bonus est!* |
| *Num aqua calida est?* What answer is expected? | No. *Minimē, frīgida est.* |
| Notice: *magnum est*, *bonus est*, *frīgida est*. Why three different endings? | Each agrees with a different noun: *mālum* (n.), *cāseus* (m.), *aqua* (f.). |

## 8. Lectiō: At the fruit stall

:: Tolle, lege. || Pick it up and read. (Augustine)

Read each Latin line aloud. Its English sits beside it, under a small wax tablet: guess first, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English.

::: dialogue lectio
Agricola | Salvēte! || Farmer | Hello, y'all!
Iūlia | Salvē, agricola! Quid sunt? || Iūlia | Hello, farmer! What are they?
Agricola | Māla sunt. Māla magna et rubra! || Farmer | They're apples. Big red apples!
Iōannēs | Suntne bona? || John | Are they good?
Agricola | Ita, optima! || Farmer | Yes, the very best!
Iūlia | Et quid est? Nōnne pirum est? || Iūlia | And what's that? It's a pear, isn't it?
Agricola | Ita, pirum flāvum est. || Farmer | Yes, it's a yellow pear.
Iōannēs | Num pirum acerbum est? || John | The pear isn't sour, is it?
Agricola | Minimē! Pirum nōn est acerbum. || Farmer | Not at all! The pear isn't sour.
Iūlia | Et ūvae? Ūvae parvae sunt. || Iūlia | And the grapes? The grapes are small.
Agricola | Parvae sed bonae! Ūvae nigrae, vīnum nigrum. || Farmer | Small but good! Dark grapes, dark wine.
Iōannēs | Et cāseus? || John | And the cheese?
Agricola | Cāseus salsus et antīquus est. || Farmer | The cheese is salty and aged.
Iūlia | Cāseus antīquus? Bonus est! || Iūlia | Aged cheese? It's good!
Iōannēs | Grātiās, agricola! Valē! || John | Thanks, farmer! Goodbye!
Agricola | Valēte! || Farmer | Goodbye, y'all!
:::

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Why *māla magna*, not *māla magnae*? | *Māla* is neuter plural, so the adjective takes the neuter plural *-a*. |
| What is *optima*? | "Very best," the strongest form of *bona*. You met its cousin *optimē*, "very well," in Chapter 1. |
| Why does it end in *-a*? | It agrees with *māla*: neuter plural. |
| Why *parvae* for the grapes? | *Ūvae* is feminine plural. |
| Why *pirum flāvum* and not *pirum flāva*? | *Pirum* is neuter singular. |
| The farmer is good. Say it. | *Agricola bonus est.* Masculine, despite the *-a*. |
| What does *sed* mean in *parvae sed bonae*? | "But." |
| Why does the farmer say *salvēte* and *valēte*? | He's talking to two people, John and Iūlia: hello and goodbye, y'all. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking.

1. Pick three things near you. Name each one with an adjective: *mālum rubrum, mēnsa magna, aqua frīgida*.
2. Run "big" through all six forms, aloud: *magnus, magna, magnum · magnī, magnae, magna*. Then "black": *niger, nigra, nigrum · nigrī, nigrae, nigra*.
3. Make it agree: *cāseus (good) · ūvae (small) · ōva (white) · agricola (big) · liber (old)*.
4. Say a full sentence for each: *Cāseus bonus est. Ūvae parvae sunt.*
5. *Sed* drill: *Cāseus salsus sed bonus est. Ūvae parvae sed bonae sunt.*
6. Minimal pairs: *māla / mala · grātiās / grātīs*
7. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part. Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
