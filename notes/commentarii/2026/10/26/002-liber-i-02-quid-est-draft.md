# Caput II: Quid est?

*Liber I, Prīma Verba · Chapter 2*

*In which we point at things on the dinner table and name them, find out what happens when there's more than one, meet Latin's three families of nouns, and learn three ways to ask a question. There will be eggs.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Quid est? Mālum est.* · *Quid sunt? Ōva sunt.*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** noun, verb, singular and plural (number), gender, case, nominative
    - **Forms:** *est* (is) and *sunt* (are); plural endings *-a → -ae*, *-us → -ī*, *-um → -a*, *-er → -rī*
    - **Laws:** the Law of *est* and *sunt*, the Law of Endings, the Law of the Neuter Plural
    - **Asking:** yes/no questions with *-ne*, *nōnne* (expects yes), *num* (expects no), and the answers *ita* and *minimē*
    - **Reading:** a dinner-table dialogue
    - **Speaking:** name everything on your table, aloud

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as Chapter 1. Each answer hides under a wax tablet. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap to check.

---

## 1. What is it?

:: Rēs, nōn verba. || Things, not words.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| In Chapter 1, what did *quid* mean? | "What?" |
| And *est*? | "Is." |
| So what might *quid est?* mean? | "What is it?" |
| Say it. | *KWID EST?* |
| Someone holds up an apple and asks *quid est?* What is "apple"? | *Mālum.* You met it in Chapter 1. |
| So the answer is… | *Mālum est.* "It's an apple." |
| Where is the word "it"? | Latin doesn't need one. *Est* alone can mean "it is." |
| Where is the word "an"? | Latin has no "a," "an," or "the." *Mālum* can mean "an apple" or "the apple." |
| No articles at all? | None. Romans got along fine without them. You'll stop missing them. |
| Now an egg. Egg is *ōvum*. *Quid est?* | *Ōvum est.* |
| Say *ōvum*. Which vowel is long? | The first: *Ō-wum*. Remember, v sounds like w. |
| Wine is *vīnum*. *Quid est?* | *Vīnum est.* WEE-num. |
| Fruit in general is *pōmum*. *Quid est?* | *Pōmum est.* |
| Is a *mālum* a *pōmum*? | Yes. An apple is a fruit. |
| Can you also say *est mālum*? | Yes. Latin word order is flexible. *Mālum est* is the most common way to answer. |
| What kind of word is *mālum*? | A naming word: it names a thing. |
| What is the grammar term for a naming word? | A **noun**. |
| What did Roman grammarians call a noun? | *Nōmen*, "a name." The same word as in *quid est nōmen tibi?* |
| What kind of word is *est*? | A doing-or-being word. |
| What is the grammar term for a doing-or-being word? | A **verb**. *Est* is the verb "to be": "is." |

!!! info "Two words, in plain English"
    - **Noun** (*nōmen*, "name"): a word that names a person, place, or thing. *mālum, ōvum, magistra*
    - **Verb**: a word for doing or being. *est* "is", *agis* "you do"

## 2. Things on the table

:: Ab ōvō usque ad māla. || From the egg to the apples. (Horace: a Roman dinner, start to finish)

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Cheese is *cāseus*. *Quid est?* | *Cāseus est.* KAH-se-us. |
| Water is *aqua*. *Quid est?* | *Aqua est.* A-kwa. |
| A grape is *ūva*. *Quid est?* | *Ūva est.* OO-wa. |
| A pear is *pirum*. *Quid est?* | *Pirum est.* PI-rum, with a short i. |
| A table is *mēnsa*. *Quid est?* | *Mēnsa est.* MAYN-sa. |
| A chair is *sella*. *Quid est?* | *Sella est.* Say both l's: SEL-la. |
| A book is *liber*. *Quid est?* | *Liber est.* |
| Short i or long ī in *liber*? | Short. *Līber* with a long ī means "free." Same trap as Chapter 1. |
| A wax tablet for writing is *tabula*. *Quid est?* | *Tabula est.* TA-bu-la. |
| And the pointed tool for writing on it? A *stilus*. | *Stilus est.* |
| Where does our word "style" come from? | From *stilus*: the way you write. |
| Your teacher, a woman, walks in. | *Magistra est.* |
| A male student walks in. A male student is *discipulus*. | *Discipulus est.* |
| A female student? | *Discipula est.* |
| A girl is *puella*. A boy is *puer*. A girl walks in. | *Puella est.* |
| And a boy? | *Puer est.* |
| You can't see who's at the door. How do you ask "Who is it?" | *Quis est?* *Quis* is for people, *quid* for things. You met both in Chapter 1. |

## 3. More than one

:: Ē plūribus ūnum. || Out of many, one.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| In Chapter 1, the teacher said *salvēte, discipulī!* Who were the *discipulī*? | The students. More than one. |
| One student is… | *discipulus*. |
| Several students are… | *discipulī*. |
| What changed? | *-us* became *-ī*. |
| Is *est* still right for several students? | No. "Is" is for one. |
| Then what is "they are" or "are"? | *Sunt.* |
| So: the students are here. | *Discipulī sunt.* |
| And one student? | *Discipulus est.* |
| What is the grammar term for "one or more than one"? | **Number**. One is **singular**; more than one is **plural**. |
| Have you seen "plural" before? | Yes. *Salvēte* is the plural of *salvē*: hello, y'all. |
| One table: *mēnsa*. Two tables? | *Mēnsae*. MAYN-sye. |
| What changed? | *-a* became *-ae*. |
| How do you say *ae*? | Like "eye." |
| Two girls? | *Puellae.* |
| Three teachers, all women? | *Magistrae.* |
| One egg: *ōvum*. Twelve eggs? | *Ōva*. |
| What changed? | *-um* became *-a*. |
| One glass of wine: *vīnum*. Plural? | *Vīna*. "Wines." |
| Two pears? | *Pira*. |
| One book is *liber*. Many books are *librī*. What happened? | The *-er* lost its *e* and took *-ī*: *libr-ī*. |
| One male teacher: *magister*. Many? | *Magistrī.* |
| One boy: *puer*. Many boys: *puerī*. Did the e drop out? | No. *Puer* keeps it. Some *-er* words do, some don't. Learn each one with its plural. |
| One cheese: *cāseus*. Several cheeses? | *Cāseī*. KAH-se-ee. |

!!! success "The Law of *est* and *sunt*"
    One thing **is**: *est*. More than one thing **are**: *sunt*.

    *Mālum est.* It's an apple. · *Māla sunt.* They're apples.

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Many apples? | *Māla*. One *mālum*, many *māla*. |
| Careful! What does *mala* mean, with a short a? | "Bad things." Hold that ā, or you're serving bad things for dessert. |
| *Quid sunt?* (holding up grapes) | *Ūvae sunt.* |
| What does *quid sunt?* mean? | "What are they?" |
| *Quid sunt?* (holding up stili) | *Stilī sunt.* |
| *Quid est?* (one cheese) | *Cāseus est.* |
| *Quid sunt?* (two chairs) | *Sellae sunt.* |

## 4. Three families

:: Suum cuique. || To each its own.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| How many plural patterns have we seen? | Three main ones: *-a → -ae*, *-us → -ī*, *-um → -a* (plus *-er → -rī*/*-erī*, which acts like *-us*). |
| Which words did *-a → -ae*? | *mēnsa, puella, magistra, discipula, aqua, ūva, tabula, sella* |
| Do many of them name women? | Yes: *puella, magistra, discipula*. |
| Which did *-us → -ī*? | *discipulus, cāseus, stilus* (and *liber, magister, puer*) |
| Do many of them name men? | Yes: *discipulus, magister, puer*. |
| Which did *-um → -a*? | *mālum, ōvum, vīnum, pōmum, pirum* |
| Do any of them name people? | No. All things. |
| Latin sorts every noun into one of three families. What is the grammar term? | **Gender**. |
| Is a table really female? | No. Grammatical gender is just a family name for a group of nouns. Words for people usually match the person; for things it's a label. |
| What are the three genders called? | **Masculine**, **feminine**, and **neuter**. |
| What does *neuter* mean in Latin? | "Neither": neither masculine nor feminine. |
| Most nouns ending in *-a* are… | feminine. |
| Most nouns ending in *-us* or *-er* are… | masculine. |
| Nouns ending in *-um* are… | neuter. |
| Is *mēnsa* masculine, feminine, or neuter? | Feminine. |
| *Stilus*? | Masculine. |
| *Ōvum*? | Neuter. |
| A farmer is *agricola*. In Latin it's treated as a man's word. Gender? | Masculine, even with the *-a* ending! The meaning wins. A sailor, *nauta*, is masculine too. |
| So the ending rule is… | a strong habit, not an iron law. The person wins over the ending. |

!!! success "The Law of Endings"
    | gender | one | more than one | example |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | feminine | *-a* | *-ae* | *mēnsa → mēnsae* |
    | masculine | *-us* / *-er* | *-ī* | *stilus → stilī*, *liber → librī* |
    | neuter | *-um* | *-a* | *ōvum → ōva* |

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Look at *ōva* and *mēnsa*. What do their endings have in common? | Both end in *-a*. |
| Is *ōva* one thing or many? | Many. It's the plural of *ōvum*. |
| Is *mēnsa* one thing or many? | One. It's feminine singular. |
| So when you see a word ending in *-a*, what must you ask? | "Is it a feminine singular, or a neuter plural?" |
| How can you tell? | Know the word, and look at the verb: *ōva sunt* (plural), *mēnsa est* (singular). |
| Which verb tells you *ōva* is plural? | *Sunt*. |
| *Pira sunt.* One pear or several? | Several. |
| *Aqua est.* Neuter plural? | No. Feminine singular: *aqua*, water. |

!!! success "The Law of the Neuter Plural"
    A neuter noun's plural **always** ends in *-a*. So an *-a* ending can mean one feminine thing or many neuter things. Let the verb (*est* or *sunt*) and the word itself tell you which.

## 5. The naming form

:: Nōmina rērum. || The names of things.

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| In every sentence so far, what is the noun doing? | Being something, or just being there. *Mālum est:* it's an apple. |
| What is the grammar term for the word a sentence is about, the one doing or being? | The **subject**. In *discipulī sunt*, the subject is *discipulī*. |
| Do Latin nouns change their endings for other jobs too? | Yes, a lot. That's what Latin is famous for. |
| What is the grammar term for the form that shows a noun's job? | Its **case**. |
| How many cases will we meet? | Six or seven, one at a time over the next chapters. No rush. |
| Which case have you used all chapter? | The naming case, used for the subject and for the thing after *est*. |
| What is it called? | The **nominative**, from *nōmen*: the "naming" case. |
| Is *mēnsa* nominative? | Yes. Nominative singular. |
| *Mēnsae*? | Nominative plural. |
| Does English have cases? | A little. "He" vs. "him," "they" vs. "them." Latin does this with almost every noun. |
| Should you worry about the other cases now? | No. Chapter 4 brings the next one. |

!!! info "More grammar words, in plain English"
    - **Number**: singular (one) or plural (more than one)
    - **Gender**: masculine, feminine, or neuter ("neither"): a noun's family
    - **Subject**: who or what the sentence is about
    - **Case**: the form of a noun that shows its job in the sentence
    - **Nominative**: the naming case, for the subject (and the thing after *est*)

## 6. Asking questions

:: Quaerite et inveniētis. || Seek, y'all, and you will find.

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| How do you say "It's an apple"? | *Mālum est.* |
| How do you ask "Is it an apple?" | Add *-ne* to the first word: *Estne mālum?* |
| Say it. | *EST-ne MĀ-lum?* |
| What is *-ne*? | A little tag that sticks to the end of a word and turns the sentence into a yes/no question. |
| Old-school grammar name for a tag that sticks on like that? | An **enclitic** ("leaning on"). |
| Which word usually gets the *-ne*? | The first word of the question, often the verb: *estne, suntne*. |
| *Suntne ōva?* | "Are they eggs?" |
| How do you say yes? | *Ita.* Or *ita est*, "it is so." |
| How do you say no? | *Minimē.* "Not at all." Or *nōn est*, "it isn't." |
| *Estne vīnum?* (it's water) | *Minimē! Aqua est.* |
| *Estne cāseus?* (it is) | *Ita, cāseus est.* |
| Latin has two more question words. *Nōnne* asks a question expecting **yes**. *Nōnne mālum est?* | "It's an apple, isn't it?" |
| Have you seen *nōn* before? | Yes, it means "not." *Nōnne* is "not" + *-ne*. |
| What answer does *nōnne* hope for? | *Ita!* |
| *Num* asks a question expecting **no**. *Num vīnum est?* | "It isn't wine, is it?" (Surely not!) |
| What answer does *num* expect? | *Minimē!* |
| Your friend holds up a book at dinner. Use *num*. | *Num pānis est?* "That's not bread, is it?" *Minimē, liber est!* |
| Which word does a hopeful teacher use: *nōnne* or *num*? | *Nōnne.* "You did your homework, didn't you?" |
| Do you need to master *nōnne* and *num* now? | No. Just recognize them. You'll hear them again and again. |

!!! success "Three ways to ask"
    | Latin | asks | expects |
    |---|---|---|
    | *Estne mālum?* | Is it an apple? | yes or no |
    | *Nōnne mālum est?* | It's an apple, isn't it? | *ita* |
    | *Num mālum est?* | Surely it isn't an apple? | *minimē* |

## 7. Lectiō: Reading

:: Fēstīnā lentē. || Make haste slowly.

Read each Latin line aloud. Its English sits beside it, under a small wax tablet: guess first, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English.

::: dialogue lectio
Magistra | Salvēte, discipulī! || Teacher | Hello, students!
Discipulī | Salvē, magistra! || Students | Hello, teacher!
Magistra | Iōannēs, quid est? || Teacher | John, what is it?
Iōannēs | Mālum est. || John | It's an apple.
Magistra | Et quid sunt? || Teacher | And what are they?
Iōannēs | Ōva sunt. || John | They're eggs.
Magistra | Estne vīnum? || Teacher | Is it wine?
Iūlia | Minimē, aqua est. || Iūlia | No, it's water.
Quīntus | Nōnne cāseus est? || Quīntus | It's cheese, isn't it?
Magistra | Ita, cāseus est. || Teacher | Yes, it's cheese.
Iōannēs | Quid sunt? || John | What are they?
Iūlia | Ūvae et pira sunt. || Iūlia | They're grapes and pears.
Magistra | Quīnte, num liber est? || Teacher | Quīntus, that's not a book, is it?
Quīntus | Minimē! Pānis est. || Quīntus | No! It's bread.
Magistra | Optimē. Valēte, discipulī! || Teacher | Excellent. Goodbye, students!
:::

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Why does John say *ōva sunt* and not *ōva est*? | *Ōva* is plural (eggs), so the verb is *sunt*. |
| Is *aqua* one thing or many? | One. Feminine singular. |
| What answer does Quīntus expect with *nōnne*? | Yes. |
| What answer does the teacher expect with *num*? | No. And she gets it: *minimē!* |
| Why does the teacher say *Quīnte* instead of *Quīntus*? | It's how you call someone named Quīntus. That form gets its own chapter (Chapter 9). For now, just hear it. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Ōre, nōn oculīs. || With the mouth, not the eyes.

1. Point at five things around you and ask yourself *Quid est?* Answer: *___ est.*
2. Singular to plural, aloud: *mēnsa → mēnsae · discipulus → discipulī · ōvum → ōva · liber → librī · puer → puerī*
3. *Est* or *sunt*? *Ōva ___ · Aqua ___ · Pira ___ · Cāseus ___ · Puellae ___*
4. Yes/no: *Estne mālum? Ita, mālum est. · Estne vīnum? Minimē, aqua est.*
5. *Nōnne cāseus est? Ita! · Num liber est? Minimē!*
6. Minimal pairs: *māla / mala · liber / līber*
7. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing every part. Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
