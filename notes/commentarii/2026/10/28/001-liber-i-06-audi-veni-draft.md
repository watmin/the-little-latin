# Caput VI: Audī! Venī!

*Liber I, Prīma Verba · Chapter 6*

*In which the last two verb families arrive, and you learn to boss people around. "Listen!" "Come!" "Eat!" "Don't laugh!" A Latin command is a verb with its tail clipped off, and by the end of this chapter you'll be giving orders and obeying them.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *Venī hūc!* · *Audīte, discipulī!* · *Nōlī rīdēre!* · *Dīc iterum, quaesō.*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** infinitive, imperative (command form), third and fourth conjugation
    - **Forms:** *legō, legis … legunt*; *audiō, audīs … audiunt*; *capiō, capis … capiunt*; commands *amā/amāte, vidē/vidēte, lege/legite, audī/audīte, cape/capite*; *dīc, dūc, fac, fer*; *nōlī, nōlīte*
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Little i, the Law of the Long ī, the Law of the Four Families, the Law of the Clipped Command, the Second Law of *-te*, the Four Rebels, the Law of *Nōlī*
    - **Words:** eating, drinking, buying, reading, writing, running, hearing, coming, sleeping, knowing, taking, making, leading, bringing
    - **Reading:** Iūlia runs the kitchen
    - **Speaking:** give commands, obey them, and ask someone to say it again, slowly

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. This chapter is noisy on purpose: commands want to be shouted.

---

## 1. *Edō, edis, edit*

:: Nōn vītae sed scholae discimus. || We learn not for life but for school. (Seneca, *Letters* 106.12, as a complaint; schools later flipped it)

Since Chapter 4, Marcus has been eating in the third person only. Today he gets the whole set.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 4: *Marcus edit*. Chapter 5: "I" ends in *-ō*. So "I eat"? | *Edō.* E-doh. |
| "You eat" is *edis*. What's new? | A short *i* before the *-s*: *ed-i-s*. E-dis. |
| "He, she eats"? | *Edit.* You've known it since Chapter 4. |
| "We eat" is *edimus*. Ending? Vowel? | *-mus*, with a short *i* in front: *ed-i-mus*. |
| Then "y'all eat"? Guess. | *Editis.* E-di-tis. |
| "They eat"? | *Edunt.* Chapter 4 already said it: the *-it* verbs make *-unt*. |
| Chant all six. | *Edō, edis, edit, edimus, editis, edunt.* |
| *Amāmus* and *habēmus* have a long vowel. Does *edimus*? | No. This family's vowel is a little, short *i* (and *u* in "they"). |
| So where does the stress (the emphasis) fall in *edimus*? | E-di-mus. The **penult** (the next-to-last syllable, *di*) is short, so the stress jumps back one. Compare a-MAH-mus, where the penult is long. |
| *Bibō*: "I drink." We drink, y'all drink. | *Bibimus, bibitis.* BI-bi-mus, BI-bi-tis. |
| *Emō*: "I buy." You buy. | *Emis.* |
| *Legō*: "I read." What are you reading? | *Quid legis?* |
| Chapter 1's *Quid agitis?* Look at *ag-i-tis*. Which family? | This one! *Agō, agis, agit, agimus, agitis, agunt.* That chunk was a little-i verb all along. |
| And Chapter 1's *intellegō*? | Same family. *Intellegitis?* "Do y'all understand?" |
| The motto's *discimus*? | "We learn." *-imus*: the little-i family again. |

!!! success "The Law of the Little i"
    | | singular | plural |
    |---|---|---|
    | 1st | *legō* | *legimus* |
    | 2nd | *legis* | *legitis* |
    | 3rd | *legit* | *legunt* |

    The family vowel is a short *i* everywhere, except *u* in "they" and nothing at all in *legō*. It's never long, so the stress slides back: LE-gi-mus, LE-gi-tis.

## 2. Reading the dictionary

:: Repetītiō māter studiōrum. || Repetition is the mother of learning. (proverb)

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| How did Chapter 5 list a verb? | By two forms, "I do it" and "to do it": *amō, amāre*; *habeō, habēre*. |
| "To eat" is *edere*. Say it. | E-de-re. Every vowel short. |
| *Habēre* and *edere* both end in *-ere*. Same family? | No! *Habēre* has a long ē (ha-BAY-re). *Edere* has a short e (E-de-re). |
| So how does the dictionary mark the little-i family? | A short *-ere*: *legō, legere*; *bibō, bibere*; *emō, emere*. |
| Without macrons, could you tell *docēre* from *legere*? | Not from the spelling. That's why this book marks every long vowel: Chapter 1's Commandment of Length pays off. |
| Stress *legere*, then *docēre*. | LE-ge-re, do-KAY-re. |
| The "to do it" form has a grammar name. What is it? | The **infinitive**, "unlimited": it doesn't say who does it. No person, no number. *Edere* is just "to eat." |
| Chapter 4's proverb had *ēst*, long e, for "eats." "You eat" has a shorter twin too: *ēs*, with a long ē. And *es*, short e? | "You are," from Chapter 5. *Ēs* (long) = you eat; *es* (short) = you are. One macron, a whole meaning. |
| Which should you say for "you eat"? | *Edis.* Just recognize *ēs* when a Roman uses it. |
| *Quid edis?* | "What are you eating?" |
| Answer: an apple. | *Mālum edō.* |
| *Quid bibitis?* Answer for the group. | "What are y'all drinking?" *Aquam bibimus.* |

## 3. The ī family: *audiō*

:: Audīmus et discimus. || We listen and we learn.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Audiō*: "I hear, I listen." Think "audio." Say it. | OW-di-oh. *au* as in "how." |
| Is that i long? | No. A vowel right before another vowel is short (Chapter 5). |
| "You hear" is *audīs*. Compare *legis*. | Long ī! OW-dees. *Legis* has a short i. |
| "We hear": *audīmus*. Stress? | ow-DEE-mus. The long penult takes it. Compare LE-gi-mus. |
| Y'all hear? | *Audītis.* ow-DEE-tis. |
| "He hears" is *audit*. Long or short i? | Short. Chapter 5's rule: a long vowel shrinks before a final *-t*. |
| *Audit* and *legit* look like twins. How do you tell their families apart? | Ask about "you" or "we": *audīs, audīmus* (long) vs *legis, legimus* (short). |
| "They hear": *audiunt*. Which family's "they" does that look like? | The little-i family's *legunt*, with the i kept: *audi-unt*. OW-di-unt. |
| Chant *audiō*. | *Audiō, audīs, audit, audīmus, audītis, audiunt.* |
| How does a dictionary list it? | *audiō, audīre*. A long *-īre*: ow-DEE-re. |
| What are these two new families called? | The little-i family (*legō, legere*) is the **third conjugation**. The ī family (*audiō, audīre*) is the **fourth**. |
| *Veniō, venīre*: "come." I come. They come. | *Veniō. Veniunt.* WE-ni-oh, WE-ni-unt. |
| Not "VEH-nee-oh"? | Not in classical Latin: v is w. VEH-nee-oh is the church habit. |
| *Dormiō, dormīre*: "sleep." Think "dormitory." We sleep. | *Dormīmus.* dor-MEE-mus. |
| *Sciō, scīre*: "know." Think "science." You know! | *Scīs!* SKEES. Classical *sc* is always "sk"; church Latin says "sh." |
| *Nesciō*? | "I don't know." *ne-* ("not") + *sciō*. NE-ski-oh. Say it often; you'll need it. |

!!! success "The Law of the Long ī"
    | | singular | plural |
    |---|---|---|
    | 1st | *audiō* | *audīmus* |
    | 2nd | *audīs* | *audītis* |
    | 3rd | *audit* | *audiunt* |

    The ī is long, except before *-t*, *-nt*, or another vowel. "They" keeps the i and adds *-unt*.

## 4. In between: *capiō*

:: Fugit irreparābile tempus. || Time flies, never to be won back. (Virgil, *Georgics* 3.284)

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Capiō*: "I take, I grab." Think "capture." Which family does *-iō* look like? | The ī family: *audiō*. KA-pi-oh. |
| But the dictionary says *capiō, capere*, short *-ere*. Which family is that? | The little-i family, like *legere*. KA-pe-re. |
| So which is it? | A bit of both. "You take" is *capis*, short i, just like *legis*. |
| We take. Y'all take. | *Capimus, capitis.* KA-pi-mus, KA-pi-tis. Short, like *legimus*. |
| They take? | *Capiunt.* Like *audiunt*: the i shows itself again. |
| So where does the i show? | In "I" and "they": *capiō, capiunt*. Everywhere else it's a twin of *legō*. |
| What does grammar call them? | **-iō verbs** of the third conjugation. A small club, but busy. |
| *Faciō, facere*: "make, do." Think "factory." Chant it. | *Faciō, facis, facit, facimus, facitis, faciunt.* FA-ki-oh. |
| FA-chee-oh? | Church habit. Classical c is always k: FA-ki-oh, FA-kis. |
| *Fugit* in the motto? | "It flees, it flies," from *fugiō, fugere*, another -iō verb. Time flies. |
| *Quid facis?* | "What are you doing? What are you making?" |
| Answer: dinner. | *Cēnam faciō.* (Romans also said *Cēnam parō*, "I'm preparing dinner.") |

!!! success "The Law of the Four Families"
    The two dictionary forms tell you the family:

    | dictionary | family | you | they |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | *amō, amāre* | 1st (ā) | *amās* | *amant* |
    | *habeō, habēre* | 2nd (ē) | *habēs* | *habent* |
    | *legō, legere* | 3rd (little i) | *legis* | *legunt* |
    | *capiō, capere* | 3rd, -iō | *capis* | *capiunt* |
    | *audiō, audīre* | 4th (ī) | *audīs* | *audiunt* |

    Long *-āre*, long *-ēre*, short *-ere*, long *-īre*. A short *-ere* with *-iō* in front is the in-between club.

!!! info "Grammar words, in plain English"
    **Infinitive**: the "to do it" form, the second form in the dictionary. *Amāre*, to love. It names the action without saying who does it.

    **Third and fourth conjugation**: the little-i family (*legō, legere*) and the ī family (*audiō, audīre*). With Chapter 5's ā and ē families, that's all four.

## 5. You've been giving orders since Chapter 1

:: Cavē canem. || Beware of the dog. (a doorway mosaic at Pompeii)

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 1: what did *salvē* literally say? | "Be healthy!" A command. |
| And *valē*? | "Be well! Be strong!" Another command. |
| Chapter 5 gave you the verb behind *valē*. Its two dictionary forms? | *valeō, valēre*. |
| Put *valēre* next to *valē*. What's missing? | The *-re*. |
| Chapter 5's Lectiō motto was *Audī, vidē, tacē*. The infinitives? | *audīre, vidēre, tacēre*. Clip the *-re*: *audī, vidē, tacē*. |
| What does grammar call a command form? | The **imperative**, from *imperāre*, "to give orders" (think "emperor," *imperātor*, the man giving them). |
| "Sing!" (*cantāre*) | *Cantā!* KAN-tah. |
| "Sit!" (*sedēre*) | *Sedē!* |
| "Sleep!" (*dormīre*) | *Dormī!* |
| "Come!" (*venīre*) | *Venī!* WE-nee. Half the chapter title. |
| Now to a whole room. Chapter 1's law? | Add *-te*: *Cantāte! Sedēte! Venīte!* Chapter 1 promised more *-te* words in Chapter 6. Here they are. |
| "Y'all, listen!" | *Audīte!* ow-DEE-te. |
| Where's the stress in *cantāte*? | kan-TAH-te. The long ā takes it. |
| The motto's *cavē*? | "Beware! Watch out!" from *caveō, cavēre*. (*Canem*, "dog," is an object form from a family you meet in Book II.) |

!!! success "The Law of the Clipped Command"
    To give an order to **one** person, take the infinitive and clip off *-re*. To **y'all**, add *-te*.

    | infinitive | to one | to y'all |
    |---|---|---|
    | *amāre* | *amā!* | *amāte!* |
    | *vidēre* | *vidē!* | *vidēte!* |
    | *audīre* | *audī!* | *audīte!* |

!!! info "Grammar words, in plain English"
    **Imperative** (old school: "the command form"): the form of a verb that tells someone to do something. *Venī!* Come! *Audīte!* Listen, y'all!

## 6. *Lege! Legite!*

:: Carpe diem. || Pick the day (like ripe fruit). (Horace, *Odes* 1.11.8)

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Clip the *-re* off *legere*. | *Lege!* "Read!" LE-ge. |
| *Edere? Bibere?* | *Ede! Bibe!* "Eat! Drink!" |
| The motto's *carpe*: what was its infinitive? | *Carpere*, "to pluck, to pick." *Carpe diem*: pick the day while it's ripe. (*Diem*, "day," is an object form from a later family.) |
| Now "Read!" to a whole class. *Legete?* | Close, but no: *Legite!* LE-gi-te. |
| What happened to the e? | Before *-te* it turns into the family's little i, just like *legitis*. |
| "Eat, y'all! Drink, y'all!" | *Edite! Bibite!* E-di-te, BI-bi-te. |
| "Write!" (*scrībō, scrībere*), to one, then to many. | *Scrībe! Scrībite!* SKREE-be, SKREE-bi-te. |
| "Run!" (*currō, currere*) | *Curre! Currite!* |
| And the -iō club? *Capere.* | *Cape! Capite!* "Take! Grab!" Same pattern as *lege, legite*. |
| *Age!* from *agere*? | "Do! Drive!" In everyday speech: "Come on! Go on!" *Agite!* to a group. |
| "Grab a stylus! Write!" | *Cape stilum! Scrībe!* |
| "Buy cheese!" | *Eme cāseum!* |
| "Read the book, y'all!" | *Legite librum!* |

!!! success "The Second Law of *-te*"
    Clipping the *-re* still works for one person: *legere → lege*. But for y'all, the third family (and its -iō club) turns that *e* into its little *i*: *-ite*.

    | infinitive | to one | to y'all |
    |---|---|---|
    | *legere* | *lege!* | *legite!* |
    | *capere* | *cape!* | *capite!* |

    Every family still ends its plural command in *-te*. Chapter 1's law holds everywhere.

## 7. Four rebels: *dīc, dūc, fac, fer*

:: Dīc, dūc, fac, fer! || Say! Lead! Make! Bring! (every Latin classroom, again)

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "Say!" from *dīcō, dīcere*. Clip the *-re*. | You'd expect *dīce*. Romans said ***dīc!*** DEEK. |
| "Lead!" from *dūcō, dūcere*. | *Dūc!* DOOK. Think "duke," the leader, and "conduct." |
| "Make! Do!" from *faciō, facere*. | *Fac!* FAK. |
| "Bring! Carry!" | *Fer!* From *ferō, ferre*, a stubborn verb like *sum*. For now, only its commands. |
| What did all four lose? | Their final *-e*. Four rebels, one chant: *dīc, dūc, fac, fer*. |
| Do their plurals rebel too? | Only *fer*: *ferte!* The rest are regular: *dīcite, dūcite, facite*. |
| How do you say *dīcite*? | DEE-ki-te. Not DEE-chee-te: that's the church habit. |
| *Fer aquam!* | "Bring water!" |
| To a waiter: "Bring wine, please." | *Fer vīnum, quaesō.* |
| Chapter 1 words: *Dīc iterum, quaesō!* | "Say it again, please!" The most useful sentence in this book. |
| "Say it more slowly, please." | *Dīc lentius, quaesō.* |
| *Dūc puerōs!* | "Lead the boys!" (Take them along.) |
| To two friends: "Say! Do! Bring!" | *Dīcite! Facite! Ferte!* |

!!! success "The Four Rebels"
    | to one | to y'all |
    |---|---|
    | *dīc!* say! | *dīcite!* |
    | *dūc!* lead! | *dūcite!* |
    | *fac!* do, make! | *facite!* |
    | *fer!* bring, carry! | *ferte!* |

    Four commands drop their *-e*. Only *fer* drops the i in the plural too.

## 8. *Nōlī!* Don't!

:: Nōlī mē tangere. || Don't touch me. (John 20:17, in the Latin Bible)

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| How do you say "Don't laugh!"? Try *Nōn rīdē!* | Not the everyday Roman way. In ordinary prose they used *nōlī* plus the infinitive: *Nōlī rīdēre!* |
| What does *nōlī* literally say? | "Be unwilling!" *Nōlī rīdēre*: "be unwilling to laugh." NOH-lee. |
| Which form follows *nōlī*? | The "to do it" form, the infinitive: *rīdēre*, not *rīdē*. |
| "Don't be afraid!" | *Nōlī timēre!* |
| To a group? | *Nōlīte timēre!* noh-LEE-te. The *-te* law again. |
| "Don't eat the grapes!" | *Nōlī ūvās edere!* |
| "Y'all, don't fall asleep!" | *Nōlīte dormīre!* |
| "Don't sell the book!" | *Nōlī librum vendere!* |
| The motto: which word is the infinitive? | *Tangere*, "to touch." (*Mē* is "me" as an object; it waits for Book II.) |
| Whose Gospel is the motto from? | John's. In Latin he's *Iōannēs*, your namesake. |
| Is *nōlī* from a regular verb? | No, from *nōlō*, "I don't want," which comes in Book II. For now, *nōlī, nōlīte* is a chunk. |

!!! success "The Law of *Nōlī*"
    "Don't!" is ***nōlī*** (to one) or ***nōlīte*** (to y'all) **+ the infinitive**: *Nōlī timēre! Nōlīte dormīre!*

## 9. *Venī!* *Vēnī!*

:: Vēnī, vīdī, vīcī. || I came, I saw, I conquered. (Julius Caesar, 47 BC, as reported by Suetonius, *Julius* 37)

{#g-09}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Someone calls *Venī hūc!* (*hūc*: to here, this way). Obey, and say so. | *Veniō!* "I'm coming!" |
| *Hūc* vs Chapter 5's *hīc*? | *Hīc* is "here," where you are. *Hūc* is "to here," where you're headed. HOOK. |
| *Audī!* Answer. | *Audiō.* "I'm listening." |
| *Audīte, discipulī!* Answer for the class. | *Audīmus.* |
| See the pattern? | A command to one: answer with *-ō*. A command to y'all: answer with *-mus*. |
| *Tacēte!* | *Tacēmus.* (Then actually do it.) |
| *Lege librum!* | *Librum legō.* |
| *Cape stilum et scrībe!* | *Stilum capiō et scrībō.* |
| *Venīte statim!* (*statim*: at once) | *Statim venīmus!* STA-tim. |
| Now Caesar's famous line, *Vēnī, vīdī, vīcī*. Is it "Come! See! Conquer!"? | Check the macrons. Caesar's *vēnī* has a long ē. Your command *venī* has a short e. |
| So what is *vēnī*? | "I came." A past form you'll meet in Book III. *Vīdī*: I saw. *Vīcī*: I conquered. |
| Say both. | WE-nee (come!) · WAY-nee (I came). And Caesar: WAY-nee, WEE-dee, WEE-kee. Not VAY-nee, VEE-dee, VEE-chee. |
| So one macron turns "Come!" into what? | "I came." *Venī!* — *Vēnī.* Order given, order done. |

## 10. Lectiō: Dinner duty

:: Festīnā lentē. || Make haste slowly. (Augustus's favorite saying, says Suetonius; he said it in Greek)

Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English.

::: dialogue lectio
Iūlia | Salvēte, puerī! Venīte hūc! || Iūlia | Hi, boys! Come here!
Marcus | Venīmus! Quid est? || Marcus | We're coming! What is it?
Iūlia | Hodiē cēnam parāmus. Audīte! || Iūlia | Today we're making dinner. Listen up!
Iōannēs | Audīmus. Dīc! || John | We're listening. Tell us!
Iūlia | Iōannēs, cape pōculum et fer aquam. || Iūlia | John, grab a cup and bring water.
Iōannēs | Ecce aqua! || John | Here's the water!
Iūlia | Bene. Nunc lavā ūvās. Tū quoque, labōrā! Cāseum parā! || Iūlia | Good. Now wash the grapes. *You* too (to Marcus), get to work! Get the cheese ready!
Marcus | Labōrō, labōrō! || Marcus | I'm working, I'm working!
Iōannēs | Iūlia, vidē! Marcus cāseum edit! || John | Iūlia, look! Marcus is eating the cheese!
Iūlia | Nōlī cāseum edere! Cēna est! || Iūlia | Don't eat the cheese! It's dinner!
Marcus | Nōn edō! Cāseum spectō! || Marcus | I'm not eating it! I'm *looking* at the cheese!
Iōannēs | Rīdeō! || John | I'm laughing!
Iūlia | Nōlī rīdēre, Iōannēs. Labōrā! || Iūlia | Don't laugh, John. Work!
Iōannēs | Quid faciō nunc? || John | What do I do now?
Iūlia | Mēnsam parā et sellās portā. || Iūlia | Set the table and carry the chairs.
Iōannēs | Dīc iterum, quaesō. Lentius! || John | Say it again, please. More slowly!
Iūlia | Mēnsam… parā. Sellās… portā. || Iūlia | The table… set it. The chairs… carry them.
Iōannēs | Intellegō! || John | Got it!
Marcus | Nunc dormīmus? || Marcus | Do we sleep now?
Iūlia | Nōlīte dormīre, puerī! Nunc edimus et bibimus. || Iūlia | Don't fall asleep, boys! Now we eat and drink.
Iōannēs | Edite, bibite! Cēna bona est. || John | Eat up, drink up, y'all! Dinner is good.
Marcus | Optima est! || Marcus | It's the best!
Iūlia | Grātiās. Nunc, puerī, mēnsam lavāte! || Iūlia | Thanks. Now, boys, wash the table!
Marcus | Venī, Iōannēs! Currimus! || Marcus | Come on, John! We're running for it!
Iūlia | Manēte! Venīte hūc! || Iūlia | Stay put! Come back here!
:::

{#g-10}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Venīte hūc!* Why *-te*? | Iūlia calls two boys: y'all. |
| *Venīmus!* Why *-mus*? | Marcus answers for both of them: we. |
| *Cape pōculum et fer aquam.* Name each command's family. | *Cape*: an -iō verb (*capere*). *Fer*: one of the four rebels. |
| *Lavā ūvās.* Infinitive and family? | *Lavāre*, the ā family: clip the *-re*, get *lavā*. |
| *Nōlī cāseum edere!* Why *edere*, not *ede*? | After *nōlī*, use the infinitive. |
| *Dīc iterum, quaesō. Lentius!* When will you use this? | Every time someone speaks Latin too fast for you. |
| *Nōlīte dormīre, puerī!* Why *nōlīte*? | Two boys: y'all. |
| *Edite, bibite!* Who's talking to whom? | John, to everyone at the table: commands to y'all. |
| *Currimus!* Which family? | The little-i family: *currō, currere*. "We're running!" |
| *Manēte!* | "Stay (y'all)!" *manēre* → *manē* → *manēte*. |
| Sort every command in the Lectiō: one person, or y'all? | One: *dīc, cape, fer, lavā, labōrā, parā, vidē, nōlī…, portā, venī*. Y'all: *venīte, audīte, nōlīte…, edite, bibite, lavāte, manēte*. |

## 11. Sound check

:: Sapere audē. || Dare to be wise. (Horace, *Epistles* 1.2.40)

{#g-11}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *legimus*? | LE-gi-mus. Short i, so stress the third syllable from the end. |
| *audiunt*? | OW-di-unt. |
| *facite*? | FA-ki-te. Hard c, always. |
| *venīte*? | we-NEE-te. v is w. |
| *scīre*? | SKEE-re. Not SHEE-re. |
| *legere* vs *habēre*? | LE-ge-re vs ha-BAY-re. |

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking.

1. Chant *legō*, *audiō*, and *capiō* through all six persons. Then *faciō* and *veniō* without looking.
2. Boss your kitchen around, one person at a time: *Venī hūc! Fer aquam! Lavā ūvās! Cape pōculum!* Then to a crowd: *Venīte! Ferte! Lavāte! Capite!*
3. Obey yourself: *Venī hūc! Veniō!* · *Audīte! Audīmus!* · *Lege librum! Librum legō.*
4. The rebels, ten times fast: *dīc, dūc, fac, fer · dīcite, dūcite, facite, ferte.*
5. Forbid things: *Nōlī rīdēre! Nōlī cāseum edere! Nōlīte timēre!*
6. Your survival kit: *Dīc iterum, quaesō. Dīc lentius, quaesō. Nesciō. Intellegō.*
7. *Venī* vs *vēnī*: WE-nee, WAY-nee. Then Caesar: *Vēnī, vīdī, vīcī.*
8. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing all three parts. Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
