# Caput X: Liber Puerī

*Liber I, Prīma Verba · Chapter 10*

*In which everything in the house belongs to somebody, and we finally learn to say whose. One little ending turns "the boy" into "the boy's," the front hall fills up with shoes, swords, dolls, and cookies, and every case you know lines up in one table. The cheese, of course, belongs to the family. Briefly.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    - **Saying:** *liber puerī* · *Cuius est? Marcī est!* · *Mea est!* · *librī puerōrum* · *in stomachō Marcī*
    - **Grammar words, in plain English:** genitive (the "of" case, the possessive case); declension (a noun family)
    - **Forms:** "of" endings *-ae, -ī* (one owner), *-ārum, -ōrum* (many owners); *fīliī, Cornēliī*; the whole 1st and 2nd declension so far, in one table
    - **Laws:** the Law of the Owner, the Law of the Twins
    - **Words:** belongings: book box, ball, doll, toga, tunic, shoe, sword, ring, purse, cookie; *cuius?*
    - **Reading:** *Cuius est?* (a front hall full of stuff, and one piece of cheese)
    - **Speaking:** ask and answer "whose is this?"

!!! tip "How to read this chapter"
    Same as always. Say your answer **out loud**, then tap the wax tablet to check. This time, *point*: every time you say an owner, point at something you own.

---

## 1. *Liber puerī*

:: Annō Dominī. || In the year of the Lord: A.D. (how Latin counts the years)

Ecce puer. Ecce liber. Liber puerī est.

{#g-01}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapter 2's *liber* is "book." Here is "the boy's book": *liber puerī*. Which word is the boy, and what did it grow? | *Puerī*: *puer* plus **-ī**. That *-ī* means "of the boy, the boy's." LI-ber PU-e-ree. |
| "Marcus's book"? | *Liber Marcī.* The *-us* comes off, *-ī* goes on: *Marc-us → Marc-ī*. |
| "The teacher's book"? (*magister*) | *Liber magistrī.* Like Chapter 2's *liber, librī*, *magister* drops its e before the ending. |
| *Tarentum*, Chapter 8's town, is an *-um* word. "The forum of Tarentum"? | *Forum Tarentī.* *-um* words take *-ī* too. (Have you heard *Tarentī* before? Hold that thought until section 7.) |
| Where does the owner go? | Usually **after** the thing owned: *liber Marcī*, "the book of Marcus." English *'s* goes before, but English *of* goes after, like Latin. Put the owner first to stress it: *Marcī liber*, "**Marcus's** book." |
| "We're in the friend's garden." | *In hortō amīcī sumus.* *Hortō* takes the ablative after *in*. *Amīcī* stays *amīcī*. |
| "I see Marcus's book." | *Librum Marcī videō.* *Librum* is the object, so it's accusative. *Marcī* still doesn't budge. |
| So does the owner's ending care what the owned thing is doing? | Never. The owned thing changes for its job; the owner just says "of me." |
| You know four cases: nominative (naming), accusative (object), ablative (where, from, with), vocative (calling). This "of" form is a fifth. Its name? | The **genitive**. JEN-i-tiv in English. |
| The motto, *Annō Dominī*: which word is "of the Lord"? | *Dominī*, from Chapter 9's *dominus*. *Annō*, "in the year" (*annus*, year), is an ablative use for later. A.D. 2026 = *annō Dominī* 2026. |

!!! info "Grammar words, in plain English"
    **Genitive**: the "of" case, the owner's form: *liber puerī*, "the boy's book, the book of the boy." Old English grammars call this the **possessive case** (the *'s*). The name comes from Latin *genitīvus*, "of birth, of origin" (as in "genesis," "generation"): it tells where a thing comes from or whom it belongs to. It picks up other jobs later; for now, it is the "whose" case.

## 2. *Mēnsa puellae*

:: Aqua vītae. || The water of life. (an old name for strong spirits)

Puella mēnsam habet. Mēnsa puellae est.

{#g-02}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| The *-a* family has its own "of" ending. "The girl's table": *mēnsa puellae*. What's the ending? | **-ae.** *Puell-a → puell-ae.* pu-EL-lye. |
| *Pūpa*: doll (think "puppet"; long ū). "Cornēlia's doll"? | *Pūpa Cornēliae.* |
| *Ānulus*: ring. AH-nu-lus. "Grandma's ring"? | *Ānulus aviae.* |
| *Agricola* (farmer) is masculine. "The farmer's money": does he switch to the *hortus* ending, *agricolī*? | No: *pecūnia agricolae.* The ending follows the family (*-a* words), not the gender. A farmer still bends like *puella*. |
| Have you seen *puellae* before? | Chapter 2: *puellae*, "girls." Same spelling, two jobs: "girls" or "of the girl." |
| How do you tell? *Puellae cantant* vs *liber puellae*. | *Cantant* needs a subject: "the girls sing." Sitting after *liber*, *puellae* says whose: "the girl's book." The sentence decides. |
| Does section 1's *-ī* have the same twin? *Puerī cantant* vs *liber puerī*. | Yes. *Puerī* is "boys" or "of the boy." *Amīcī* is "friends" or "of the friend." |
| *Liber puerī* vs Chapter 2's *līber puer*? | "The boy's book" vs "a free boy." Long ī makes *līber* "free." Two vowels, two meanings. |
| The motto, *Aqua vītae.* *Vīta* is "life." | "The water of life": *vītae*, "of life." Distillers used it for strong spirits; French *eau-de-vie* (brandy) is a straight translation. |

!!! success "The Law of the Twins"
    In the singular, the "of" form looks just like the "many" form:

    | one | of one | many |
    |---|---|---|
    | *puella* | *puellae* | *puellae* |
    | *puer* | *puerī* | *puerī* |
    | *hortus* | *hortī* | *hortī* |

    The sentence tells you which twin you have. (Neuters break the pattern: *mālum, mālī*, but "apples" is *māla*.)

## 3. *Cuius est?*

:: Cuius est? Marcī est! || Whose is it? It's Marcus's!

Cuius est capsa? Marcī est. Cuius est pūpa? Cornēliae est.

{#g-03}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| To ask "whose?" Latin says *cuius?* KOO-yus (Romans doubled that y: KOOY-yus). *Cuius est?* | "Whose is it?" Learn it as a chunk. *Cuius* is the "of" form of *quis?*, "who?" (Chapter 2); Chapter 29 takes it apart. |
| *Capsa*: a Roman schoolboy's box for carrying his book rolls. *Cuius est capsa?* (It's Marcus's.) | *Capsa Marcī est.* Or just *Marcī est.*, "It's Marcus's," as in English: the thing owned is understood. |
| *Calceus*: shoe (KAL-ke-us). *Cuius sunt calceī?* (They're Quīntus's.) | *Quīntī sunt.* *Cuius* never changes: one thing or many. |
| *Cuius est tunica?* (*tunica*: tunic, the everyday Roman shirt-dress) It's Cornēlia's. | *Cornēliae est.* |
| *Cuius est capsa?* Answer "Mine!" | *Mea est!* Chapter 9's *meus*, agreeing with *capsa*: feminine. |
| *Gladius*: sword (think "gladiator"). *Cuius est gladius?* Say "It's yours." | *Tuus est.* Masculine, matching *gladius*. |
| *Cuius sunt calceī?* "They're ours!" | *Nostrī sunt!* Masculine plural, matching *calceī*. |
| Your name, *Iōannēs*, is third family; its "of" form waits for Book II. So how do you claim your shoes today? | *Meī sunt!* A possessive does the job. |
| "It's not Marcus's, it's Quīntus's." | *Nōn Marcī est, sed Quīntī.* |
| *Pila*: ball. PI-la, short i. (*Pīlum*, with a long ī, is a soldier's javelin. Don't play catch with that.) *Cuius est pila?* (Iūlia's) | *Iūliae est.* |

## 4. *Librī puerōrum*

:: Repetītiō māter studiōrum. || Repetition is the mother of learning. (proverb)

Liber puerī. Librī puerī. Librī puerōrum!

{#g-04}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Now two boys, with many books: "the boys' books" is *librī puerōrum*. Find the "of the boys" ending. | **-ōrum.** *Puer-ōrum.* pu-e-ROH-rum: the long ō takes the stress. |
| "The girls' dolls"? Guess the *-a* family's version. | *Pūpae puellārum.* **-ārum**, long ā: pu-el-LAH-rum. |
| What's the pattern? | The family's own vowel, made long, plus *-rum*: *ā* for the *-a* family, *ō* for the *hortus* family. |
| *-um* words? "The streets of the towns." | *Viae oppidōrum.* Neuters use *-ōrum* too. |
| Chapter 9's *līberī*, children. "The children's books"? | *Librī līberōrum.* LI-bree lee-be-ROH-rum. Say it three times fast. |
| Sort them: *liber puerī · librī puerī · librī puerōrum.* | The boy's book · the boy's books · the boys' books. English hides the difference in an apostrophe; Latin says it out loud. |
| *Crustulum*: cookie, little cake (KRUS-tu-lum), neuter. "The girls' cookies"? | *Crustula puellārum.* Horace says kind teachers handed out *crustula* to coax little boys into learning their letters (*Satires* 1.1.25–26). |
| The motto, *Repetītiō māter studiōrum*, appeared in Chapters 3 and 6. Which word is genitive, and what's its naming form? | *Studiōrum*, "of studies": plural of *studium*, study, eagerness. "Repetition is the mother of studies." It was a genitive all along. |

!!! success "The Law of the Owner"
    The owner goes in the **genitive**:

    | | one owner | many owners |
    |---|---|---|
    | *-a* family | *-ae* (*puellae*) | *-ārum* (*puellārum*) |
    | *hortus* family | *-ī* (*puerī, hortī, mālī*) | *-ōrum* (*puerōrum, hortōrum, mālōrum*) |

    It usually follows the thing owned (*liber puerī*), and its ending never changes with that thing's job: *liber puerī, librum puerī, in librō puerī*.

## 5. *Fīliī, Cornēliī*

:: Fīlius Cornēliī sum. || I am Cornēlius's son.

Gladius fīliī. Toga Cornēliī. Pūpa Cornēliae.

{#g-05}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Fīlius* ends in *-ius*. Use the rule: *-us* off, *-ī* on. "The son's sword"? | *Gladius fīliī.* *Fīli-us → fīli-ī*: two i's, FEE-li-ee. |
| Seen *fīliī* before? | Chapter 9: *Venīte, fīliī!*, "sons." The Law of the Twins again, just like *puerī*. |
| And Chapter 9's *fīlī*, with one i? | That's calling him: *Venī, fīlī!* |
| Cicero and Vergil actually wrote the genitive with one i: *fīlī*, "of the son." Which does this book use? | **This book writes *fīliī*,** the regular form that later Romans and dictionaries use (*fīlius, fīliī*). In older authors, *fīlī* can mean "of the son"; the sentence will tell you. |
| Cornēlia's father is a Cornēlius (Chapter 9). Marcus is his son too. Marcus says: "I'm Cornēlius's son." | *Fīlius Cornēliī sum.* kor-NAY-li-ee. |
| *Toga*: the white wool gown of a Roman citizen. "Cornēlius's toga" vs "Cornēlia's doll"? | *Toga Cornēliī · pūpa Cornēliae.* Father *-iī*, daughter *-ae*. |
| "The sons' swords"? | *Gladiī fīliōrum.* *Gladius* is an *-ius* word too: *gladius, gladiī*. |

## 6. *In stomachō Marcī*

:: Tuī Catullī plēnus sacculus est arāneārum. || Your Catullus's purse is full of cobwebs. (Catullus 13.7–8)

Ubi est cāseus? In stomachō Marcī!

{#g-06}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chapters 7 and 8 both said *Cāseus in stomachō est!* but never said whose stomach. Say it now. | *Cāseus in stomachō Marcī est!* |
| Chapter 9 caught the real thief. Correct the record. | *Minimē! In stomachō Quīntī!* |
| An owner can have an adjective: "the little boy's cookie." | *Crustulum puerī parvī.* *Parvī* matches *puerī*: genitive too. |
| An owner can have a possessive: "my friend's house." | *Vīlla amīcī meī.* *Meī* matches *amīcī*: "of my friend." Chapter 9's Law of the Owned Thing, still working. |
| "In my friend's kitchen." | *In culīnā amīcī meī.* |
| *Sacculus*: little bag, purse (SAK-ku-lus). Find the two genitives in the motto, *Tuī Catullī plēnus sacculus est arāneārum.* | *Tuī Catullī*, "of your Catullus" (*tuī* matches *Catullī*), and *arāneārum*, "of cobwebs" (*arānea*: spider, cobweb). *Plēnus*, "full" (Chapter 3), takes a genitive: "full **of**." That's a second job for the "of" case, not ownership; more jobs come later. |
| Why is Catullus's purse full of cobwebs? | He's inviting his friend Fabullus to dinner: "You'll dine well at my place, Fabullus, if you bring a big, good dinner, and the wine, and the wit, and all the laughs." Then the punchline: the poet is broke. |

## 7. *Quīnque cāsūs*

:: Vōx populī, vōx Deī. || The voice of the people is the voice of God. (medieval saying)

Puella, puellae, puellam, puellā. Hortus, hortī, hortum, hortō, horte!

{#g-07}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Name the five cases you know, with a job for each. | Nominative (naming, subject), genitive (of, whose), accusative (object; where to), ablative (where, from, with), vocative (calling). |
| A noun's full set of endings is its **declension**, from *dēclīnāre*, "to bend aside": the noun bends its ending. Reciting them is "declining" the noun. Which families have you met? | The *-a* family (*puella*) is the **first declension**. The *hortus, puer, mālum* family is the **second declension**. Chapter 9's *pater* and *Iōannēs* belong to the third (Book II). |
| Decline *puella*, singular: nominative, genitive, accusative, ablative, vocative. | *puella, puellae, puellam, puellā, puella.* |
| Now *hortus*. | *hortus, hortī, hortum, hortō, horte.* |
| *Puer*? | *puer, puerī, puerum, puerō, puer.* |
| *Mālum*? | *mālum, mālī, mālum, mālō, mālum.* A neuter's naming, object, and calling forms are always the same. |
| Plurals: *puella*, then *hortus*. | *puellae, puellārum, puellās, puellīs, puellae · hortī, hortōrum, hortōs, hortīs, hortī.* |
| Latin dictionaries list a noun by **two** forms: *puella, puellae* · *hortus, hortī* · *mālum, mālī*. Which two? | Nominative and **genitive** singular. The genitive shows the family: *-ae* = first declension, *-ī* = second. From this chapter on, our word lists do the same. |
| Chapter 8's "at" forms: *Rōmae, Tarentī*. Which row of the table do they look like? | The genitive singular: *Rōmae* like *puellae*, *Tarentī* like *hortī*. For first- and second-declension towns with singular names, "at" looks like "of." (*Domī* is a leftover of its own.) |
| The order most American textbooks use is nominative, genitive, **dative**, accusative, ablative, vocative. What's the dative? | The "to, for" case. It's where Chapter 1's *mihi* and *tibi* live, and it's next chapter. The table leaves a row for it. |
| The motto, *Vōx populī, vōx Deī*: find the genitives. | *Populī*, "of the people" (*populus*), and *Deī*, "of God" (*Deus*). The scholar Alcuin quoted it to Charlemagne around A.D. 800 only to warn him **not** to believe it. |

!!! success "Quīnque cāsūs: the first and second declensions so far"
    **Singular**

    | case | 1st: *puella* | 2nd: *hortus* | 2nd: *puer* | 2nd: *liber* | 2nd n.: *mālum* |
    |---|---|---|---|---|---|
    | nom. (naming) | *puella* | *hortus* | *puer* | *liber* | *mālum* |
    | gen. (of, whose) | *puellae* | *hortī* | *puerī* | *librī* | *mālī* |
    | dat. (to, for) | *Chapter 11* | | | | |
    | acc. (object; where to) | *puellam* | *hortum* | *puerum* | *librum* | *mālum* |
    | abl. (where, from, with) | *puellā* | *hortō* | *puerō* | *librō* | *mālō* |
    | voc. (calling) | *puella* | *horte* | *puer* | *liber* | *mālum* |

    **Plural**

    | case | 1st: *puella* | 2nd: *hortus* | 2nd: *puer* | 2nd: *liber* | 2nd n.: *mālum* |
    |---|---|---|---|---|---|
    | nom. | *puellae* | *hortī* | *puerī* | *librī* | *māla* |
    | gen. | *puellārum* | *hortōrum* | *puerōrum* | *librōrum* | *mālōrum* |
    | dat. | *Chapter 11* | | | | |
    | acc. | *puellās* | *hortōs* | *puerōs* | *librōs* | *māla* |
    | abl. | *puellīs* | *hortīs* | *puerīs* | *librīs* | *mālīs* |
    | voc. | *puellae* | *hortī* | *puerī* | *librī* | *māla* |

    - **Vocative** (Chapter 9): same as the nominative, except singular *-us → -e* (*horte, Marce*) and *-ius → -ī* (*fīlī*).
    - **Locative** (Chapter 8): town names and *domus* only: *Rōmae, Tarentī, domī*. For towns it matches the genitive singular.

!!! info "Grammar words, in plain English"
    **Declension**: a noun family, the set of endings a noun runs through (from *dēclīnāre*, "to bend aside"). To **decline** a noun is to say all its forms in order. Latin has five declensions; Book I uses the first two.

## 8. Lectiō: *Cuius est?*

:: Cuius est cāseus? || Whose is the cheese?

Read each Latin line aloud. Guess the English, then tap to check. For the reverse drill, tap **Tege Latīna** and rebuild each line from the English.

::: dialogue lectio
Māter | Līberī! Ecce ātrium: plēnum est! Cuius est capsa? || Mother | Kids! Look at the front hall: it's full! Whose is the book box?
Marcus | Mea est! Capsa Marcī est. || Marcus | It's mine! It's Marcus's box.
Māter | Et cuius sunt calceī? || Mother | And whose are the shoes?
Quīntus | Calceī Quīntī sunt! Meī sunt! || Quīntus | They're Quīntus's shoes! They're mine!
Māter | Cuius est pūpa? || Mother | Whose is the doll?
Cornēlia | Pūpa Cornēliae est. Mea est! || Cornēlia | The doll is Cornēlia's. It's mine!
Iōannēs | Et pila? Cuius est pila? || John | And the ball? Whose is the ball?
Quīntus | Pila nostra est! Pila līberōrum! || Quīntus | It's our ball! The kids' ball!
Avus | Et ubi est gladius meus? Gladius avī in ātriō nōn est! || Grandpa | And where's my sword? Grandpa's sword isn't in the hall!
Avia | Gladius tuus in hortō est, ave. Sed ubi est ānulus meus? || Grandma | Your sword is in the garden, Grandpa. But where's my ring?
Cornēlia | Ecce! Pūpa mea ānulum aviae habet! || Cornēlia | Look! My doll has Grandma's ring!
Pater | Et toga mea? Hodiē ad forum eō! || Father | And my toga? I'm going to the forum today!
Māter | Toga Cornēliī in cubiculō est, mī Cornēlī. || Mother | Cornēlius's toga is in the bedroom, my dear Cornēlius.
Marcus | Sed ubi est liber meus? Sine librō ad lūdum nōn eō! || Marcus | But where's my book? I'm not going to school without my book!
Iōannēs | Cuius est sacculus? Plēnus est! || John | Whose is the little bag? It's full!
Quīntus | Nōn meus est! || Quīntus | It's not mine!
Cornēlia | Sacculus Quīntī est. Semper Quīntī! || Cornēlia | It's Quīntus's bag. Always Quīntus's!
Māter | Quid est in sacculō? || Mother | What's in the bag?
Iōannēs | Crustula… et liber Marcī! || John | Cookies… and Marcus's book!
Marcus | Liber meus! Et in librō… cāseus! || Marcus | My book! And in the book… cheese!
Pater | Cuius est cāseus? || Father | Whose is the cheese?
Marcus | Nōn meus! || Marcus | Not mine!
Quīntus | Nōn meus! || Quīntus | Not mine!
Avus | Cāseus nōn Marcī, nōn Quīntī est. Cāseus familiae est! || Grandpa | The cheese isn't Marcus's, and it isn't Quīntus's. It's the family's cheese!
Quīntus | Ita! Cāseus noster est! || Quīntus | Right! So it's our cheese!
Māter | Marce! Quīnte! Nōlīte edere! || Mother | Marcus! Quīntus! Don't eat it!
Iōannēs | Ecce: cāseus familiae nunc in stomachīs puerōrum est. || John | Look: the family's cheese is now in the boys' stomachs.
Avus | Semper cāseus, semper Marcus. Repetītiō māter studiōrum! || Grandpa | Always cheese, always Marcus. Repetition is the mother of learning!
:::

{#g-08}

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| *Cuius est capsa? Mea est!* Why *mea*? | It matches *capsa*, feminine: "It's mine." |
| *Calceī Quīntī sunt.* Is *Quīntī* "Quīntuses"? | No: "Quīntus's." One Quīntus, the owner. *Calceī* is the plural. |
| *Pila līberōrum*: one owner or many? | Many: *-ōrum*, "of the children." |
| *Ānulum aviae habet.* Which word is the object, and which is the owner? | *Ānulum* is the object (accusative), *aviae* the owner (genitive): "Grandma's ring." |
| *Toga Cornēliī … mī Cornēlī.* Two forms of one name. Which is which? | *Cornēliī* (two i's) is "of Cornēlius." *Cornēlī* (one i) is calling him. And *mī*: Chapter 9's "my!" |
| *Sine librō ad lūdum nōn eō.* Any genitive here? | None! *Librō*: ablative after *sine*. *Lūdum*: accusative after *ad*. Not every noun is an owner. |
| *In stomachīs puerōrum*: whose stomachs, and how many? | The boys': *puerōrum* (many owners), *stomachīs* (two stomachs, ablative plural after *in*). |
| Grandpa's last line: where have you seen it? | Chapters 3 and 6's motto. *Studiōrum* is a genitive plural. The cheese story repeats; Grandpa calls it learning. |

## Vocābula: Words of this chapter

:: Verba volant, scrīpta manent. || Spoken words fly away, written ones stay.

From now on, nouns are listed the dictionary way: **naming form, "of" form** (nominative, genitive singular), and gender.

| Latin (nominative, genitive) | gender | English |
|---|---|---|
| *capsa, capsae* | f. | box for book rolls, school bag |
| *pila, pilae* | f. | ball |
| *pūpa, pūpae* | f. | doll |
| *toga, togae* | f. | toga |
| *tunica, tunicae* | f. | tunic |
| *vīta, vītae* | f. | life |
| *calceus, calceī* | m. | shoe |
| *gladius, gladiī* | m. | sword |
| *ānulus, ānulī* | m. | ring |
| *sacculus, sacculī* | m. | little bag, purse |

## Speak it aloud

:: Dīcendō discimus. || We learn by speaking.

1. Walk around your house, point, and claim: *Liber meus est. Mēnsa mea est. Pōculum meum est.* Then say *Cuius est?* before each one.
2. Give your things away to the family: *liber Marcī, pūpa Cornēliae, gladius avī, ānulus aviae, toga Cornēliī.*
3. One owner, then many: *liber puerī → librī puerōrum · pūpa puellae → pūpae puellārum · hortus amīcī → hortus amīcōrum.*
4. Decline out loud, slowly, then fast: *puella, puellae, puellam, puellā, puella* · *hortus, hortī, hortum, hortō, horte.* Then both plurals.
5. Play lost-and-found with a friend or with Grok Bot: one asks *Cuius est …?*, the other answers *Marcī est · Mea est · Nōn meus est, sed Quīntī.*
6. Say the line three chapters have waited for: *Cāseus in stomachō Marcī est!*
7. Say the mottos and point at each genitive: *Annō Dominī · Aqua vītae · Repetītiō māter studiōrum · Vōx populī, vōx Deī · Agnus Deī.*
8. Read the *Lectiō* aloud, playing all eight parts. Then hide the Latin and rebuild it.
